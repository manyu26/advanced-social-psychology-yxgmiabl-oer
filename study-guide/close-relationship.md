## Module logic

Attraction, commitment, and relationship quality are related but distinct. This module follows relationships from initial encounters through interdependence, maintenance, change, and dissolution, while treating the partners and their context as a connected system.

## 10.1 Attraction and its target

::: success
**Objective 10.1a:** Distinguish general desirability, personal preferences, and unique attraction.
:::

Attraction can be studied through hypothetical preferences, first meetings, or developing relationships. Those settings answer different questions. Stated ideals may describe what someone expects to want without strongly predicting which particular person they will like after interaction. A desirable attribute can predict attraction generally even when individual differences in preference for that attribute add little prediction.

The **social relations model** separates an actor tendency to like others, a partner tendency to be liked, and a relationship-specific component. Unique attraction concerns the particular pairing beyond these general tendencies. Estimating these components requires a design in which people encounter several partners; a single pair cannot supply all the necessary comparisons.

Attraction is also dynamic. Opportunities to meet, repeated interaction, and changing goals shape its development. A snapshot from a speed-date cannot establish the eventual quality or duration of a relationship. The earlier lecture’s emphasis on instrumentality can be developed by asking which goals a potential relationship appears to serve, without assuming that all attraction is a conscious calculation.

**Different sources of attraction**

- Actor: tendency to like others
- Partner: tendency to be liked
- Relationship: distinctive pairing

**Different research settings**

- Hypothetical ideals
- Initial interaction
- Development across repeated encounters

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
In a hypothetical additive example, the grand mean is 5, an actor component is +1, a partner component is +0.5, and a relationship component is +0.5. What is the fitted rating?
:::
::: tab Solution
The fitted rating is 7: 5 + 1 + 0.5 + 0.5. Only the final component represents the distinctive pairing beyond those general tendencies. This constructed illustration omits residual error and other model details; it does not show that a single rating can identify all components.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** People who are judged warm receive higher attraction ratings. Does this show that people who say they especially value warmth are especially responsive to it?

## 10.2 Studying two connected people

::: success
**Objective 10.2a:** Evaluate dyadic, longitudinal, and behavioral evidence in relationship research.
:::

Partners’ experiences are statistically and psychologically linked. Treating two reports from a couple as independent observations can misrepresent uncertainty and obscure actor and partner processes. A person’s characteristic may relate to their own outcome, their partner’s outcome, or both. Dyadic designs make those distinctions explicit.

Different methods illuminate different parts of a relationship. A global satisfaction questionnaire summarizes an evaluation; a diary captures experiences closer to when they occur; observed interaction provides behavior in a sampled setting. None covers the entire relationship. Laboratory behavior can be informative while remaining sensitive to observation and task demands.

Prospective data improve temporal information but do not automatically establish causation. Stress may influence interaction and satisfaction while satisfaction also shapes reports of stress. Model time carefully, measure plausible common causes, and distinguish changes within a couple from differences between couples. The question is not which method is universally best, but which combination can test the stated explanation.

**Dyadic structure**

- Actor association
- Partner association
- Dependence within the couple

**Method and timescale**

- Global evaluation
- Repeated daily reports
- Observed interaction

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A study obtains two reports from each of 100 couples and analyzes 200 independent people. What should the analysis reconsider?
:::
::: tab Solution
The reports are nested in couples and may be correlated. Use an analysis that represents dyadic dependence and the actor or partner questions being asked. The number of reports does not equal the number of independent couples. Sampling and measurement decisions also determine which effects can be identified.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Couples with more positive communication are happier. Why is teaching positive communication not already proven to cause happiness?

## 10.3 Interdependence and attachment

::: success
**Objective 10.3a:** Explain relationship outcomes through interdependence and attachment dimensions.
:::

**Interdependence** concerns how each person’s outcomes depend on their own and the other person’s actions. Partners can coordinate, provide benefits, create costs, or constrain choices. A disagreement over work schedules is therefore not only a difference in preference; it may reflect a situation in which one person’s choice changes the other’s opportunities.

Attachment perspectives examine regulation of closeness and security. **Attachment anxiety** involves concern about rejection and availability; **avoidance** involves discomfort with intimacy or dependence. These dimensions are more informative than assigning people to rigid boxes. Orientations can vary across relationships and occasions as well as showing enduring tendencies.

A person’s interpretation of a partner’s behavior can become part of a reciprocal process. Expecting rejection may shape behavior that changes the interaction. Such possibilities do not place responsibility for mistreatment on the recipient. Explain each actor’s behavior and the structural context, and avoid treating an attachment score as a diagnosis or an unavoidable relationship fate.

**Interdependence**

- Own actions affect outcomes
- Partner actions affect outcomes
- Coordination changes available options

**Attachment dimensions**

- Anxiety about rejection
- Avoidance of intimacy
- Variation across relationships and time

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
One partner wants reassurance after a disagreement while the other withdraws. Can their attachment categories be diagnosed from this episode?
:::
::: tab Solution
No. The episode is compatible with several explanations, including stress, conversational timing, and learned expectations. Repeated measures and appropriate instruments can assess attachment-related tendencies. Describe the interaction before assigning a trait, and examine how each response affects the next.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** How could a schedule conflict be reduced without changing either partner’s preferences?

## 10.4 Commitment and investment

::: success
**Objective 10.4a:** Distinguish satisfaction, alternatives, investments, and commitment.
:::

**Satisfaction** evaluates the relationship’s quality. **Commitment** concerns an intention to persist and an orientation toward a shared future. They are related but not identical. A person can remain strongly committed while dissatisfied, or feel satisfied while retaining attractive alternatives and limited commitment.

The **investment model** identifies satisfaction, investments, and the perceived quality of alternatives as predictors of commitment. Investments include resources that would be lost or diminished if the relationship ended. Alternatives concern what is available outside the current arrangement, which can include being alone rather than another partner.

Commitment can support maintenance behaviors, but persistence is not a universal criterion for a good outcome. Constraints can sustain a harmful or unrewarding relationship. A research design should assess well-being, autonomy, and relationship quality separately from duration. Do not turn a predictive model of staying into advice that people should stay.

**Predictors of commitment**

- Satisfaction with the relationship
- Investments tied to it
- Perceived quality of alternatives

**Outcomes to separate**

- Intention to persist
- Maintenance behavior
- Quality and well-being

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Two people report equal satisfaction, but one has greater relationship-specific investments and fewer attractive alternatives. What does the investment model predict?
:::
::: tab Solution
Other things equal, the second person may report greater commitment. The prediction concerns commitment, not necessarily better relationship quality. Measure the three predictors separately and avoid treating duration as proof of satisfaction. Observational associations also leave causal questions open.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** An intervention increases relationship duration but does not measure participants’ welfare. Why is its success claim incomplete?

## 10.5 Maintenance and responsiveness

::: success
**Objective 10.5a:** Explain how responsiveness, shared goals, and constructive responses can support relationships.
:::

Relationship maintenance involves ordinary interactions as well as major commitments. **Perceived responsiveness** concerns feeling understood, valued, and cared for by a partner. The helper’s intention and the recipient’s experience can differ. An action that seems supportive to one person may feel intrusive or mismatched to the other’s goal.

Responding to positive events is also important. Capitalization research examines sharing good news and the response it receives. An engaged response can help the event become a shared positive experience. The question is broader than whether partners avoid conflict; relationships also depend on how they respond to opportunities for growth and enjoyment.

Partners can support each other’s goals while retaining distinct identities. Coordination requires knowing what the other person actually wants rather than assuming that a preferred self-image should be imposed on them. Measure the recipient’s goals, the support offered, and later progress. A correlation between supportive perceptions and satisfaction may partly reflect an overall positive view, so behavioral and repeated measures add value.

**Responsiveness**

- Understanding the partner
- Valuing their perspective
- Responding to relevant needs

**Growth and positive events**

- Sharing good news
- Engaged response
- Support for the recipient’s goals

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A student shares news of a small research award. Their partner immediately explains how to win a larger one. Why might the response miss the mark?
:::
::: tab Solution
The student may be seeking shared enjoyment or recognition rather than strategy advice. Assess what response was wanted and how the actual response was experienced. Advice is not inherently unsupportive, but its fit matters. This hypothetical example distinguishes intention to help from perceived responsiveness.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** How would you test whether a good-news response matters beyond the couple’s general satisfaction?

## 10.6 Stress, conflict, and trajectories

::: success
**Objective 10.6a:** Compare relationship trajectories using vulnerability, stress, and adaptation.
:::

Relationships develop over time rather than follow one universal arc. Early intensity, later satisfaction, and stability can vary independently. An average decline does not describe every couple, and a single high satisfaction score can conceal substantial fluctuation.

The **vulnerability–stress–adaptation model** connects enduring vulnerabilities, external stressors, and adaptive interaction processes. A demanding work schedule can affect opportunities for connection and the capacity to respond constructively. Treating every difficulty as poor communication can overlook the conditions under which communication occurs.

Conflict behavior also depends on context and timing. Constructive adaptation may involve understanding the disagreement, changing routines, or altering a recurring situation. A study needs to measure the proposed process and subsequent outcomes. A statistical pathway from stress to communication to satisfaction is informative, but causal mediation requires stronger assumptions than temporal ordering alone.

**Sources of variation**

- Enduring vulnerabilities
- External stressors
- Adaptive interaction processes

**Trajectory questions**

- Average level
- Fluctuation and change
- Direction and timing of effects

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A couple communicates less warmly during a month of unstable work hours. Why is a purely personality-based account incomplete?
:::
::: tab Solution
The schedule may reduce time, increase stress, and change opportunities for support. Stable traits may still matter, but the within-couple change calls for a contextual explanation. Measure the stressor and interaction over time and compare competing accounts rather than infer a new enduring trait from one difficult month.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Two couples have the same mean satisfaction but different week-to-week variability. What additional question becomes important?

## 10.7 Rejection and dissolution

::: success
**Objective 10.7a:** Distinguish rejection, relationship dissolution, and variation in adjustment.
:::

Rejection can occur before a relationship forms, within an ongoing relationship, or as part of dissolution. The desired relationship may not be equally valued by both people. An account of attraction therefore needs to consider asymmetry rather than assume that one person’s increasing interest reflects mutual progress.

Dissolution can change routines, resources, social networks, and the self-concept. Distress and adjustment vary; a breakup can remove costs while introducing new difficulties. A study that samples only immediately after separation may capture a different part of the process from a later follow-up.

Avoid prescriptive conclusions from averages or selected samples. Remaining together and separating each have multiple possible consequences. In research commentary, ask whose outcome is measured, when it is measured, and whether the analysis includes partners and others affected. Persistent unwanted pursuit is not evidence of relationship success or commitment that should be celebrated.

**Separate events**

- Unreciprocated attraction
- Perceived rejection within a relationship
- Dissolution of an established bond

**Adjustment outcomes**

- Emotion and self-concept
- Resources and social networks
- Variation over time and between people

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A study measures distress one week after a breakup and concludes that separation is always harmful. What is missing?
:::
::: tab Solution
The claim exceeds the time window and population studied. Assess prior relationship conditions, changes in resources, longer-term adjustment, and relevant comparison trajectories. Acute distress can coexist with later improvement. Observing an average does not establish that every person would have been better off remaining together.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Why should a study of rejection collect reports from both people when feasible?

## 10.8 Diversity and relationship microcultures

::: success
**Objective 10.8a:** Evaluate generalization and explain relationships as locally organized patterns.
:::

Relationship science has often relied on limited samples and assumptions about which arrangements count as normal. Findings about one population or relationship form should not automatically be generalized to all. Measures can also build in assumptions, such as equating commitment with exclusivity.

Consensually nonmonogamous arrangements differ from violations of an agreed relationship boundary. The relevant distinction is agreement and its meaning, not simply whether more than two people are involved. Research should describe the arrangement and sample precisely rather than collapse diverse relationships into one category.

Finkel also reviews the idea of relationship **microcultures**: partners create shared meanings, routines, and expectations. A ritual can matter because of its local significance, not because every couple performs it. This connects relationship science to Module 7 and to goal systems in Module 8. It also suggests why a technique that works in one pair may need a different interpretation in another.

**Generalization questions**

- Who was represented?
- What assumptions are in the measure?
- What boundaries were agreed?

**Microcultural processes**

- Shared routines
- Locally meaningful practices
- Negotiation and change

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A commitment scale treats any additional romantic partner as evidence of low commitment. What validity problem arises?
:::
::: tab Solution
The scale can confuse commitment with an assumption of exclusivity. In an explicitly consensual arrangement, the item may not measure the intended construct. Define commitment and test whether items function appropriately across relationship forms. This does not imply that every existing scale item is invalid in its original population.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A couple values a weekly walk, while another dislikes scheduled rituals. What would a microcultural account investigate?

## Synthesis and seminar preparation

Attraction, commitment, and relationship quality are related but distinct. This module follows relationships from initial encounters through interdependence, maintenance, change, and dissolution, while treating the partners and their context as a connected system.

For the final commentary, identify a claim from a chosen article, specify the construct and measure, and develop an alternative that the design cannot yet exclude. Propose one feasible comparison and explain how its possible outcomes would change the interpretation.
