## 5.1 Representing an evaluation{{attrs[#blk-bf7314qvjaw1]}}

::: success
**Objective 5.1a:** Distinguish attitude valence, evaluative bases, and ambivalence.
:::

An **attitude** is an evaluation of a specified object: a person, practice, policy, or other target. Valence describes its favorable or unfavorable direction. The object matters. Support for open science is not the same judgment as support for publicly posting a sensitive dataset. A useful measurement plan states the target before choosing a scale.

Evaluations can draw on feelings, beliefs, and experiences of acting. These are possible bases, not three components that must contribute equally to every attitude. A researcher may enjoy sharing materials, believe sharing improves scrutiny, and have established a sharing routine. Those observations suggest several inputs but do not reveal their causal weights.

**Ambivalence** requires special care. A midpoint on a single unfavorable–favorable scale could indicate little evaluation or strong reactions in both directions. Separate positive and negative ratings help distinguish these possibilities. The coexistence of positive and negative associations also differs from consciously feeling conflicted. Measure that experience separately instead of assuming that a computed score captures it.

Accounts of attitude representation differ in how much evaluation is retrieved from memory versus constructed in the moment. The same target can elicit different responses as information becomes accessible. Neither a direct report nor an indirect task uniquely settles this debate. **Affective and cognitive bases** concern what supports an attitude; **meta-bases** concern what people believe supports it. A student may describe a policy preference as evidence-driven even when an emotional experience contributed. Matching a message to a perceived basis may matter without establishing that this perception is causally accurate.

**Specify the target**

- Object: the particular policy
- Valence: favorable or unfavorable
- Basis: feeling, belief, or experience

**Separate mixed reactions**

- Positive and negative ratings
- Experienced conflict
- A midpoint can hide different states

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Two students both choose the midpoint on a policy scale. One sees few consequences; the other sees substantial benefits and substantial costs. What should the next measure distinguish?
:::
::: tab Solution
Collect separate positive and negative ratings and a report of felt conflict. The first student may be indifferent; the second may be ambivalent. A midpoint alone cannot distinguish them. Do not diagnose ambivalence solely from delayed responding, because uncertainty or difficulty understanding the question can also slow a response.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Which conclusion follows from a high positive rating and a high negative rating? A. The person is indifferent. B. Opposing evaluations coexist. C. Behavior cannot be predicted. D. The attitude is inaccurate.

## 5.2 Functions and behavior{{attrs[#blk-2swktuomova0]}}

::: success
**Objective 5.2a:** Evaluate attitude–behavior correspondence using matched targets and contexts.
:::

Attitudes can help people approach desirable options and avoid undesirable ones. They may also organize knowledge, express values, support identity, or serve practical interests. A favorable evaluation is therefore not merely a detached factual estimate. Two people supporting the same policy may be serving different functions, which can matter for the information they find relevant.

The relationship between an attitude and behavior depends partly on correspondence between the measures. A broad environmental evaluation is a weak match for one difficult recycling decision in a particular building. Measure the relevant act, setting, and time, or use a suitably broad collection of behaviors. Improving correspondence does not make prediction perfect.

Behavior also depends on opportunities, habits, competing goals, and social expectations. Someone may favor attending a seminar but be scheduled to teach at that hour. An intention is closer to an action plan than a general evaluation, yet implementation can still fail. The graduate-level task is to explain the missing link rather than conclude that attitudes either determine everything or matter nowhere.

The **MODE model** highlights motivation and opportunity in whether an accessible attitude guides responding relatively automatically or whether deliberation becomes possible. Reasoned-action approaches, including the **Theory of Planned Behavior**, distinguish attitudes toward an act from perceived norms, perceived control, intentions, and behavior. A favorable evaluation can coexist with low control. These frameworks ask different questions about the conditions under which evaluation contributes to action.

**What an attitude can do**

- Guide approach or avoidance
- Express values and identity
- Organize relevant information

**Why behavior may differ**

- Target or time mismatch
- Opportunity and resources
- Habits and competing goals

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A survey measures support for public transportation. The outcome is whether a student took an infrequent bus to campus yesterday. What would make the test more informative?
:::
::: tab Solution
Measure evaluation of that journey and document schedule, access, weather, and competing travel needs. Alternatively, retain the broad evaluation and aggregate multiple transport choices. A failure to predict one constrained trip does not show that evaluations are irrelevant. Matched measures improve the interpretation without eliminating contextual constraints.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** An imagined campaign raises stated intention from 40 to 60 on a 100-point scale, while observed attendance stays at 30%. Can the campaign be described as changing behavior?

## 5.3 Strength beyond extremity{{attrs[#blk-2s7z9c3t6llo]}}

::: success
**Objective 5.3a:** Separate defining consequences of attitude strength from its predictors.
:::

An extreme evaluation need not be durable or influential. **Attitude strength** is characterized by consequences such as persistence, resistance, and impact on processing or behavior. Accessibility, importance, certainty, knowledge, moralization, extremity, and ambivalence are related dimensions that may predict those consequences. They are not interchangeable measurements of one fixed quantity.

Certainty concerns how confident people feel about an evaluation. It can arise from perceived evidence quality, completeness, relevance, social agreement, or the experience of thinking. Such appraisals may be mistaken. Feeling certain therefore cannot validate the evaluation’s factual basis.

The effects of these dimensions are conditional. Ambivalence can encourage information seeking, but people may prefer information that reduces conflict rather than information that challenges a favored conclusion. Moralized attitudes can organize action and make compromise difficult. To test strength, measure the proposed antecedent and the specific consequence over time. Avoid defining a strong attitude by the very outcome the study intends to explain.

**Potential predictors**

- Accessibility and importance
- Certainty and knowledge
- Moralization and ambivalence

**Consequences to observe**

- Persistence over time
- Resistance to a challenge
- Effects on judgments or behavior

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A participant selects the most favorable scale endpoint but changes position after one weak message. Which strength claim has been tested?
:::
::: tab Solution
The endpoint shows extremity; the subsequent change provides evidence about resistance under that particular challenge. It does not support treating extremity as sufficient proof of strength. Assess persistence and behavioral impact separately, and check whether the message was actually weak for this participant rather than only for the researcher.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A study defines strong attitudes as attitudes that resist persuasion, then concludes that strong attitudes resist persuasion. Repair the design.

## 5.4 Learning evaluations{{attrs[#blk-982ucz6gavpk]}}

::: success
**Objective 5.4a:** Compare exposure, evaluative pairing, and inference-based learning.
:::

People acquire evaluations through direct experience, observing others, encountering information, and repeated exposure. **Mere exposure** refers to changes in liking associated with familiarity, whereas **evaluative conditioning** concerns pairing a target with valenced material. Familiarity and pairing can occur together in everyday media, so a study must separate them if it claims a specific mechanism.

Calling a procedure associative does not settle whether its effects require awareness, relational understanding, or deliberate inference. A person seeing a product near a pleasant scene may infer that its maker supports the scene’s values. A careful account distinguishes the procedure from the mental process proposed to explain it.

Sampling also shapes what is learned. People may repeatedly approach an initially liked object and avoid a disliked one, producing unequal opportunities to discover counterevidence. An apparent stability of preference could partly reflect an information environment created by prior choices. This connects attitude learning to Module 4’s selection of self-relevant evidence and Module 9’s social transmission of messages.

**Different procedures**

- Repeat the target
- Pair it with valenced material
- Provide evidence about attributes

**Mechanisms remain testable**

- Familiarity or fluency
- Learned evaluative relations
- Interpretation of what pairing means

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A fictional badge appears ten times beside pleasant scenes. Liking rises. Which comparison separates repeated exposure from positive pairing?
:::
::: tab Solution
Compare equally repeated badges paired with neutral versus pleasant scenes, with baseline liking and presentation conditions matched. A no-exposure condition can estimate the contribution of repetition. The result identifies a procedural contrast; additional measures or manipulations are needed to distinguish association, fluency, and propositional interpretation.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Participants choose which reviews to read. Their later attitudes become more extreme. Is selective reading a demonstrated cause?

## 5.5 Reflective change{{attrs[#blk-0io54eb2ylh1]}}

::: success
**Objective 5.5a:** Distinguish dissonance, self-perception, and correction accounts.
:::

People can infer an evaluation from their behavior, seek consistency among commitments, or reconsider an evaluation they believe was biased. **Cognitive dissonance** accounts emphasize aversive inconsistency and its reduction. **Self-perception** accounts emphasize inference from behavior and context, particularly when prior internal evidence is weak. Similar attitude reports can be compatible with either account.

External justification matters because it changes what behavior implies. Voluntarily defending a position with little external pressure may carry different meaning from doing so as an assigned role. However, observing change after a choice does not by itself prove discomfort or its reduction. Measure or manipulate the theoretically relevant conditions.

Correction also requires an inference: the person must identify a possible influence and estimate its direction or size. Correction can be insufficient or excessive when that estimate is wrong. More reflection is therefore not automatically more accuracy. It may instead produce a more elaborate defense, a revised evaluation, or reduced certainty without a change in valence.

**Competing explanations**

- Dissonance: reduce inconsistency
- Self-perception: infer from conduct
- Correction: adjust a suspected influence

**Discriminating evidence**

- Strength of prior attitude
- Perceived choice and justification
- Discomfort and perceived bias

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A student voluntarily writes in support of an unfamiliar policy and later reports liking it. Why is dissonance not the only explanation?
:::
::: tab Solution
The student might infer support from voluntarily writing the essay, learn new arguments while composing it, or experience inconsistency and resolve it. Assess initial attitude strength, perceived choice, external justification, discomfort, and message learning. An attitude shift alone does not identify which process produced it.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A reviewer rejects a paper more harshly after realizing its author is a friend. Does that establish an unbiased correction?

## 5.6 Persuasion as a process{{attrs[#blk-9cml7285sd89]}}

::: success
**Objective 5.6a:** Use elaboration and thought confidence to generate conditional persuasion predictions.
:::

Persuasion depends on what recipients do with a message. Elaboration approaches distinguish conditions that encourage substantial consideration of issue-relevant arguments from conditions favoring simpler cues. Motivation and ability both matter. A long message or an intelligent audience does not establish that a particular message received extensive scrutiny.

The same variable can play different roles. Expertise may serve as a shortcut, provide relevant evidence, change processing effort, bias the direction of thought, or affect confidence in thoughts already generated. It is a mistake to classify a source characteristic as permanently peripheral. Its role depends on timing, context, and what recipients infer from it.

Self-validation adds a metacognitive question: how much do recipients trust their own thoughts? Confidence can strengthen the influence of favorable thoughts, but it can also strengthen unfavorable thoughts and increase resistance. Record thought direction separately from confidence. A manipulation that increases confidence should not be expected to increase agreement regardless of what people were thinking.

The **Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)** organizes persuasion around the extent of issue-relevant thinking. The **Heuristic–Systematic Model (HSM)** distinguishes reliance on simpler judgment rules from more systematic processing; both caution against assigning one permanent role to a message feature. Expertise might act as a shortcut in one context and invite scrutiny in another. Predict the role under stated conditions rather than treating a cue as intrinsically peripheral.

**During processing**

- Motivation and ability
- Argument quality
- Expertise can serve several roles

**After thoughts form**

- Thought direction
- Confidence in those thoughts
- Greater confidence can amplify rejection

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Recipients generate mostly negative thoughts about a message. A later manipulation increases confidence in those thoughts. What does a self-validation account predict?
:::
::: tab Solution
Greater reliance on the negative thoughts can reduce persuasion. The account does not predict that confidence always helps the communicator. Check the timing of the manipulation and distinguish confidence in one’s thoughts from confidence in the source. Measure thought direction and evaluate the proposed interaction rather than only an overall main effect.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** An expert endorses a technically complex method. Give two explanations for agreement that require different evidence.

## 5.7 Receptiveness and advocacy{{attrs[#blk-wofggg0r98c7]}}

::: success
**Objective 5.7a:** Separate openness, attitude change, and advocacy effectiveness.
:::

Being willing to hear a disagreement is not the same as adopting it. **Receptiveness** concerns engagement with other views; change concerns movement in evaluation. Listening and asking substantive questions may support engagement without eliminating disagreement. Reactance can arise when a message threatens perceived freedom, but resistance should not automatically be labeled irrational.

**Advocacy** includes expressing support or opposition. Its frequency, style, audience, and success are distinct outcomes. Certainty may encourage expression, while uncertainty can also motivate discussion aimed at learning. A highly active advocate is therefore not necessarily highly certain, well informed, or effective at changing other minds.

In a seminar intervention, distinguish the advocate’s behavior from the recipient’s experience. Count substantive questions, assess perceived listening, measure willingness to return, and track evaluations separately. An intervention could improve dialogue while leaving attitudes stable. That may be a useful outcome if dialogue was the stated aim, but it should not be redescribed as evidence of persuasion.

**Engagement outcomes**

- Willingness to listen
- Questions and sustained discussion
- Perceived threat to autonomy

**Evaluation and action**

- Attitude direction and certainty
- Decision to advocate
- Effect on the recipient

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A listening workshop increases willingness to discuss a divisive issue but leaves policy evaluations unchanged. Did it fail?
:::
::: tab Solution
The answer depends on the preregistered target. It may have improved receptiveness while showing no detected attitude change. Report these separately with uncertainty. If persuasion was the target, the workshop has not demonstrated it. If constructive dialogue was the target, stable disagreement does not erase an engagement benefit.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Design a follow-up that tests whether increased advocacy actually changes another person’s view.

## Synthesis and seminar preparation{{attrs[#blk-w3q28h23p8ah]}}

An evaluation can be favorable yet unstable, mixed yet consequential, or persuasive without changing behavior. Begin by specifying what is being evaluated and how it is measured; then ask what supports the evaluation, how it changes, and when it guides action.

For the final commentary, identify a claim from a chosen article, specify the construct and measure, and develop an alternative that the design cannot yet exclude. Propose one feasible comparison and explain how its possible outcomes would change the interpretation.
