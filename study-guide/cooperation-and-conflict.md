## 12.1 Map the interdependent situation

::: success
**Objective 12.1a:** Use a payoff structure to distinguish conflicting interests from mutual benefit.
:::

**Interdependence** means outcomes depend on more than one actor’s choices. Before assigning motives, map who affects whose outcomes, what each knows, and which options are available. People may disagree about procedures while still benefiting from coordination. Friendly attitudes do not remove incompatible incentives.

A **social dilemma** involves tension between individually attractive choices and collective outcomes. Distinguish it from a fixed-sum situation, in which one party’s gain corresponds to another’s loss. Real conflicts can mix issues: an appointment is scarce, while scheduling, training, and future opportunities may permit joint gains.

The chapter examines conflicts of interest, dependence, and uncertainty. A payoff table simplifies these features; it does not prove participants calculate explicit numbers. State the model’s assumptions, then test whether participants understand the options and value outcomes as assumed.

**Map the situation**

- Available actions
- Jointly determined outcomes
- Information and uncertainty

**Interpretation limits**

- Disagreement need not be fixed-sum
- Good intentions do not remove incentives
- A model is not a report of thought

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Mutual cooperation gives each person 3 points; unilateral defection gives the defector 5 and cooperator 0; mutual defection gives each 1. Explain this hypothetical dilemma.
:::
::: tab Solution
Defection yields 5 rather than 3 if the partner cooperates, and 1 rather than 0 if the partner defects. Yet mutual cooperation gives each 3 rather than 1. Individual incentives conflict with a mutually better outcome under these payoffs.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Why check understanding of a payoff table?

## 12.2 Prosocial outcomes and inferred motives

::: success
**Objective 12.2a:** Distinguish helpful behavior from concern, equality, reciprocity, and reputation.
:::

**Prosocial behavior** is action undertaken with the aim of benefiting others, even when the attempt fails. An **altruistic motive** makes another’s welfare an ultimate goal rather than only a means to personal reward (Li, n.d.-b). A beneficial act can also serve reputation, future reciprocity, or personal reward. The act alone does not uniquely reveal its motive. This retains a central distinction from the earlier prosocial lecture.

People can attend to their own outcomes, others’ outcomes, equality, and reciprocal treatment. These concerns coincide in some situations and diverge in others. One actor may prefer equal allocations; another may prioritize need. A single allocation cannot distinguish motives when several preferences predict it.

Design comparisons that separate predictions. Vary visibility, future interaction, or beneficiary need, while identifying what else changes. Motives may coexist. Explain what a result makes more plausible without claiming access to the actor’s complete internal state.

**Keep constructs distinct**

- Intent to benefit another
- Other’s welfare as an ultimate goal
- Equality and reciprocity

**Discriminating conditions**

- Public versus private conduct
- Future interaction versus one encounter
- Equal resources versus unequal need

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A volunteer publicly helps and receives praise. Is this purely altruistic?
:::
::: tab Solution
The act can benefit others while concern, enjoyment, reputation, and reciprocity coexist. Compare conditions that alter incentives. Public praise does not establish that concern is absent, and the helpful act does not establish a pure motive.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Does an equal split prove indifference to need?

## 12.3 The boundaries of cooperation

::: success
**Objective 12.3a:** Explain how within-group cooperation can coexist with between-group conflict.
:::

Cooperation has beneficiaries and boundaries. A coordinated team can produce public benefits or exclude outsiders. **Parochial cooperation** privileges a narrower circle; broader concern extends beyond it. High ingroup contribution is not sufficient evidence of universal prosociality.

Representation can change a decision’s meaning. A representative may feel responsible for protecting constituents and become less flexible toward another group. Behavior that appears cooperative within the ingroup may increase between-group competition. Evaluate outcomes for all affected parties.

Networks shape interaction, reputational knowledge, and visible norms. Repeated contact can support cooperation while trust remains limited across boundaries. Connect information diffusion in Module 9 and identity in Module 11 by asking who enters cooperative relationships and who bears their costs.

**Levels**

- Within a dyad
- Within a group
- Across group boundaries

**Boundary questions**

- Who benefits?
- Who bears costs?
- What changes under representation?

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A team shares information internally while blocking another team’s access to shared equipment. Is it cooperative?
:::
::: tab Solution
It cooperates internally but imposes costs across the boundary. Specify the level and beneficiaries. Evaluate internal coordination and effects on other users rather than giving one global label.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** How could representation increase conflict despite good intentions toward constituents?

## 12.4 Communication, trust, and considerate choices

::: success
**Objective 12.4a:** Explain how communication and preserving options can support cooperation.
:::

Communication can reveal priorities, establish commitments, and expose misunderstandings. Its effectiveness depends on credibility and context. A discussion does not automatically change incompatible incentives, and an untrusted promise may have little effect.

**Social mindfulness** includes choices that preserve another person’s options. Selecting one of several identical items can leave the next chooser meaningful alternatives; taking the only distinctive item removes one. Such choices make another’s agency relevant without implying an obligation to surrender every preference.

Trust involves expectations and accepting vulnerability. Separate it from verified trustworthiness and from a demand to accept risk. Study behavior, available information, and safeguards that make cooperation feasible. Considerate conduct, communication, and institutional protections can complement one another.

Interaction **noise** is an unintended difference between what an actor means to deliver and what the partner receives. A delayed message may look like deliberate disregard. Strict retaliation can amplify this error. Honest clarification and a margin of generosity may interrupt the sequence, although they do not remove the need to address persistent harmful behavior.

**Possible supports**

- Clarify priorities
- Make credible commitments
- Preserve another’s options

**Distinctions**

- Trust versus trustworthiness
- Talk versus changed incentives
- Consideration versus unlimited sacrifice

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Three blue notebooks and one green notebook remain. How can the first chooser preserve options?
:::
::: tab Solution
Choosing blue leaves both colors available to the next person. Choosing green removes that option. This illustrates choice structure, not a definitive moral diagnosis: preferences, awareness, and information still matter.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** More discussion produces no improvement. Does this disprove all benefits of communication?

## 12.5 Conflict, aggression, and escalation

::: success
**Objective 12.5a:** Distinguish incompatible interests from intended harm and analyze escalation.
:::

A bridge to the earlier aggression lecture is useful: **aggression** concerns conduct aimed at harming a target who wants to avoid that harm (Li, n.d.-a), whereas **conflict** concerns incompatibility or perceived opposition. Conflict can be managed without aggression. A harmful outcome does not itself establish harmful intent. This module examines conflict processes rather than providing an exhaustive review of aggression theories.

Escalation can involve reciprocal responses, hostile interpretations, and growing investment in a contest. Each side may regard its action as a response, so locating a provocative personality is not a complete explanation. Trace events and options at each stage.

Changing dependence, boundaries, or incentives may matter more than asking actors to feel warmer. Apologies require interpretation through acknowledgment, credibility, and subsequent conduct. Explaining reconciliation must not become a demand that a harmed person forgive or continue an unsafe interaction.

**Conceptual boundaries**

- Conflict: incompatible interests
- Aggression: intended, unwanted harm
- Harm: an outcome

**Process questions**

- What preceded the response?
- Which interpretation guided action?
- Could the situation change?

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Collaborators dispute authorship and start withholding information. What should a process account examine?
:::
::: tab Solution
Map decisions, expectations, allocation rules, and consequences. Determine whether harm was intended rather than assuming it from disagreement. Consider procedures that clarify contributions and reduce retaliation incentives.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Is demanding immediate forgiveness a sufficient strategy?

## 12.6 Negotiation and third-party involvement

::: success
**Objective 12.6a:** Distinguish process control from decision control in conflict resolution.
:::

A procedure distributes authority over how discussion occurs and who selects its result. A third party may facilitate communication without deciding the outcome, or make a decision after hearing the parties. These arrangements distribute **process control** and **decision control** differently.

The chapter uses this distinction to organize roles. Mediation emphasizes process assistance while parties retain decision control; adjudication gives the third party the decision. Other arrangements combine control over both aspects or neither. State actual powers because institutional details vary.

Evaluate voice, information, implementation, fairness, and substantive consequences. Agreement alone does not demonstrate fairness; a party may accept because alternatives are worse. Disagreement may reveal a real incompatibility. Investigate power and available options alongside the procedure’s label.

**Control questions**

- Who organizes the process?
- Who determines the result?
- What participation is possible?

**Evaluation**

- Voice and information
- Implementation
- Fairness and substantive effects

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A facilitator structures discussion, but either party can reject a proposal. Which distinction matters?
:::
::: tab Solution
The facilitator influences process while parties retain decision control, resembling mediation. Specify voluntariness and actual powers because procedures can differ from the simple category.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A neutral party imposes a decision. What should be assessed beyond completion?

## 12.7 Reputation, sanctions, and institutions

::: success
**Objective 12.7a:** Evaluate benefits and costs of institutional supports for cooperation.
:::

Future interaction, reputation, and rules can support cooperation by changing expected consequences. They need not make everyone more altruistic. A rule may work through incentives and expectations rather than enduring personal change.

Sanctions can deter harmful conduct but impose costs and may be perceived as unfair. Ask who monitors, who imposes consequences, whether mistakes can be corrected, and whether enforcement is consistent. Reputation depends on information quality; inaccurate reports can undermine cooperation or unjustly exclude people.

Track compliance alongside costs, unequal burdens, trust, and repair. A laboratory result can identify a mechanism under defined conditions; applying it requires attention to implementation. Evaluate persistence rather than assuming an immediate behavioral improvement is a complete institutional success.

**Supports**

- Repeated interaction
- Credible reputation
- Rules and sanctions

**Tradeoffs**

- Enforcement costs
- Errors and unequal burdens
- Trust and repair

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Cleanup fines improve compliance, but disputed penalties rise and junior staff pay most fines. What belongs in the evaluation?
:::
::: tab Solution
Report compliance alongside accuracy, burden distribution, and trust. Investigate schedules and unclear duties. Consider clearer responsibilities and an appeal procedure. Compliance alone does not establish fairness or sustainability.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Does cooperation under monitoring establish changed prosocial motivation?

## 12.8 Integrate a multilevel explanation

::: success
**Objective 12.8a:** Connect a situation, its interpretation, interaction, and institutions in a testable account.
:::

The synthesis asks how outcomes are jointly determined, how actors interpret their options, how actions influence subsequent responses, and which rules sustain the pattern. Addressing only one question can miss why an intervention fails.

Connect modules selectively. Attitudes concern evaluations; goals organize action; identity defines memberships; emotion influences interpretation; cultural practices shape meanings; relationships create histories. Specify links supported by evidence and mark those that remain assumptions. Listing constructs is not an explanation.

For the final commentary or proposal, choose a bounded claim, identify a credible alternative, and design a separating comparison. Consider outcomes for differently affected groups and a result that would change the account. A sharper distinction or better test can constitute an original contribution without promising a universal solution.

**Explanatory levels**

- Situation and incentives
- Interpretation and interaction
- Networks and institutions

**Contribution**

- Bounded claim
- Competing explanation
- Result that changes the conclusion

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A cooperative scheduling app fails. Give two explanations with different implications.
:::
::: tab Solution
It may preserve incompatible incentives, or users may misunderstand or distrust its records. Assess incentives and comprehension separately. Vary rule clarity and incentive alignment while measuring use and equitable access.
:::
::::
