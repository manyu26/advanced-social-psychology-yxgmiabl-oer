## 11.1 Stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination{{attrs[#blk-eyh239tc86s9]}}

::: success
**Objective 11.1a:** Distinguish beliefs, evaluations, and treatment while identifying their levels of analysis.
:::

A **stereotype** concerns attributed characteristics, **prejudice** concerns evaluation of a group or its members, and **discrimination** concerns differential treatment tied to group membership. These constructs can be related without being interchangeable. A favorable description can still restrict opportunity: portraying a group as naturally suited to supportive work can help justify excluding its members from leadership.

The unit of analysis matters. A person may express little hostility while participating in procedures that repeatedly disadvantage a group. Conversely, unequal outcomes alone do not identify the particular psychological process that produced them. Institutional rules, access to preparation, interpersonal decisions, and historical arrangements can operate together. Ask which comparison would distinguish these contributions.

The earlier prejudice lecture separated evaluation and treatment. Extend that distinction to organizational evidence. Specify whether an outcome is an evaluation, a decision, or an allocation of resources. Then state what would count as evidence against the proposed mechanism, rather than treating every inequality as a direct measure of personal animus.

**Constructs**

- Attributed traits
- Group-directed evaluation
- Differential treatment

**Evidence choices**

- Descriptions or expectations
- Evaluative measures
- Decisions and resource allocation

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A manager describes women as caring and assigns them support tasks instead of leadership opportunities. Identify the distinctions.
:::
::: tab Solution
The trait attribution is a stereotype; its positive tone does not establish equal treatment. The assignment pattern can restrict opportunities. Compare assignments under equivalent qualifications and examine the decision process. The description alone does not establish the manager’s complete emotional evaluation.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Unequal promotion rates identify one prejudiced decision maker: evaluate this claim.

## 11.2 Categorization and social identity{{attrs[#blk-scdwl7kwjuy4]}}

::: success
**Objective 11.2a:** Distinguish ingroup preference from outgroup hostility in group-based behavior.
:::

People can define themselves as distinct individuals or as members of a social category. **Social identity** concerns group memberships that become part of the self in a particular context. Categorization can change comparison standards, the meaning of outcomes, and expectations. A category need not be permanently central to every member.

Minimal-group research examines distinctions created with little substantive history. Group-based allocation patterns do not always require an established material conflict. This does not show that categorization invariably causes hatred or that laboratory allocation tasks reproduce entrenched inequality. Expectations of reciprocity and task meaning can contribute to behavior.

Separate **ingroup favoritism** from **outgroup derogation**. Giving an extra benefit to one group can create inequality without an intention to harm another. A design measuring only the difference between two allocations may obscure that distinction. Include a meaningful baseline and separate opportunities to benefit or disadvantage recipients. This connects the earlier group-process lecture to intergroup research.

**Identity processes**

- Context makes a category salient
- Membership informs the self
- Comparison standards change

**Inference limits**

- Favoritism differs from hostility
- Baselines reveal different patterns
- Task meaning and reciprocity matter

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Participants give an ingroup member 6 tokens and an outgroup member 4. What comparison helps distinguish favoritism from derogation?
:::
::: tab Solution
Include a comparable recipient without the relevant group distinction. A neutral allocation of 4 is consistent with an ingroup bonus; a neutral allocation of 6 suggests an outgroup disadvantage. Neither pattern alone proves a hostile motive. The baseline must be comparable.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Would a minimal-group effect establish that history is irrelevant to discrimination?

## 11.3 Content, status, and forms of bias{{attrs[#blk-pdi89u6k45ug]}}

::: success
**Objective 11.3a:** Analyze how apparently helpful behavior can preserve unequal status.
:::

Group judgments differ in content, not only overall favorability. The chapter discusses **warmth and competence** as dimensions of group images. A group can be regarded sympathetically while being denied competence, or respected for ability while treated with suspicion. These combinations can relate to different emotional and behavioral responses.

Help therefore needs closer analysis. Assistance that enables independent action differs from assistance that maintains dependence on the helper. A benevolent practice can protect a hierarchy if it withholds control, credit, or opportunities to develop capability. This concerns the structure and consequences of help; it does not mean that all assistance is oppressive.

Ask who defines the need, who controls the resource, and whether recipients gain future options. The earlier lecture’s discussion of subtle bias becomes more informative when students trace a pathway from a group image to a decision and its consequences. Measure each link instead of inferring a complete motive from one outcome.

**Images of groups**

- Perceived warmth
- Perceived competence
- Mixed evaluations

**Consequences to inspect**

- Access to autonomy
- Control over resources
- Opportunity and dependence

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A program completes tasks for a disadvantaged group but excludes members from training and decisions. Why is counting help episodes insufficient?
:::
::: tab Solution
The amount of assistance does not reveal whether recipients gain capability or remain dependent. Assess control, opportunities, and recipient preferences. Support could be redesigned to expand training and participation. Evidence about effects is needed before assigning one motive to helpers.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Can a positive stereotype guarantee a benefit to its targets?

## 11.4 Legitimacy and responses to inequality{{attrs[#blk-0ohw87qfy1fv]}}

::: success
**Objective 11.4a:** Compare hierarchy maintenance with responses available to lower-status group members.
:::

**Social dominance orientation** concerns preference for group-based hierarchy; **system justification** concerns legitimizing existing arrangements. These ideas overlap in some settings but ask different questions. Supporting an institution is not automatically a preference for every hierarchy, and a preference measure does not fully explain institutional persistence.

Responses to lower status depend partly on how a situation is understood. People may pursue individual mobility, redefine valued comparisons through social creativity, or engage in collective action. Perceived permeability of boundaries and the legitimacy or stability of inequality can affect which response seems feasible. These are conditional explanations, not personality types assigned to entire groups.

A person may decline protest because participation is costly, not because the arrangement seems fair. Another may pursue advancement while supporting collective change. Study meanings, risks, alternatives, and behavior over time. Avoid interpreting one choice as a transparent indicator of consent to inequality.

**Explanatory questions**

- Is hierarchy preferred?
- Is the arrangement legitimized?
- Which alternatives seem possible?

**Possible responses**

- Individual mobility
- Social creativity
- Collective action

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Employees accept an unequal policy but fear retaliation. Does compliance establish perceived legitimacy?
:::
::: tab Solution
No. Compliance can reflect constraints and costs. Measure legitimacy judgments separately and compare settings with different protections for dissent. Beliefs and opportunities may jointly shape action.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** How could individual mobility and collective action coexist?

## 11.5 Threat, history, and collective emotions{{attrs[#blk-0xof118to20j]}}

::: success
**Objective 11.5a:** Explain how interpretations of history and threat can sustain conflict.
:::

Conflict includes interpretations of what happened, who bears responsibility, and what is feared. Threat may concern resources, standing, values, or recognition. Different interpretations can mobilize similar behavior, so opposition alone does not reveal which concern operates.

Collective guilt, victimhood, anger, and identification can shape reactions to historical harm. Acknowledgment may seem a route to repair to some people and a challenge to group identity to others. Digital environments can amplify conflict, but exposure, audience incentives, and message meanings require investigation. A platform does not have one uniform psychological effect.

Group labels should not erase internal disagreement. Identify speakers, audiences, institutions, and historical claims. Distinguish explaining resistance from endorsing it. Compare accounts of the same reaction and ask whether a response reduces defensiveness while recognizing harm and supporting repair.

**Interpretations**

- Material or symbolic threat
- Responsibility for harm
- Recognition of victimhood

**Research safeguards**

- Measure distinct concerns
- Sample disagreement within groups
- Track recognition and repair separately

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Two communities oppose a memorial: one cites exclusion of its history; another fears resource loss. Why might one reassurance fail?
:::
::: tab Solution
Similar opposition can arise from different concerns. Funding reassurance may not address recognition; symbolic inclusion may not address costs. Measure both concerns and test targeted responses while allowing mixed motives.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Does lower collective guilt always indicate reconciliation?

## 11.6 Measuring bias and testing mechanisms{{attrs[#blk-84f15dibbkzp]}}

::: success
**Objective 11.6a:** Match measures and comparisons to a bounded claim about intergroup behavior.
:::

Bias research uses reports, response-time tasks, decisions, physiological observations, and institutional records. Each provides evidence about an operationalized outcome under specified conditions. An indirect task does not reveal a hidden essence of a person. Reports also depend on questions, reporting capacity, and context.

Biological responses require the same care. A group-related signal does not uniquely identify prejudice or establish that a social pattern is inevitable. Categories, expectations, experience, and task demands can affect its meaning. Avoid replacing a psychological explanation with the name of a brain region.

Specify the mechanism before choosing the measure. Distinguish reliability from predictive validity. A correspondence experiment with matched applications can test treatment under its conditions; it does not directly measure every decision maker’s emotion. The interpretation must stay at the level supported by the comparison.

**Measurement distinctions**

- Reported evaluation
- Task-specific response
- Observed treatment

**Design questions**

- Is the measure reliable?
- Does it predict the outcome?
- Which mechanism is isolated?

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A participant receives a personal label solely from one indirect-task score. What is wrong?
:::
::: tab Solution
The score describes performance under a procedure, with uncertainty and limited scope. It does not warrant a comprehensive personal label or establish discriminatory conduct. Assess reliability, validity, and the relevant behavioral prediction.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Matched applications receive 30% versus 20% callbacks in a hypothetical experiment. Interpret the difference.

## 11.7 Contact and intervention design{{attrs[#blk-3mbhxkjp2k7z]}}

::: success
**Objective 11.7a:** Evaluate contact using interaction quality, status, and consequences for inequality.
:::

Contact can support revised expectations and trust, but cooperative work, proximity, hostile encounters, and unequal service roles are different experiences. Study interaction quality, the wider setting, and the intended outcomes rather than treating contact as a uniform treatment.

Reduced discomfort is not necessarily reduced inequality. Positive interaction may improve feelings while leaving barriers intact; it can also change willingness to recognize disadvantage. This makes evaluation criteria consequential. Include perspectives and opportunities of differently positioned groups.

Identity approaches can emphasize common membership or recognize subgroups within a broader category. Neither is universally best. A shared label may support inclusion yet obscure experiences if imposed carelessly. Test trust, recognition, equitable participation, behavior, and persistence beyond the immediate activity.

Classical facilitating conditions include equal status within the interaction, shared goals pursued cooperatively, support from relevant authorities, and meaningful opportunities to become acquainted. These are conditions that can support beneficial contact, not a guarantee of success or a requirement that every benefit must involve every condition.

**Contact features**

- Quality and cooperation
- Relative status and voice
- Support from the setting

**Outcomes beyond liking**

- Recognition of disadvantage
- Equitable participation
- Durable behavioral change

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A workshop improves liking but leaves unequal speaking time unchanged. What follows?
:::
::: tab Solution
It may improve the measured evaluation without demonstrating equal participation. Report outcomes separately, examine whose liking changed, and test participation rules. A partial benefit is not evidence of every intended effect.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** What risk arises when common identity requires ignoring subgroup experiences?
