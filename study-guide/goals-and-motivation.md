## Module logic

Goals connect desired outcomes with actions. This module follows goal systems from selection through sustained pursuit, then examines conflicts and social coordination. Progress is evaluated against the intended outcome rather than effort alone.

## 8.1 Goals and means

::: success
**Objective 8.1a:** Analyze goal hierarchies, equifinality, and multifinality.
:::

A **goal** represents a desired state that the person wishes to pursue. Favoring an outcome without an inclination to act differs from holding it as an active goal. Goals may be concrete targets or continuing aspirations. The same activity can be a goal at one level and a means to a broader goal at another.

Goal systems organize relationships among ends and means. **Equifinality** means that different routes can serve one goal. **Multifinality** means that one route can serve several goals. A writing group might support manuscript completion, learning, and belonging, while several other activities could also serve each end.

More connections are not automatically better. Alternative routes provide flexibility but can dilute commitment to a particular route. A means serving several ends may be attractive yet perceived as less specialized for any one of them. Separate the actual usefulness of a route from the person’s belief about it. A useful intervention can change either the structure of opportunity or how the structure is represented.

Unifinality describes a means linked to one focal goal; counterfinality describes advancing one goal while obstructing another. Distinguish commitment to a goal from attachment to one means: changing an ineffective strategy may preserve pursuit of the goal rather than signal abandonment.

**Goal structure**

- Desired end state
- Subordinate means
- Hierarchies depend on the question

**Patterns of connection**

- Several means for one goal
- One means for several goals
- Perceived usefulness can differ from actual usefulness

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A student uses a writing group for productivity and friendship, and also writes alone to advance the manuscript. Identify both structural patterns.
:::
::: tab Solution
The group is multifinal because it serves productivity and friendship. The group and solo writing are equifinal routes to manuscript progress. These labels describe connections; they do not establish which route is most effective or motivating. Measure progress and perceived instrumentality separately.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Adding three alternative study strategies reduces use of the original strategy. Does that prove motivation to learn declined?

## 8.2 What makes pursuit worthwhile

::: success
**Objective 8.2a:** Distinguish intrinsic value, external outcomes, and regulatory orientations.
:::

Intrinsic motivation involves value in the activity itself. Extrinsic motivation concerns outcomes separable from doing the activity. Both can occur together: a student may enjoy solving a problem while also seeking a qualification. The distinction should not be reduced to whether money is present or whether the outcome is socially valued.

Approach and avoidance goals emphasize attaining a desired state or preventing an undesired one. Promotion and prevention concerns further distinguish aspirations and advancement from duties and security. These distinctions can shape what success and failure mean. They should not be used to assign permanent types to people without measuring the relevant context.

Autonomy, competence, and relatedness provide useful connections to self-determination perspectives discussed in the earlier lectures. Supporting choice and meaningful reasons differs from abandoning structure. A study should measure whether an activity feels worthwhile and manageable, and whether the environment permits action. Enjoyment alone cannot remove missing resources or unclear expectations.

**Sources of value**

- Enjoyment in doing
- Benefits of the outcome
- Several motives can coexist

**Orientation toward the goal**

- Attaining desired states
- Avoiding undesired states
- Advancement or responsibility

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A student enjoys coding and also wants a research job. Must the coding goal be classified as exclusively intrinsic or extrinsic?
:::
::: tab Solution
No. The activity can carry immediate enjoyment and value as a route to employment. Measure each source of motivation separately. A change in one does not imply that the other disappeared. The relevant prediction may concern persistence during practice versus commitment to obtaining the credential.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A supervisor offers unrestricted choice but no guidance. Why might that fail to support motivation?

## 8.3 Targets and incentives

::: success
**Objective 8.3a:** Evaluate target setting and incentives against the desired outcome.
:::

A target specifies a level or endpoint against which progress can be assessed. A useful target must be connected to actions the person can identify and perform. Monitoring can support a cycle of comparing the current state with the target, acting, and reassessing. A number without a feasible route does not supply that route.

Incentives can change what people attend to and work toward. They may increase effort on a measured outcome while neglecting quality or other goals. Rewards can also alter the meaning of an activity. The chapter discusses circumstances in which an added incentive undermines engagement, particularly when the activity becomes understood as a means to a reward that later disappears.

Avoid universal claims that rewards always help or always destroy intrinsic motivation. Specify the task, reward arrangement, timing, and outcome. A payment for completed pages may improve page production without improving reasoning. Include the quality criterion and consider what happens after the incentive ends.

**Targets**

- Desired level and deadline
- Feasible actions
- Feedback about the gap

**Incentive design**

- What is rewarded?
- What may be neglected?
- What happens after removal?

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A hypothetical reading program rewards the number of articles opened. Students open more articles but cannot explain them better. What changed?
:::
::: tab Solution
The rewarded action increased; demonstrated understanding did not improve in the observed comparison. Opening articles is an imperfect proxy for learning. Measure comprehension and sustained engagement, and test whether the incentive shifts effort toward superficial completion. The count should not be relabeled as learning.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Design an incentive study that can detect both short-term gains and later loss of engagement.

## 8.4 Progress and persistence

::: success
**Objective 8.4a:** Explain how progress framing and implementation plans affect continued pursuit.
:::

A goal intention identifies an outcome; an **implementation intention** connects a relevant situation with a planned action. Planning when and where to write can help bridge wanting a manuscript and beginning a writing session. Plans still depend on feasible actions and available resources.

Progress can change motivation in different ways. Approaching an all-or-nothing endpoint can make the next step especially valuable. For continuing or aspirational goals, progress may instead suggest that sufficient effort has been invested, making another goal more attractive. Attention to completed versus remaining work can change how progress is experienced.

The middle of a pursuit may feel less distinctive than the beginning or end. Shorter subgoals can make progress visible, but should not encourage checking boxes at the expense of the substantive aim. Assess sustained work and quality together. Faster completion can coexist with weaker standards, so speed alone is not a complete measure of motivation.

**Link intention to action**

- Relevant situational cue
- Specific feasible response
- A plan for predictable obstacles

**Interpret progress**

- Proximity can increase value
- Progress can license switching
- Quality can diverge from speed

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A student has completed 8 of 10 required sections. What percentage of the original target remains, and why does that number not determine motivation?
:::
::: tab Solution
Two sections remain, or 20% of the original target. The next section completes half of what remains. Motivation also depends on perceived difficulty, goal commitment, and what the endpoint means. These hypothetical proportions illustrate framing; they do not establish a universal psychological response.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A plan says, “I will become a better writer.” Turn it into an implementation plan without replacing the broader goal.

## 8.5 Learning from feedback

::: success
**Objective 8.5a:** Distinguish motivational and informational barriers to learning from failure.
:::

Feedback can influence both commitment and knowledge. Negative feedback may threaten the self, reduce attention, or make someone question whether the goal is worth pursuing. It can also be difficult to interpret: learning that an answer is wrong does not always reveal what the right answer should be.

These barriers require different repairs. Reducing defensiveness may improve willingness to engage, while explicit explanation can improve the information available for correction. Observing another person’s mistake can reduce personal threat but may also make the lesson seem less relevant. The effectiveness of a strategy depends on the barrier it addresses.

A growth-oriented interpretation treats ability as capable of development, but it does not guarantee progress. Practice quality, instructional support, opportunities, and task demands remain important. Connect this section to Module 4: feeling encouraged after criticism is different from making an accurate revision. Test both outcomes when a feedback intervention claims to improve learning.

**Why feedback may be missed**

- Threat to self-view
- Withdrawal of effort
- Difficulty inferring the correction

**Match the response**

- Reduce defensive processing
- Clarify the actionable lesson
- Measure the next attempt

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A student accepts that an answer was wrong but still cannot solve a new problem. Which barrier might remain?
:::
::: tab Solution
The student may lack information about the correct principle or how to transfer it. Willingness to accept criticism is not sufficient for learning. Provide a diagnostic explanation and test a new problem, while considering other barriers such as prerequisite knowledge. The result need not indicate defensiveness.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** An intervention improves confidence after failure. What evidence would support a learning benefit?

## 8.6 Conflicts and self-control

::: success
**Objective 8.6a:** Analyze competing goals and distinguish strategic self-control from effortful inhibition.
:::

People often pursue goals that compete for time or resources. They can compromise, alternate, or prioritize. Whether activities are seen as substitutes or complements matters: completing one substitute may justify moving on, while completing one complement may make the next step more useful. Goal conflict does not always have a single correct solution.

Self-control involves recognizing a conflict and responding to it. A tempting action may seem harmless when considered once but consequential as part of a repeated pattern. Broader framing can make that pattern visible. Strategies can also operate before temptation becomes intense by changing cues, routines, or access.

Habits link contexts with responses and can support or undermine current goals. Strong self-control need not mean frequent heroic resistance. It may reflect an environment with fewer conflicts or routines that make desired action easier. Avoid treating delayed reward choices as a pure measure of character; available options and their credibility affect the choice.

**Managing several goals**

- Compromise or alternate
- Prioritize one goal
- Distinguish substitutes from complements

**Self-control strategies**

- Recognize the recurring conflict
- Change cues and routines
- Inhibit an activated response when needed

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A student stops checking messages while writing after moving the phone out of the room. Is this a failure to develop self-control?
:::
::: tab Solution
No. Changing the situation can be an effective self-control strategy. The outcome does not require repeated inhibition of an available cue. Evaluate whether the arrangement supports the intended work and whether it is sustainable. Effortful resistance is one possible process, not the definition of successful regulation.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A study finds that people who wait for a larger reward later achieve better outcomes. What prevents a simple causal conclusion?

## 8.7 Pursuit with other people

::: success
**Objective 8.7a:** Distinguish social facilitation, reduced effort, and coordinated division of labor.
:::

Others can support goal pursuit by providing resources, making progress visible, or sharing work. Their presence can also create evaluation pressure or competition. Social facilitation accounts distinguish effects on well-practiced versus difficult responses rather than predicting that an audience always helps.

When a group works together, lower individual effort may reflect social loafing, but it may also reflect efficient coordination. Judge the individual’s contribution alongside the group’s output and the task’s interdependence. A reduction in duplicate work can improve the collective result even while each person spends less time.

Partners may imitate one another, compensate for one another, or treat a partner’s action as completing a shared goal. Identity and perceived complementarity shape these possibilities. The capstone is to design a writing-group intervention that specifies individual and collective targets, records contributions, and tests whether support improves the substantive product.

The earlier group-process lecture’s social-facilitation distinction predicts that others’ presence can strengthen a dominant response: this may improve well-practiced performance and impair difficult or unfamiliar performance. Treat task difficulty, skill, audience, and evaluation as conditions to investigate. A difference between solitary and group performance does not by itself distinguish facilitation, coordination loss, and reduced effort.

**Audience and performance**

- Task familiarity
- Evaluation pressure
- Competition and distraction

**Shared work**

- Individual contribution
- Collective output
- Coordination versus free riding

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Four students each spend less time reviewing a manuscript after dividing its sections. Is this social loafing?
:::
::: tab Solution
Not necessarily. If coverage and quality improve because redundant work declines, the pattern may be efficient division of labor. Measure individual contributions and the final review. Social loafing concerns reduced effort in a collective setting; inferring it requires more than a lower time total after task redesign.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Design a writing-group study that distinguishes social support from simply adding more working time.
