## Module Logic{{attrs[#blk-7uutkuf71wd7]}}

This is the course's first substantive module, and it takes up the most basic social-psychological process:
one mind making sense of another. The chapter's organizing device is time — what a perceiver has before
meeting, in the first instant, moments later, across encounters, and once both parties are doing all of it to
each other simultaneously. Its organizing *argument* is that accuracy is the wrong default goal.

**Visual description:** A vertical chain following the chapter's temporal organization, from pre-encounter
baselines through first-instant categorization, moments-later state decoding, and trait inference across
encounters, ending in full interdependent interaction. A separate node — pragmatics rather than accuracy —
connects to both ends of the chain, marking it as the stance that governs the whole sequence rather than a
stage within it. The chain terminates in the chapter's closing turn: perceivers retain their inferences while
losing the evidence that produced them.

::: warning
**A boundary on the stage model.** The authors are explicit that the distinctions between demographics,
states and traits at best approximate reality, blur at the boundaries, and define types somewhat arbitrarily.
They ask readers to squint a little. Treat the sequence as a scaffold for organizing processes, not a claim
that impression formation proceeds through discrete phases.
:::

## 3.1 The Miracle, and Why Accuracy Is the Wrong Default{{attrs[#blk-oljvrw9nbhc5]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Explain what "knowing the unknowable" names and why the review is organized temporally.
- Articulate the criterion problem in accuracy research.
- Distinguish the three sources of variance in accuracy.
:::

Human beings work one another out by trying to read what is going on in another head. The difficulty is stated in the
chapter's title: the other person's mind is **ultimately unknowable**. So the review is not about what
perceivers know but about the broad range of what people take themselves to be able to know, organized by what perceivers
know when.

The chapter aims to be data-driven — describing the patterns of evidence people actually use at different
points in impression formation, rather than starting from theory. That approach surfaces a finding worth
sitting with. Work on person perception, the authors observe, has seldom concerned **persons** as entire self-governing individuals, nor **perception** in the strict sense of what registers directly at the eye or ear. What perceivers mostly evaluate are cues in isolation; the people being perceived arrive as partial, broken-up,
motionless fragments; and what gets called perception is in fact cognition and judgment. That is a critique of the
field's own construct, delivered inside a review of it, and it is worth noticing how it echoes Module 2's
concern that constructs and their operationalizations drift apart.

**Beyond accuracy as a goal.** One might expect accuracy to be person perception's main goal. Several lines of
evidence argue against that assumption, and the difficulties begin with measurement.

::: warning
**The criterion problem.** How do we know there is a *there* there — what is the best indicator of the
target's actual feelings or personality, against which to compare the perceiver's judgment? Targets'
self-report cannot simply serve as the objective criterion. And even if the field settled on a standard, the
sheer distance between standard and judgment does not capture the perceiver's *ability* to be accurate. This
is exactly the class of problem Module 2 described: without a validated criterion, a well-powered study of
accuracy measures something, but not necessarily accuracy.
:::

Accuracy also has more than one possible home. It might reside in the **perceiver**, who is simply good at reading minds generally. It might reside in the **target**, who is simply an open book that any perceiver could
read. Or it might reside in the **relationship** — perceiver and target resonating in a way particular to
them. These three sources of variance were implied by a critique showing that earlier research was doing the
mathematics wrong; and even corrected, the mathematics said nothing about the perceiver's processes.

**Self-check:**

- Why can't a target's self-report serve as the criterion for whether a perceiver read them accurately?
- If accuracy resides in a relationship rather than a person, what does that imply for how it should be studied?
- Restate the authors' critique of the terms "person" and "perception" in your own words.

## 3.2 Baselines: What Perceivers Know Before Meeting{{attrs[#blk-vgvyi3fu8dr6]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Explain why person perception begins before sensory input.
- Trace the inference from intent to mind to mentalizing.
- Describe the valence and content of pre-encounter expectations.
:::

The most counterintuitive claim in the chapter comes first: **person perception commences before any sensory
input.** Perceivers show up already treating the target as a person who intends things, and intending things brings a
whole inner life with it. Many are disposed favorably toward the target before any meeting takes place.

The inferential chain runs like this. Perceivers **seek the target's intent**, viewing intent as reflecting
long-term behavioral tendencies and personality. **Having intent implies having a mind.** The habitual way of construing another person is in terms of whatever mental furniture marks a mind out as human: others are not taken as objects shoved about by external forces, but as driven from within by what they believe, want and feel. That assumption is held so firmly that explanation reaches first for causes inside the person and only afterwards for causes outside — a default strong enough that it is applied at times even to
non-human targets. Intent is inferred from the target having had a choice and attending to one alternative,
especially when it is the harder one.

::: info
**Notice what has already happened.** Before a word is exchanged, the perceiver has committed to treating the
target as an autonomous, intentional causal agent whose unobservable states and traits can be inferred from
behavior — which requires the perceiver to have a *mentalizing imagination*. Every later process operates on
top of that commitment. When you meet the fundamental attribution error at the end of this module, note that
its raw material was laid down here: the intentional default is an explanatory preference for internal causes,
installed before any evidence arrives.
:::

Pre-encounter expectations have both **valence** — a broadly optimistic stance — and **content**: that humans
have agency and experience complex emotions.

**Self-check:**

- Why does the chapter treat "having intent implies having a mind" as an inference rather than a definition?
- What does it tell us that the intentional default is sometimes applied to non-human targets?

## 3.3 Freeze-Frame: Categories, Warmth, and Competence{{attrs[#blk-z95sm02p8jn5]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Describe what is extracted automatically in the first moment of encounter.
- Explain the warmth and competence dimensions and what each signals.
- Connect the two-dimensional space to intergroup outcomes.
:::

On meeting, an opening impression of where the target sits socially — gender, race, class — is registered
without effort. Surface cues are read off and converted into a guess at group membership. Classifying an action can yield a trait on the spot: handing over money reads as generosity. So the
perceiver mind-reads from the outset — though the decision this mostly serves is a binary one: **approach**, if the
target reads as one of us, or **avoid**, if they read as one of them.

What the categories primarily imply are two dimensions:

:::: cols
::: col
**Warmth / communality**
The target's **intent** — their interest in playing well with others. Signals **safety**.
:::
::: col
**Competence**
The target's **capability**. Signals **dominance**.
:::
::::

These are not independent checkboxes. They **interact**: a target scoring low on communality and warmth might
well be higher on the other dimension. Their combinations, in the Stereotype Content Model, map onto distinct
social positions — the corner where both run high belongs to one's own group and its friends, while the corner where both run low
is occupied by the groups a society pushes furthest out. An individual's perceived **interdependence** with the perceiver
predicts their perceived warmth.

::: warning
This is the module's highest-yield content for later work. Module 11 takes up prejudice, stereotyping and
discrimination, and it will not introduce a separate mechanism — stereotyping *is* this category-based process
applied to social groups, and the warmth–competence space carries over directly. Learn it properly here.
:::

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A student objects: "If categorization happens automatically from superficial cues and delivers an
approach-or-avoid decision, then person perception is just prejudice with a neutral name. Why does the field
treat these as different topics?"

Evaluate this objection using the chapter's framework.
:::
::: tab Solution
The objection is partly right, and the interesting work is in locating where it overreaches.

**Where it is right.** The chapter does not soften the continuity. Freeze-frame demographic categories
register an opening impression of social standing without effort, off surface cues; the perceiver
mind-reads from the outset; and the primary output is whether to approach the target as ingroup or avoid them
as outgroup. The warmth–competence dimensions that organize person perception are the same dimensions that
organize the Stereotype Content Model's account of how groups are placed, including which groups are seen as
low on both. A student who expected a clean separation between "perceiving individuals" and "stereotyping
groups" should give that expectation up.

**Where it overreaches — three points.**

*Categorization is one stage, not the whole process.* The chapter's temporal organization exists precisely
because more follows: state decoding moments later, then trait inference across encounters, moving from
category-based toward target-based, individuating knowledge. Prejudice is a claim about impressions that stay
category-based; person perception includes the machinery by which they need not.

*The baseline stage precedes categorization and is not group-specific.* Before any sensory input, perceivers
assume intent, a mind, agency and complex emotions, often with an optimistic valence. That is applied to the
target as a human, not as a category member — and it is the foundation the later individuating processes build
on.

*Automatic does not mean fixed.* The chapter's whole later argument is that targets actively curate what they
present and that interaction is interdependent. An account on which the first-moment category determined the
impression could not accommodate self-presentation, behavioral confirmation, or the effect of interdependence
on perceived warmth.

**The better formulation.** Rather than "person perception is prejudice renamed," say: prejudice and
individuating impression formation are outcomes of the *same* apparatus operating under different conditions —
different amounts of time, attention, interdependence and motivation. That is a more useful claim because it
predicts *when* each outcome should occur, which is the question Module 11 will take up.
:::
::::

**Self-check:**

- Why does the chapter describe categories as carrying "messages of possibility"?
- What does it mean that perceived interdependence predicts perceived warmth, and where might that be exploitable?

## 3.4 Real-Time: Decoding Target States{{attrs[#blk-ijmj3h9snv0e]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Describe what perceivers extract in the moments after categorization.
- Explain why state inference feels immediately accessible.
- Distinguish state inference from trait inference.
:::

A beat after the category lands, the target is supplying **cues to how they are right now**, in words and
outside them — in a good mood or a bad one. Perceivers experience this inference as immediately accessible: it arrives from
facial expression, posture and movement without any sense of having reasoned to it. The chapter notes that
**mind-reading is deeper here** than in categorization; the perceiver is now inferring contents of the mind
rather than a social position.

The state–trait distinction is what organizes the rest of the chapter, and it is temporal and evidential
rather than merely conceptual. States are **momentary and rapidly sensed** from cues present right now. Traits
require **accumulation across occasions**. A single observation can license a state inference; the same
observation cannot license a trait inference, though — as the next section notes — people make immediate trait
inferences anyway.

**Self-check:**

- Why should an inference that feels immediate be treated with more suspicion rather than less?
- Give a cue that could support either a state or a trait reading, and say what would settle it.

## 3.5 Over Time: Traits, Consistency, Weight, and Context{{attrs[#blk-6r5ygupllce4]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Explain what distinguishes trait inference from earlier, faster processes.
- Describe the role of consistency across occasions.
- Locate trait inference on the category-based to target-based continuum.
:::

Across a series of encounters, what people arrive at is more often a **trait belonging to that particular person**, worked out deliberately from fragments of their conduct in context. The word *scraps* is doing
honest work: the evidence is fragmentary, and it accumulates over time rather than arriving whole.

The normative requirement is clear — a trait attribution **ought to rest on the same thing showing up repeatedly, on separate occasions**. The gap between that requirement and what perceivers actually do is where the
interesting findings live, since spontaneously categorizing what trait a behavior implies is an *earlier,
immediate* inference, made from a single observation. So the same perceiver may hold a trait belief arrived at
in one instant and a trait belief accumulated across months, with no phenomenological marker distinguishing
them.

**Consistency, weight and context** govern how observations combine — how much a given observation counts,
and how the situation in which it occurred modulates what it licenses. This is the far end of the chapter's
continuum: **individuating, target-based knowledge** as opposed to the category-based knowledge of the first
instant.

**Self-check:**

- What follows for a perceiver who cannot tell which of their trait beliefs was formed instantly and which accumulated?
- Why does context modulate the weight of an observation rather than simply adding to it?

## 3.6 Not a Lump: Active Targets and Interdependent Interaction{{attrs[#blk-ry0yjakd42lr]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Explain why the target cannot be modeled as a passive information source.
- Describe self-presentation goals and their downside risks.
- Explain the chapter's conclusion about interdependence, and the perceiver's opacity to themselves.
:::

Most of the review concerns the perceiver. But the target, in the chapter's phrase, is **not a lump**
passively shedding social information. Targets are steadily and reciprocally managing what they let out, moment by moment,
and their goals might not align with the perceiver's. The chapter invokes William James, who observed that we carry a distinct social self for every audience whose regard matters to us, presenting a different face to each.

**Self-presentation and impression management.** Targets accomplish this by controlling what they highlight.
People are motivated to share information about themselves, but that information can be highly filtered or
even deceptive — and the most powerful lever is simply **choosing what to say and what to withhold**. Five
self-presentation goals have been described — being liked, respected, admired, feared, helped — each with a way
of enacting it and a characteristic **downside risk**. Ingratiation, for instance, might involve agreeing with
the perceiver, but it must be done sincerely: the risk is appearing inauthentic, and mere flattery backfires.

**Interaction.** Perceiver and target are not distinct roles assigned to two people. In everyday interaction
**each player plays both roles simultaneously** — updating an impression while managing the one they present,
reading another's expressions while producing their own. And these are not merely simultaneous but
**interdependent**: one's emotions can be changed by another's, and the depth of one's self-disclosure shifts
to match what was just received.

Mere social presence matters in its own right: what the presence of another person sets in motion, real or merely believed, differs
fundamentally from those in private or with non-human or hypothetical actors, and social presence by default
makes people more self-conscious. Work on objective self-awareness used mirrors, cameras and audiences to make
people aware of being a target, and the usual result is a sense of falling short, measured against an ideal that becomes vivid precisely because
one is now viewing oneself from outside. The eyes of others act as a social mirror.

::: warning
**The chapter's own spoiler.** It concludes that person perception matters most where it
happens between **two people whose outcomes depend on each other** — which means most of the literature, built on
perceivers judging static stimuli, has been studying the less significant case. New neuroimaging and AI
methods are what make the naturalistic case tractable. This is a Module 2 point wearing different clothes: the
paradigm was chosen for feasibility, and the field's picture is shaped accordingly.
:::

**The perceiver's opacity to themselves.** The chapter's closing turn is that people are poorly equipped to
access their own minds. What survives is usually **the conclusion, not the evidence behind it**. Asked why one face appeals more than another, or why this option was taken over that one, people cannot say, and will offer a reasonable-sounding account that may have nothing to do with the cause. Sometimes they infer their own emotions
or preferences by observing the choices they watch themselves make — applying to themselves the same social
inference they would apply to another.

This is where **attribution** enters. People often begin a social inference by anchoring on a character
judgment — Leslie is cranky — and then correct away from it by taking the situation into account. But they are
often unaware that they fail to complete the correction, and the part of the situation they overlook most reliably is **their own contribution to it**. The out-of-awareness correction can also be curtailed when time or effort runs short.

::: info
For the classic treatment of attribution theory — the systematic account of how people infer causes, and the
research tradition built on it — read Kelley and Michela (1980), assigned for this module. This guide
deliberately does not summarize it: it is a separate reading with its own argument, and the seminar discussion
will be better if you come to it directly rather than through a précis.
:::

## 3.7 The Machinery: Automatic and Controlled Processing{{attrs[#blk-o7zohxt4b49h]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- State the three organizing principles of social cognition and what each claims.
- Distinguish will from intent, and locate goal-related automaticity between them.
- Distinguish the three kinds of priming, and explain why they carry different evidential weight.
:::

The anchor chapter organizes person perception by *time*. A complementary way in is to ask what kind of
cognitive system is doing the work. Social cognition — the thinking that lets us act in a social world, people
making sense of other people and of themselves — is usefully described by three principles. People deploy
**automatic and deliberate processes flexibly**, moving between them rather than using one; they attend
**pragmatically**, to cues that appear diagnostic rather than to everything available; and they **store what
looks useful**, representing information in memory for later use. Notice that all three are pragmatic claims,
which is the same stance §3.1 identified in the chapter's rejection of accuracy as the goal.

**Will and intent are not the same thing.** *Will* concerns goals and a desired end state. *Intent* concerns
choice, attention and effort — and it carries a presumption of alternatives: someone who could have been on
time and was not is judged to have been late *intentionally*. This distinction matters for §3.2, where
perceivers infer intent from a target having had a choice and having attended to one option over another.

Between the fully automatic and the fully controlled sits **goal-related automaticity**, and this is where
most social cognition actually happens. Motives operate along the whole spectrum, and sometimes automatic
processing wins over deliberate processing precisely because a motive favors it. A need to belong feeds
ingroup favoritism without deliberation. Thinking hard about someone you are attracted to does not yield
objective information about them, because the motive to affiliate is shaping the deliberation itself. Effort
does not guarantee accuracy.

**Three kinds of priming, three levels of confidence.**

| Kind | What it activates | Evidential status |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronic / habitual** | A concept made accessible by repeated activation, so that individuals differ in which concepts are chronically available to them | Well established; explains why the same prime affects different people differently |
| **Conscious** | Semantic content, attitudes or evaluations the person is aware of — group labels priming stereotypic associations; a question about what you like priming a favorable stance | Well established |
| **Subliminal** | Associations cued without awareness — semantic, affective, or behavioral | **Depends sharply on which** |

::: warning
**The subliminal category does not travel as a unit, and this is the point to carry into every reading.**
Semantic priming — word association and the like — is relatively uncontested. Affective priming is likewise
not much disputed. **Behavioral** priming is a different matter: it has produced a large number of findings
that failed to replicate. Treating "priming" as one phenomenon with one evidential status is precisely the
error Module 2 warned about when it discussed construct breadth. Ask which kind, and what the replication
record for *that* kind looks like.
:::

::: info
Ask yourself what your own habitual associations with a common concept are — trust, insecurity, something
harder to name. Chronic accessibility predicts that the same prime will not do the same work in you as in the
person beside you, which is a claim you can check against your own experience before you check it against a
literature.
:::

**Self-check:**

- Give a case where deliberate thought produces a *less* accurate impression than a snap judgment would.
- Why does the replication record differ so much across the three subliminal varieties?

## 3.8 Attention, Accessibility, and Social Memory{{attrs[#blk-0w8gv5ikn1ho]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Describe what captures attention in a social scene, and why salience is not a property of the stimulus alone.
- Explain the link from salience to accessibility to judgment.
- Describe how social information is organized in memory.
:::

**What gets attended to.** Faces do a great deal of work: babyish facial features, the direction of gaze, and
trait inferences drawn straight from appearance — happy-looking faces read as trustworthy, angry-looking ones
as untrustworthy. Such appearance-based inferences are consequential rather than idle; snap judgments of
competence from photographs have been shown to predict voting preferences, which should trouble anyone
inclined to treat first impressions as harmless.

**Salience** is what makes a stimulus stand out, and the sources are heterogeneous: visual novelty and color;
physical position, such as standing at the front rather than the back of a group; goal relevance, including
potential mates; emotional relevance; rarity relative to what is expected; and negative behavior, which
captures attention more readily than positive behavior of the same magnitude. Note that several of these are
relational — rarity and goal relevance depend on the perceiver and the context, not on the stimulus alone.

**Salience leads to accessibility**, and accessibility is what makes a concept available to shape the next
judgment. This is the same construct §3.7 approached through priming, arriving from the attention side: a
concept can be made accessible by a prime, by chronic habit, or by being salient in the scene, and the
downstream consequence is the same.

**How social information is stored.** Person information is held in **associative networks** linking persons,
traits and behaviors, in which activation makes future activation easier — an arrangement that is adaptive
precisely because recently used information is likely to be needed again. That is mainly **declarative**
memory. Alongside it sits **procedural** memory: learned sequences and routine judgments that run without
being retrieved as facts. Above both sit **top-down representations** — categories, concepts and expectations
— which social cognition research has emphasized more than bottom-up detail. These categories behave less like
definitions than like **fuzzy sets**: a collection of exemplars held together by family resemblance around a
prototype, rather than a list of necessary and sufficient conditions.

::: warning
The fuzzy-set structure is why the sentence "all ___ are ___" is so easy to complete and so hard to defend. A
category organized around a prototype produces confident generalizations that no member need actually
satisfy. Hold this until Module 11.
:::

**Self-check:**

- Name a case where salience is entirely relational — a property of the perceiver's goals rather than the stimulus.
- Why does a prototype-organized category generate stronger generalizations than a definitional one?

## 3.9 Attribution: Correspondence, Bias, and Covariation{{attrs[#blk-b94bzdgo0tpn]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Explain correspondent inference and what it asks of an observed action.
- Describe two systematic biases in causal attribution.
- State Kelley's three covariation criteria and what each rules out.
:::

Section 3.6 ended with the perceiver's failure to complete situational correction. This section gives the
attributional machinery that failure belongs to.

**Correspondent inference** asks whether an act points back to a matching intention behind it, and so to some
underlying attitude or trait that can fairly be attributed to the actor. The perceiver's task is to decide whether behavior is
diagnostic of the person or merely of the circumstances. Note how directly this depends on §3.2: the
inferential chain from choice to intent to mind is what makes correspondence seem available at all.

**Two biases distort the process.**

*Personal interest shapes the inference.* The same ambiguous act attracts different explanations depending on
what it costs the perceiver. If your manager fails to greet you, the inference reaches for something about
you and your standing. If an unfamiliar colleague does the same, the event barely registers as needing
explanation. The behavior is constant; the inferential effort is not.

*Dispositional causes are overweighted and situational ones underweighted.* This is the bias §3.6 traced to
the intentional default installed before any evidence arrives — and it is why the correction stage exists at
all.

**Kelley's covariation model** describes what an unbiased attribution would require: three kinds of
information, each ruling out a different alternative.

| Criterion | The question | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| **Consensus** | Do other people behave this way toward this target? | A person-level explanation, if consensus is high |
| **Distinctiveness** | Does this person behave this way toward other targets too? | A target-level explanation, if distinctiveness is low |
| **Consistency** | Does this person behave this way toward this target across occasions? | A one-off circumstantial explanation, if consistency is high |

::: info
The model's value in a seminar is not as a description of what perceivers do — they frequently do not collect
this information — but as a **standard against which to see what they omit**. Note also the link back to
§3.5: consistency across occasions is the same requirement trait inference was said to demand and routinely
fails to meet. Kelley's model and the chapter's trait-inference discussion are the same normative point made
twice.
:::

**Self-check:**

- Construct a case where high consensus and low distinctiveness point to different attributions.
- Which of the three criteria is hardest to collect in ordinary life, and what follows?

## 3.10 Measuring What Is Implicit{{attrs[#blk-vmvig3yk04bb]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Explain why implicit measures were developed and what problem they address.
- Describe the main families of implicit measure.
- Explain why priming effects vary so much across procedures, and what follows for interpretation.
:::

Explicit measures of stereotypes have an obvious weakness: people can decline to report what they would
rather not report, and may not have access to it in any case — the opacity §3.6 established. Implicit
measures were developed to get at automatic responses instead, and they rely on priming. Which means
everything in §3.7 about priming's heterogeneity applies to them.

**The main families** are the Implicit Association Test, sequential priming procedures, and the Go/No-Go
Association Task. Sequential priming was originally developed to assess semantic associations in memory: each
trial presents a prime and then a target, congruent or not — names carrying gender information followed by
occupations, for instance — and the measure is whether congruent pairs are responded to faster or more
accurately. The IAT works on related logic and records reaction time.

**Why results vary so much.** A meta-analytic look at this literature found that the size of a priming effect
depends heavily on the *procedure*, not only on the attitude being measured:

- Whether the pairing is **stimulus–stimulus** or **stimulus–response** matters, with stimulus–response arrangements producing larger effects. In an S–R design the participant must indicate whether prime and target correspond, which adds a second goal to a task already presented very quickly — a structure related to the Stroop task.
- **Task type** matters: stereotype-categorization and weapon-identification tasks produce larger effects than others. Compare a lexical decision task, whose advantage is the opposite — participants judge something (word or non-word) entirely unrelated to the stereotype of interest.
- **Stereotype dimension** matters: race produces larger priming effects than gender.
- The **interval between prime and target onset** matters: short or typical intervals produce larger effects than long ones.
- What did *not* matter much: whether the prime was verbal or non-verbal, and the specific stimulus content — category labels versus jobs versus traits.

::: warning
**The methodological conclusion is the one to carry forward.** Priming is not one thing, so "an implicit
measure showed X" is not an interpretable claim on its own. Which procedure, which pairing structure, which
interval? Each choice changes the effect's magnitude, and some of them plausibly change what is being
measured. This is Module 2's construct-validity problem in a live literature — and it is why the same section
warned that a meta-analysis pooling studies done in very different ways estimates nothing in particular.
:::

::: info
**A stereotyping mechanism worth naming**, since it connects this module to Module 11. Obvious categories —
race and gender read from a face, group membership, accent — capture attention; accessibility then guides
category formation in memory, and category activation is *more* likely under cognitive load, not less.
Motivational factors cut the other way: perspective-taking, motivation to be unbiased, and induced guilt have
each been shown to reduce spontaneous stereotyping. And valence rides along with the category: exposure to
faces of a social group can speed recognition of positively or negatively valenced words that are otherwise
irrelevant, because the category makes its associated valence accessible.
:::

**Self-check:**

- Two studies use "implicit measures" of the same attitude and disagree. What would you need to know before treating that as a contradiction?
- Why does category activation increase rather than decrease under cognitive load?

## Synthesis{{attrs[#blk-m85ps6ly82fs]}}

The thread of this module is that **person perception is built for use, not for truth** — and that this
explains its architecture. Perceivers start inferring before any evidence arrives because a working assumption
is more useful than a blank slate. They extract categories in the first instant because approach-or-avoid must
be decided before anything subtler. They experience state inferences as immediate because deliberation would
cost more than it returns. They form trait beliefs from scraps rather than waiting for the consistency the
normative account requires. And they retain their conclusions while discarding the evidence, because the
conclusion is what future action needs.

Read that way, the chapter's "failures" stop looking like failures — which is exactly the move Module 1's
rationality tension prepared you for. A biased outcome produced by a well-adapted process is what a system
optimized for pragmatics rather than accuracy should produce. The place where this genuinely costs something
is the last one: perceivers overlook their own hand in the situations they explain, and cannot detect
that they have. A process well tuned for acting on other people is poorly tuned for knowing what one has done
to them.

Two things to carry forward. **Methodologically**, the criterion problem in accuracy research is a live case
of Module 2's measurement concerns, and the chapter's admission that the field studied the less significant
case for reasons of tractability is a live case of Module 1's argument that method shapes substance.
**Substantively**, the warmth–competence space and the category-based to individuating continuum are the
machinery Module 11 will use for prejudice and stereotyping, and Module 4 takes up what happens when the
target of all this apparatus is oneself.
