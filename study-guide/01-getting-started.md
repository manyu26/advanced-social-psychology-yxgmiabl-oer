## Module Logic{{attrs[#blk-frg435tvx87r]}}

This module supplies the frame for everything that follows. It does three things in order: says what social
psychology studies, sketches how the field arrived at its present shape, and then works through four
arguments the field has never settled. The history is not decoration — it is what makes the tensions
concrete, because each one is visible in specific decisions, studies and disputes with dates attached.

## 1.1 What Social Psychology Studies{{attrs[#blk-wp22heweevr1]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- State the field's subject matter using the affect–behavior–cognition framing.
- Explain what "social presence" covers, and why the actual, imagined and implied cases all count.
- Describe the range of models of the person that social psychology has worked with.
:::

Before the arguments, the object. Social psychology examines three things about a person — what they **feel**
(affect), what they **do** (behavior), and what they **think** (cognition) — and asks how each is shaped by
the presence of other people and by the situations those people create. Its ambition is both **to explain**
and **to predict**: not merely to describe what happens but to say why, and to say what would happen under
conditions not yet observed.

The most-cited definition of the field, offered by Gordon Allport in the first edition of this Handbook in
1954, makes one move worth dwelling on. It defines the field's scope by the *presence of other human beings* —
and counts that presence as **actual, imagined, or implied**. Read the original sentence yourself (Allport,
1954, p. 5); the point to take from it is that the third and fourth words of that list do enormous work. A
person alone in a room, believing they might be observed, is inside social psychology's remit. So is a person
whose behavior is shaped by a group they will never meet. The field is not the study of interaction; it is the
study of what other minds do to a mind, including when they are not there.

::: info
**Models of the person.** Social psychology has never settled on one picture of the human being, and
Baumeister's inventory of the models it has used is a useful map of what the field has variously assumed
people fundamentally are. Among them: a creature run by **hot processes** (motivation) and **cold processes**
(cognition); an **information seeker** who explains events; a **consistency seeker** who resolves dissonance;
a **situational responder**; a **group member**; a **self-esteem maximizer**; an **impression manager**; a
**terror manager**; a **naturally selected and cultural animal**; a **benighted layperson** who does not know
their own mind; and a **maker of foolish mistakes** whose errors are what generate the field's most surprising
findings. Each of these is a research programme, and several map directly onto later modules in this course.
:::

Note already that this list is not neutral with respect to the arguments ahead. Whether the person is
fundamentally a situational responder or a consistency seeker is a position on the person–situation question;
whether they are a group member or a self-esteem maximizer is a position on the individual–collective
question; whether they are an information seeker or a foolish mistake maker is a position on rationality.

**Self-check:**

- Give an example where *implied* presence changes behavior with no one actually there.
- Which two of Baumeister's models are hardest to hold at the same time, and why?

## 1.2 A Field Defined by Its Arguments{{attrs[#blk-u1b0ewtuxk3i]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Explain the *tension* lens and the two criteria used to select the four tensions.
- Identify presentism and American centrism as historiographic pitfalls.
- Explain why a history organized around disagreement suits a graduate seminar.
:::

The sixth edition of *The Handbook of Social Psychology* is the fourth time in roughly seventy years that this
series has published a history of the field. That creates a problem for whoever writes the newest one: the
events are already well documented. Miller and Laurin's response is that a history earns its keep through its
**lens**, not its chronology — and each previous Handbook history chose a different one. Gordon Allport's
first-edition chapter traced the discipline's roots; Jones's focused on a formative fifty-year stretch;
Taylor's followed a single central concept, the social being; Ross, Lepper and Ward's took up the field's
enduring themes and foundational insights. (You will meet that last one directly in §1.8.)

The lens Miller and Laurin choose is **intellectual disagreement**. A *tension*, in their usage, is a pair of
opposing positions on an important issue where both sides attracted committed proponents and both generated
sustained empirical and theoretical work. Two criteria governed selection: social psychologists have taken
differing positions on the issue from the field's inception through to the present, and the movement of the
debate over time is instructive about how the field develops.

They also name two traps. **Presentism** narrates a debate so that its current state appears to be a final
resolution — a temptation they argue is unwarranted for a tension that has run a century or more.
**American centrism** treats one national tradition as the field itself; they note that earlier Handbook
histories have been fairly criticized on this count, and expect theirs to be as well.

::: info
For a seminar, the practical value of the tension lens is portability. An effect learned in isolation has
nowhere to attach. A tension is a question you can put to any paper in the field: which side is this author
on, and do they know they have taken a side?
:::

**Self-check:**

- Why might a history organized around unresolved disagreements be more useful to a new researcher than one organized around discoveries?
- What would it take for one of these four tensions to genuinely close?

## 1.3 A Short History, and How the Experiment Came to Dominate{{attrs[#blk-slacj1f68x81]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Place the field's development across five periods.
- Trace the three pushes that established experimentation as the central method.
- Explain how Allport's definition of social behavior and his methodological preference reinforced each other.
- Contrast Lewin's and Festinger's conceptions of what an experiment is for.
:::

### The five periods

Social psychology's questions are older than its discipline. Long before there was a field, religious and
philosophical traditions were addressing what would become its subject matter — free will, prosocial and
antisocial conduct, the self, motives, attribution and forgiveness in the Judeo-Christian scriptures;
motivation, the self and social influence in the Bhagavad Gita; individual responsibility and interdependence
in the sixth-century Rule of Benedict; and reciprocity codified as law in the Babylonian Codex Hammurabi
around 1760 BCE. Among the Greeks, Aristotle argued that humans are inherently social and that understanding
them requires understanding their social environment; Plato addressed group functions, the social contract,
obedience, conformity, social facilitation and social loafing; and the clash between Socratic reasoning and
Sophist rhetoric can be read as an early two-route model of persuasion.

| Period | What happened |
|---|---|
| **1850–1930 — Emergence** | Carlo Cattaneo coins *psicologia sociale* in 1864 for the psychology of associated minds. Gustav Lindner's 1871 textbook carries a chapter on the fundamentals of social psychology. Wundt produces the ten-volume *Völkerpsychologie* between 1900 and 1920, on mental products created by a community and inexplicable in terms of individual consciousness alone. Norman Triplett runs what is generally counted the first social-psychological laboratory experiment at Indiana in 1897, having children wind fishing reels alone and alongside others — though the experimental method does not become dominant for another half-century. Floyd Allport pushes controlled laboratory work and individualist explanation. William James proposes an early "thinking is for doing" view of the self varying with social context. The field enters the scholarly map: the *Journal of Abnormal Psychology* adds "and Social Psychology" to its title and appoints Floyd Allport co-editor. |
| **1930–1945 — Maturation and migration** | The subdiscipline is established. In America, individualist emphases: the frustration–aggression hypothesis (1939), later social learning theory, and a strong interest in the structure and function of attitudes — Gordon Allport's 1935 Handbook chapter, Newcomb's Bennington longitudinal study of attitude change, LaPiere's 1934 demonstration that attitudes and actions toward outgroup members diverge, Katz and Braly's 1933 study of ethnic stereotypes, Mead on internalized social experience and the self, Murray's personality theory. In Europe, the group-mind tradition, and the enormous shadow of Hitler. Kurt Lewin, who fled to the United States, formulates behavior as a function of person and environment, promotes action-oriented experiments — including wartime work using group pressure to shift American household meat consumption — and founds the Research Center for Group Dynamics at MIT, training a cohort that includes Festinger, Schachter, Deutsch, Cartwright, Thibaut and Kelley. |
| **1946–1969 — Flourishing** | Post-war demand for educators, mental-health professionals and science produces growth everywhere, and a golden age for the laboratory experiment: complex, clever, tightly controlled. Group dynamics in the Lewinian tradition — Festinger on informal social communication, Deutsch on cooperation and competition, Asch's conformity studies, Kelley's interdependence theory. Influence and persuasion — the risky shift, Milgram's obedience studies, the Yale communication programme's "who said what to whom." Social perception — Asch's warm/cold trait impressions, the "New Look" claim that motives shape what we see, Cronbach's critique of simple trait ratings. Cognitive dissonance, self-perception, reactance. And a long list besides: two-factor emotion, similarity and attraction, self-disclosure, physical attractiveness, social exchange, equity theory, bystander intervention. Institutionally: APA Division 8 forms in 1947 with Gordon Allport as first chair; *JPSP* and *JESP* both launch in 1965. |
| **1970–1990 — The ascent of social cognition** | The field is challenged as insufficiently scientific because social behavior varies with time and culture; as dominated by North American white males in its theorizing; and as simplistic in testing one variable at a time. Social cognition rises in response: Heider's common-sense psychology, Kelley's covariation model, Nisbett and Ross on inference and construal, Kahneman and Tversky on heuristics, work on automaticity, the elaboration likelihood model, the theory of reasoned action, models of categorization and person memory, dual-process models, and models separating automatic from controlled processes in stereotyping. Motivation develops in parallel — control theory, self-determination, self-discrepancy, terror management, and a family of self-evaluation motives. Research ethics codes develop; new journals appear. |
| **1990–present — Biology, culture, big data, and reproducibility** | Computing changes what research can do. The field grows more reliant on self-report and less on behavioral measurement, partly for ethical reasons — a shift away from the era of Milgram, Asch, and the bystander studies. Neuroscience arrives, including fMRI. Culture is taken seriously, and the field begins moving beyond WEIRD samples. Most recently, big data and the reproducibility crisis, with multi-laboratory and multi-country collaborations. That last strand is Module 2's subject. |

::: warning
Notice what this timeline shows that a list of findings would not. The experiment was invented for this field
in 1897 and did not take over until the 1950s. The individual/collective split was a decision taken in the
1920s, not a discovery. And the critiques that opened the 1970s — cultural variability, a narrow demographic
base, one-variable-at-a-time designs — are recognizably the critiques of the 2010s. Tensions, not progress.
:::

### The three pushes

It is easy to assume the experiment became social psychology's central method because it was obviously the
right tool. The history says otherwise. When the discipline was established, positivism had already installed
the experiment as science's principal vehicle — but **Wilhelm Wundt**, a founding figure in psychology,
explicitly *excluded* social psychological principles from its remit while accepting it for cognition and
perception. No other social science was using experimental methods at the time.

**Floyd Allport's push (1924) — restricting the definition.** Allport narrowed social behavior to *individual
behavior directed toward social objects*: reactions to the language, gestures and movements of other people,
as distinct from reactions to non-social objects. If the forces acting on behavior are large, amorphous
sociocultural phenomena — a country's language, religion, or political system — clean experimental design is
hopeless. If instead they are small, decomposable, isolable elements such as the mere presence or absence of
other people, experiments become feasible. As Danziger observed, Allport's methodological innovation was not
even conceivable without a radical break from how the other social sciences conceived of "the social."
**Definition and method were settled together, not independently.** One casualty was the field's shared
enterprise with sociology, which until then had treated social psychology as joint territory. Compare Allport's
scope with the definition Ross had offered in 1908, which located the field in uniformities arising from the
interplay of minds — a formulation much closer to sociology's.

::: warning
**The consequence worth noticing.** Allport's restricted scope excluded phenomena that could not be easily
decomposed — culture among them. Those are precisely the phenomena that would have kept the case for
particularism visible. The narrowing did not merely permit experiments; it quietly removed the strongest
standing objection to assuming universality.
:::

**Kurt Lewin's push — principles and subjective perception.** Where Allport's definition excluded group
psychology outright, Lewin's metatheory made groups studiable both in the laboratory and across a wide range
of real settings. Deutsch identified three field-shaping components: the joint role of **person and
environment**, the distinction between **genotypic processes** and **phenotypic observations**, and the
treatment of situations as **systems under tension**.

**Leon Festinger's push — entrenching the experiment.** Festinger took over supervision of Lewin's graduate
cohort after Lewin died in 1947 at fifty-seven. In a late-1970s survey asking members of the Society for
Experimental Social Psychology to name the three greatest contributors to the field, 70% named Lewin and 79%
named Festinger, with no one else above 50%. Rather than grand laws, Festinger favored **mini-theories** —
social comparison and cognitive dissonance among them — and defined theoretical claims as statements about
causal relations between variables, which elevated the experiment precisely because experiments establish
causal relations.

:::: cols
::: col
**Lewin's experiment**
Capture the essential *genotypic* structure of a phenomenon — democracy, say — so it can be transposed to
other situations. Manipulating many variables at once is acceptable if the underlying structure is revealed.
Aspired to universal, mathematically precise theory on the model of physics.
:::
::: col
**Festinger's experiment**
Manipulate single variables systematically and under control, in keeping with non-social experimental
psychology. Prioritized *empirical* purity over *theoretical* purity. Aspired to meticulous tests of
relations among conceptual variables.
:::
::::

Lewin's aspiration failed; Festinger's succeeded. Contemporary social psychology is recognizably Festinger's
discipline, and much of what the field now argues about follows from that.

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A colleague claims that social psychology adopted the experiment because "it's simply how science works —
there was never really a choice." Using the chapter's account, construct the strongest case against this
claim. What would your colleague have to explain away?
:::
::: tab Solution
The claim cannot survive four facts.

**A founder of psychology explicitly disagreed.** Wundt endorsed experimentation for cognition and perception
while excluding social psychological principles from its remit. If the experiment's applicability to social
life were self-evident, it would not have been denied by the person best positioned to extend it. Nor was the
field an outlier: no social science used experimental methods then.

**The first experiment sat idle for fifty years.** Triplett ran his reel-winding study in 1897. If the method
were obviously correct, its adoption would not have waited until the 1950s. Something other than
demonstrated fitness governed the timing.

**The method required redefining the subject matter first.** Allport's restriction of social behavior to
individual responses to social objects was what made clean designs feasible — Danziger's point is that the
innovation was not conceivable without a break from existing conceptions of the social. A method adopted
because it works does not require the phenomenon to be redefined to fit it.

**Two influential figures wanted incompatible things from the experiment, and one lost.** Lewin sought
experiments capturing genotypic structure, tolerating confounds. Festinger sought controlled single-variable
manipulations and empirical purity. Both were committed experimentalists — so their disagreement cannot be
explained by "how science works." Festinger prevailed for reasons of influence and fit with mainstream
experimental psychology, and the field inherited his version.

**What the colleague must explain away:** why the "obvious" method was rejected by Wundt, lay unused for two
generations after its first demonstration, required a narrowed definition of the field to become workable, and
took a specific contested form. The honest conclusion is that the experiment's dominance was *achieved*, not
discovered — which is not an argument that it was the wrong choice.
:::
::::

## 1.4 Tension 1 — Particularism Versus Universalism{{attrs[#blk-aby5np6xwtc1]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Distinguish the broad form of the tension from the narrow form.
- Define external validity and relate it to claims about human universals.
- Summarize the main critiques of the experiment's generalizability.
:::

Both forms of this tension turn on **generalizability**: can a finding or theory apply beyond the specific
parameters, samples and context in which it was demonstrated?

The **broad form** asks whether some social psychological processes are permanent, unvarying equipment of our
species — the same wherever and whenever you look. Miller and Laurin note, with some understatement, that an
outsider would be surprised to learn this remains unsettled. It received little attention in the field's early
decades.

The **narrow form** is methodological and dominated instead: what can the experimental method reveal about the
world beyond its own specific operationalizations of independent and dependent variables? To the extent that
it can, we say the experiment has **external validity**.

The two are distinct but entwined. Historically the entwining ran deeper than logic requires: many early
defenders of the experiment argued in ways that presumed human psychological processes *could* be universal,
so the forces elevating the method also reinforced universalist assumptions.

| Critique | The charge | The counter-position |
|---|---|---|
| Artificiality | Laboratory situations are artificial and unrepresentative of real settings | The Lewinian reply: the goal is abstract universal theory, not generalization to the messy world |
| Humanity and agency | Experiments disregard participants as agents, treating them as reactive | Bears on what the participant is doing in the situation, not merely on sampling |
| Paradigm narrowness | Too few operational paradigms to capture the fullness of the concepts they stand for | Compounded when those paradigms were not themselves empirically validated |
| Anti-positivism | Universal truths may not exist to be found in the first place | Challenges the goal rather than the method |

::: info
**Where this tension surfaced historically.** Gergen's challenge in the early 1970s was that social psychology
could not be a science on the model of physics because social behavior varies with historical time and
culture — the anti-positivist line, arriving at the field's high-water mark of experimental confidence. The
demographic critique of the same period, that theory construction was dominated by North American white
males, is the ancestor of the WEIRD critique that Module 2 develops. Culture was taken seriously only from the
1990s. The chapter's point is that none of this is settled now either.
:::

::: warning
These are not four versions of one complaint. Artificiality is about the setting; the agency critique is about
the participant's role; paradigm narrowness is about construct coverage; anti-positivism rejects the target. A
rebuttal that answers one leaves the others standing.
:::

**Self-check:**

- Give an example where an experiment has high external validity in the narrow sense but says nothing about human universals.
- Gergen's critique is fifty years old. What in Module 2's material is its direct descendant?

## 1.5 Tension 2 — Social Context Versus the Individual{{attrs[#blk-6bfl13f4hbnp]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Explain the charge that social psychology became individualistic, and evaluate it.
- Describe the European tradition's case that groups are causal forces.
- Assess whether recent interest in broader contexts resolves the tension.
:::

Allport's position was uncompromising: any psychology of groups, he held, reduces without remainder to the
psychology of the individuals composing it. Miller and Laurin trace the long consequences. Steiner's
well-known critique asked what had happened to the study of group-level phenomena; attitudes became a case
where the argument for group-level influences was pressed. The chapter's sharper observation is that the field
came to embrace a "groupy" approach *in theory* while rarely adopting it *in practice* — a gap between stated
commitment and research design worth watching for in the papers you read this semester.

The alternative was available from the start and was set aside rather than refuted. Cattaneo's 1864 coinage
named the psychology of *associated* minds. Wundt's *Völkerpsychologie* was explicitly about mental products
created by a community and therefore not explicable through individual consciousness alone. Ross's 1908
definition located the field in what arises from the interplay of minds. Every one of these is a group-level
programme, and all predate Allport's restriction.

The strongest counterweight came from Europe. **Tajfel** developed intergroup behavior into what functioned as
a meta-theory. **Moscovici** contributed both minority influence — showing that influence is not only the many
acting on the few — and social representations, which locate shared understanding at the level of the
collective rather than the individual head. Against this stands the practical difficulty of **studying real
groups**, and the chapter notes the irony of solutions to excessive individualism that were themselves
individualistic in form.

::: info
**The Lewinian counter-current, and what happened to it.** Lewin's Research Center for Group Dynamics trained
much of the next generation, and the group-dynamics tradition produced Festinger on informal communication,
Deutsch on cooperation and competition, Asch on conformity, Kelley on interdependence, and later work on the
risky shift, groupthink, deindividuation and jury deliberation. Yet most of this remained individualist in its
unit of explanation — what a person does when a group is present. Basic research on groups has since declined
even as applied group research flourished, particularly in culture and relationships. That pattern is the
theory/practice gap in one sentence.
:::

More recently, psychological social psychology has become more interested in broader social contexts after a
long era of proximal focus, driven partly by methodological innovation and partly by a move from studying
processes to studying phenomena. Whether this resolves the tension or relocates it is a fair question, and
the chapter's own framing — that experiments have not become less individualistic — suggests caution.

**Self-check:**

- What is the difference between a theory that mentions groups and a research design that treats the group as the unit of explanation?
- Cattaneo, Wundt and Ross all offered group-level definitions before 1910. What does it mean that the field went the other way?

## 1.6 Tension 3 — Rationality Versus Irrationality{{attrs[#blk-hmniyyc6a30h]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Trace the movement from crowd irrationality to the rationality of underlying processes.
- Distinguish irrational outcomes from irrational processes.
- Evaluate the claim that apparent bias is an artifact of experimental design.
:::

The field's early position located irrationality in crowds, cast as agents of decivilization. Rationalist
counterarguments followed, challenging both the claimed outcomes and the claimed processes, and the topic's
interest gradually dwindled. Conformity research carried the thread forward under headings of suggestion,
prestige, and the voice of the herd.

The decisive move in later work was to **separate outcomes from processes**. Research demonstrating apparently
motivated bias appeared to show irrationality, but emphasis shifted toward the rationality of the *processes*
producing those outcomes. The computer metaphor made motivated reasoning harder to state cleanly, and two
reconciliations emerged: motivation as **director** with cognition as **executor**, and **dual-process
models** distributing the work across qualitatively different modes.

Newer arguments push further, questioning whether the outcomes are irrational at all: bias versus accuracy in
perception; the claim that bias is an **artifact of experiments designed to elicit it**; and the argument that
the relevant criterion is **survival, not accuracy**.

::: info
**The concrete cases worth having to hand.** The social-cognition programme of the 1970s and 80s supplied the
evidence this tension runs on — Heider's common-sense psychology, Kelley's covariation model with its
consensus, distinctiveness and consistency criteria, Nisbett and Ross on inference, the principle of construal
holding that situations are interpreted rather than objectively registered, and Kahneman and Tversky on
heuristics. Alongside these sit demonstrations that perception is unreliable in specific, replicable ways:
misattribution of arousal in the suspension-bridge study, the weapon effect, and the mere exposure effect,
which appears even for stimuli people cannot recognize — Chinese characters shown too briefly to identify,
and, in one striking animal demonstration, tones played to chicks before hatching and preferred afterward.
Each is a case where an outcome looks irrational; each invites the question of whether the process producing
it is.
:::

::: warning
The artifact argument deserves care rather than reflexive agreement. That a paradigm was built to reveal an
effect does not by itself show the effect is confined to the paradigm — but it does place the burden on the
researcher to show it appears where nobody arranged for it. This is the same demand the paradigm-narrowness
critique made in §1.4, arriving from a different direction.
:::

**Self-check:**

- Construct an example of a biased outcome produced by a well-adapted process.
- Does the mere exposure effect show irrationality of outcome, of process, or neither?

## 1.7 Tension 4 — The Dispassionate Scientist Versus the Value-Driven Advocate{{attrs[#blk-fg4zoy132vup]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Distinguish the three forms of the values debate.
- Explain why value-free science has been questioned.
- Take and defend a position on policy advocacy by psychologists.
:::

Efforts to make social psychology relevant take recognizable forms. **Action research** works collaboratively
on a specific community's concrete problem — Lewin's wartime work on household food choices is the founding
example, and it is worth noticing that the field's most influential methodologist was also its most committed
applied researcher. **Concept-based intervention research** applies a single concept across many domains. The
chapter separates relevance from newsworthiness, a distinction worth keeping.

The debate about values comes in three distinct forms, and conflating them produces bad arguments:

1. **How researchers' values shape which questions get asked and which answers get found.** A recent iteration
   focuses on political orientation as a broad marker of values.
2. **Whether advocacy is faithful to the strength of the underlying science.** Researchers may overstate the
   consistency of policy-relevant findings out of a desire to promote policies they consider morally right.
3. **Whether it is appropriate at all** for social psychologists or their organizations to advocate for
   specific policies.

On whether science can be value-free: **Weber** held that social science is necessarily *value-relevant* — all
knowledge of cultural reality is knowledge from particular points of view — while insisting science should
remain free of value *judgment*. More recent philosophy of science questions whether even that is achievable.
**McMullin** argued that the very standards by which we judge a theory are determined by what we value,
illustrating with the Bohr–Einstein disagreement over quantum theory: Bohr weighted predictive accuracy, which
the theory offered; Einstein weighted consistency with existing knowledge, with which it clashed. Both are
legitimate marks of a good theory, and choosing between them requires values. **Rooney** makes a parallel
point about parsimony, which reflects a cultural preference for simplicity rather than a fact about the world.

Within social psychology, **Reicher and Haslam** argued that classic studies such as the Stanford Prison
Experiment and Milgram's obedience paradigm contributed to the creation of ethics review boards, which
necessarily make value-based decisions — weighing societal benefit against participant harm. Those decisions
then determine which research is permissible and therefore which discoveries can be made.

::: info
**A worked historical case.** School desegregation research, and the jigsaw classroom developed in its wake,
is social psychology intervening directly in a contested public question. It is simultaneously a scientific
programme, a policy instrument, and a moral commitment — which makes it a good object for asking all three of
the questions above at once. Note also the chapter's observation that ethics codes developed alongside this
work, and that the constraints they impose are part of why the field moved from behavioral measures toward
self-report from the 1990s onward. A value judgment about participant welfare reshaped the field's evidence
base. Whether that was the right trade is a live question, and Module 2 supplies the other half of it.
:::

::: info
The chapter's conclusion is carefully limited, and worth stating precisely: the point is not that ethics
boards should not exist, nor that evidence fails to constrain conclusions. It is that we deceive ourselves if
we believe values play no part in shaping what scientific knowledge looks like.
:::

## 1.8 The Other Lens: Themes and Pillars{{attrs[#blk-fnycfiqzbx8l]}}

::: success
**Learning Objectives**

- Describe the three central themes and four pillars offered by the previous Handbook edition's history.
- Compare that lens with the four-tensions lens.
- Say what each lens makes visible that the other does not.
:::

Miller and Laurin chose their lens partly *because* the fifth edition's history, by Ross, Lepper and Ward, had
already taken up the field's enduring themes and foundational insights. That earlier account is still worth
knowing, and holding both is the most useful thing this module can give you.

**Three central themes.**

- **Norms and situational influence** — what people do in groups that they would never do alone, and how group
  norms, status and roles operate. Social exchange, risk tolerance in groups, social facilitation, groupthink,
  deindividuation, jury deliberation. Later extended to cultural influence and intergroup relations, with
  independent versus interdependent self-construal, modern racism and implicit bias, and stereotype threat.
- **Subjectivity and construal** — against the behaviorist position that mentalistic processes are unimportant
  and everyone confronts the same objective reality, social psychologists insist on the actor's interpretation.
  Situations are never objective; the prisoner's dilemma changes with how it is framed; and the field's
  attributional programme exists because causal inference is itself a subjective act.
- **Non-obviousness** — the field's characteristic interest in findings contrary to expectation. Why
  interventions fail; how motives shape perception and perception shapes motives; the drinking-norms study,
  proximity and attraction, the smoke-filled-room bystander study, foot-in-the-door compliance. The
  justification is that non-obvious results reveal that the situation's subjective meaning for the actor has
  changed.

**Four pillars.**

- **Naïve realism** — believing we see the world objectively and that those who disagree must be uninformed,
  irrational or biased.
- **Lay dispositionism** — over-attributing behavior to stable personal attributes. The fundamental attribution
  error, with false confession and eyewitness research as consequential cases. Even social psychologists
  underestimate situational power, and a self-serving asymmetry lets us see the situation's grip on ourselves
  but not on others. Experience fails to correct this because people choose situations that then constrain
  their behavior, so person and situation are confounded in everything we observe.
- **Overconfidence** — driven by needs to see oneself as consistent, rational and moral, and to maintain
  self-esteem. Self-serving bias, downward comparison, positive illusion.
- **Confirmation bias** — including self-fulfilling prophecy, where expectations produce the outcomes that
  appear to confirm them.

:::: cols
::: col
**What the tensions lens shows**
The field as *unresolved argument*. Positions have proponents on both sides; the movement of a debate is
itself evidence; and any paper can be located by which side it takes. Good for reading critically.
:::
::: col
**What the themes-and-pillars lens shows**
The field as *accumulated insight*. These are things social psychology has learned about people and largely
agrees on. Good for knowing what the discipline actually contributes.
:::
::::

::: warning
**Do not treat these as rivals to be adjudicated.** Notice instead that the pillars *are* positions inside the
tensions. Lay dispositionism is a finding about where people locate explanation — which is the person/situation
question of Tension 2 turned into an object of study. Naïve realism and confirmation bias are findings about
rationality, Tension 3. That two Handbook editions organize the same material so differently is itself the
best evidence for Miller and Laurin's claim that a history is made by its lens.
:::

**Self-check:**

- Take one of the four pillars and say which tension it sits inside, and on which side.
- If you were writing the seventh edition's history, what lens would you choose, and what would it cost you?

## Synthesis{{attrs[#blk-52aqrpw3dlsl]}}

One thread runs through all four tensions: **social psychology's methods and its claims about human nature
were built together, and neither can be evaluated without the other.** Allport's narrowed definition made the
experiment feasible and, in the same stroke, removed culture from view — which is why the universalism
question could remain dormant for decades. The individual/collective divide persists partly because the
designs the field inherited suit individuals better than groups, and partly because the group-level programmes
of Cattaneo, Wundt and Ross were set aside rather than defeated. The rationality debate turned on whether
paradigms built to elicit bias were measuring the mind or measuring themselves. Even the values tension has a
methodological face: ethics review determines which studies can exist, and its constraints helped push the
field from behavior toward self-report.

The history makes one more thing visible. Every critique that opened the 1970s — that findings are
culture-bound and time-bound, that the field's theorists were demographically narrow, that designs test one
variable at a time — is recognizably alive today. A student who reads Module 2's replication and
generalizability material as a recent emergency will misread it. It is the latest turn of an argument that is
as old as the discipline.

For the rest of this seminar, that gives you a standing set of questions to put to any reading: what had to be
assumed about the social world for this method to be usable here; whose position on generalizability does this
design take; is the explanation located in the person because the evidence put it there or because the method
did; and what does the author want this finding to change in the world? Module 2 takes the first of these up
directly.
