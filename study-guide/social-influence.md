## 9.1 Influence and explanation

::: success
**Objective 9.1a:** Distinguish informational influence, normative influence, and public compliance.
:::

People may align with others because those others provide useful information, because agreement carries social benefits, or because disagreement carries costs. **Informational influence** concerns using others as evidence. **Normative influence** concerns social expectations and their consequences. Both can operate in one situation.

Public agreement does not necessarily reveal private acceptance. A junior researcher may repeat a senior colleague’s recommendation while privately doubting it. Conversely, learning from a colleague can change private judgment without any demand to agree. Compare behavior across audiences and time rather than infer a motive from conformity alone.

Classic conformity and obedience studies helped establish these questions, but a historical demonstration is not a universal estimate of susceptibility. Task ambiguity, authority arrangements, participant expectations, and ethical limitations shape interpretation. The useful lesson is to design a comparison that identifies when social information or social consequences matter.

**Information**

- Others provide evidence
- Ambiguity can increase reliance
- Private judgment may change

**Social consequences**

- Approval or disapproval
- Audience and status
- Public agreement may exceed private acceptance

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A student endorses a group answer publicly but gives the original answer anonymously. What does this pattern suggest?
:::
::: tab Solution
It is consistent with public compliance shaped by social consequences. It does not uniquely establish that process: differences in timing, instructions, or perceived confidentiality could matter. Compare equivalent judgments under randomized audience conditions and assess what participants believe others expect.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A group becomes more accurate after discussion. Is that evidence against conformity?

## 9.2 Value in a social setting

::: success
**Objective 9.2a:** Explain the valuation account without reducing influence to money or one brain region.
:::

The chapter organizes behavior change around the subjective value of available options. Value can include immediate enjoyment, anticipated outcomes, identity relevance, effort, status, and connection. A social message may change one of these inputs and thereby change which action seems worthwhile.

Personal and social rewards overlap. Belonging can be personally rewarding, and an apparently private choice can communicate identity to others. The framework does not require people to calculate a numeric utility consciously. It proposes that diverse inputs contribute to how appealing actions become.

Neural findings provide evidence about systems involved in valuation, but observing activity does not identify a complete causal pathway. A signal can predict later behavior while remaining compatible with several processes. Connect the neural measure to a clearly defined task and behavioral outcome, and avoid describing any region as an exclusive influence center.

**Inputs to value**

- Expected benefits and effort
- Identity relevance
- Connection, approval, and status

**Evidence about valuation**

- Behavioral choices
- Subjective appraisals
- Neural signals with bounded interpretation

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A message makes volunteering seem more consistent with a student’s identity. What outcome would test the proposed behavior-change pathway?
:::
::: tab Solution
Measure perceived identity relevance, the relative appeal of volunteering, and actual participation using an appropriate comparison. An increase in stated identity fit alone establishes neither increased value nor behavior. Even a statistical indirect effect requires assumptions before it can support a causal mediation claim.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Brain activity during a message predicts later participation. What additional claim would require different evidence?

## 9.3 Relevance, identity, and autonomy

::: success
**Objective 9.3a:** Evaluate personalization and autonomy support as conditional influence strategies.
:::

A message can become relevant when it connects an action to a recipient’s goals, identity, or circumstances. Personalization is more than inserting a name. It requires information about what the person values and which barriers they face. The same appeal may be meaningful to one recipient and irrelevant to another.

Identity can support action, but messages can also threaten valued self-understandings. Perceived pressure may elicit reactance or defensive processing. Autonomy-supportive communication offers meaningful reasons and acknowledges choice while still conveying clear information. It should not be equated with weak recommendations or a lack of structure.

The earlier lecture’s principles of liking, reciprocity, consistency, authority, scarcity, and social proof can be analyzed as possible changes in information or value. Their labels do not substitute for a mechanism, and none guarantees compliance. A research commentary should ask why the strategy should work for this audience, in this setting, on this behavior.

**Potential benefits**

- Personally relevant consequences
- Connection with valued identity
- Meaningful reasons and feasible choices

**Potential limits**

- Identity threat
- Perceived pressure
- A label is not a mechanism

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
Two students receive the same message about contributing to a shared dataset. One values collaboration; the other worries about credit. Why might their responses differ?
:::
::: tab Solution
The message can change different components of value for each recipient. Collaboration may be an immediate identity benefit for one, while unresolved credit concerns remain a cost for the other. Measure those appraisals and address the actual barrier rather than assume that greater message repetition solves the problem.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** An authority endorsement increases agreement. List a mechanism that concerns information and one that concerns social consequences.

## 9.4 From intention to action

::: success
**Objective 9.4a:** Compare action planning, skills, friction, and defaults as behavior-change targets.
:::

An attractive action can remain difficult to carry out. Action planning connects intentions to a time, place, and response. Skill development increases the ability to act. Reducing friction changes the practical effort required. These mechanisms can produce similar increases in uptake through different routes.

Defaults can influence choice because remaining with an assigned option is easier, because it appears recommended, or because people do not actively reconsider it. A default effect therefore does not automatically show a changed preference. Ask whether people understood the options and whether the arrangement supports the intended outcome.

Design interventions around the bottleneck. If a student cannot access a resource, a stronger persuasive message may not help. If access is easy but the activity feels irrelevant, simplifying a form may have limited impact. Measure the proposed barrier before and after the intervention, and assess maintenance rather than only initial uptake.

**Different barriers**

- Unclear next action
- Missing skill or access
- Time and administrative effort

**Different interventions**

- Implementation plan
- Training and resources
- Reduced friction or revised default

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A campus program shortens a registration form, and sign-ups rise. Must participants now value the program more?
:::
::: tab Solution
No. Lower effort can increase participation without changing evaluation. A revised form might also signal accessibility or institutional support. Measure value and perceived effort separately, and track attendance or completion. Registration is an intermediate behavior rather than the final educational outcome.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** In a hypothetical randomized trial, 45 of 100 invited students sign up under a simplified process versus 30 of 100 under the standard process. Interpret the difference.

## 9.5 Norms and reference groups

::: success
**Objective 9.5a:** Distinguish descriptive and injunctive norms and evaluate unintended norm messages.
:::

**Descriptive norms** concern what people do. **Injunctive norms** concern what people approve or expect. They can diverge: a group may disapprove of lateness while frequently arriving late. A message about prevalence should not be treated as a message about approval.

A norm’s relevance depends on the reference group and context. Students may attend more to peers they identify with than to a broad average they see as unrelated. Salience also matters: the norm made prominent at the moment of choice may differ from a norm people know in general.

Warnings can unintentionally normalize the behavior they oppose by emphasizing how common it is. That possibility does not justify inventing favorable statistics. Use accurate evidence, make the desired expectation clear, and test the interpretation recipients actually draw. Changing perceived norms is an intermediate outcome; later behavior and subgroup responses must also be assessed.

**Descriptive norms**

- What people do
- Which reference group?
- How prevalent is it?

**Injunctive norms**

- What people approve
- Which expectations are salient?
- What consequences are anticipated?

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A poster says that many students submit work late while urging punctuality. Why might the message have competing effects?
:::
::: tab Solution
It communicates disapproval but also suggests that lateness is common. Recipients may infer that the behavior is normal despite the request. Compare messages with accurate, clearly specified norm information and assess perceived prevalence and approval separately before interpreting behavior.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A department reports 80% approval of data sharing. Can that number be used as evidence that 80% actually share data?

## 9.6 Groups and distributed information

::: success
**Objective 9.6a:** Evaluate group discussion without treating consensus as accuracy.
:::

Influence operates within groups whose members differ in information, roles, and status. Agreement can reflect successful integration of evidence, deference to a prominent member, or a failure to hear less visible information. Consensus alone does not distinguish these possibilities.

The earlier course lectures raised groupthink, polarization, facilitation, and loafing. These labels concern different outcomes. A more extreme group judgment is not the same observation as reduced individual effort, and neither establishes inaccurate reasoning by itself. Module 8 develops audience effects and contribution; the present task concerns how social information shapes a collective judgment.

For a seminar team, record initial judgments, information available to each member, speaking opportunities, and final decisions. Compare the group’s decision with a defensible criterion. An intervention that equalizes turns might increase participation without improving evidence use, so assess both. Treat structural changes as testable designs rather than guaranteed cures.

In the earlier group lecture, **group polarization** describes discussion followed by movement toward a more extreme version of a group’s initial tendency. **Groupthink** concerns concurrence seeking that weakens scrutiny or discourages dissent. They are different claims: extremity need not imply poor deliberation, and poor deliberation need not produce a more extreme judgment. A committee example should assess initial preferences, information exchange, dissent, and decision quality separately (Li, n.d.).

**Observe the discussion**

- Initial judgments
- Who holds which information?
- Status and speaking opportunities

**Evaluate the result**

- Agreement or extremity
- Accuracy against a criterion
- Use of previously unshared evidence

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A committee agrees unanimously after its chair speaks first. Which observation would help distinguish good integration from deference?
:::
::: tab Solution
Compare initial private judgments and examine whether members introduce and use independent evidence. Vary speaking order or collect initial judgments before discussion. Unanimity is compatible with accurate convergence and social pressure; it cannot identify either on its own.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** A structured discussion increases the number of speakers but leaves decision quality unchanged. What should an evaluation report?

## 9.7 Networks and message sharing

::: success
**Objective 9.7a:** Distinguish message exposure, sharing, and downstream influence.
:::

People often encounter a campaign through acquaintances rather than directly from its original source. Sharing is itself a decision: a person may expect the message to help someone, signal identity, create connection, or earn approval. What motivates a sender need not match what persuades the recipient.

A message’s reach, repetition, and credibility depend on the network through which it travels. Similar responses among connected people can arise from influence, similar prior preferences, or shared environments. A network association alone does not show contagion. Design a comparison that separates exposure from the tendency to select similar contacts.

Measure the stages separately. A message can spread widely yet have little effect, or reach a small relevant audience and change behavior. Counting shares is useful for reach but insufficient for persuasion. Also inspect whether the message changes as it is retold, connecting social influence to Module 7’s cultural transmission.

**Sender and network**

- Expected usefulness to others
- Identity and social rewards
- Who can reach whom?

**Recipient and outcome**

- Actual exposure
- Interpretation and credibility
- Later behavior

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A message receives many shares but no detectable increase in program uptake. What does this establish?
:::
::: tab Solution
It documents sharing and no detected uptake change in the evaluated setting. The message may not reach eligible recipients, may be misinterpreted, or may fail to address barriers. Those are hypotheses to test. High reach should not be relabeled as a behavioral effect.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Friends adopt similar study tools. What design could provide stronger evidence of peer influence than a one-time network survey?

## 9.8 Narratives and evaluation

::: success
**Objective 9.8a:** Design a behavior-change evaluation that separates mechanisms and outcomes.
:::

Narratives can invite identification with a character, sustained attention, and a new perspective. Those experiences may make information meaningful or reduce counterarguing, but a compelling story is not automatically representative evidence. Distinguish the persuasive experience from the factual support for its claims.

A behavior-change evaluation should name the target behavior, population, comparison, time horizon, and relevant costs. Message recall, attitude, intention, uptake, and maintenance occupy different places in the causal chain. A program can succeed at one stage and fail at another.

The final commentary can combine Modules 5 and 8 with this module: ask whether the intervention changes an evaluation, changes a goal, or changes the ease of acting. Include possible spillovers and unequal access. A useful proposal explains what result would support the chosen mechanism and what result would require revising it.

**Narrative processes**

- Attention and identification
- Perspective and social connection
- Possible changes in counterarguing

**Evaluation plan**

- Target behavior and timeframe
- Comparison and mechanism measures
- Maintenance, access, and spillovers

### Worked example

:::: tabs
::: tab Problem
A campaign improves story recall and intentions, but the follow-up behavior measure is missing for half the sample. What conclusion is justified?
:::
::: tab Solution
Report the observed recall and intention outcomes and the limits of the behavioral evidence. Examine attrition patterns and use appropriate sensitivity analyses; missingness may depend on engagement or outcomes. The campaign has not established a robust behavior-change effect merely because upstream measures improved.
:::
::::

**Self-check:** Propose a design comparing a narrative with a factual message without confounding story format and information quality.

## Synthesis and seminar preparation

Social influence can change what people know, value, expect, and find feasible. Move from an observed change to a proposed mechanism, then test how the effect travels through groups and networks.

For the final commentary, identify a claim from a chosen article, specify the construct and measure, and develop an alternative that the design cannot yet exclude. Propose one feasible comparison and explain how its possible outcomes would change the interpretation.
