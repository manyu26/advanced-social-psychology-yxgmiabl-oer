# 1.1 What social psychology studies

<!-- slide -->
## Three things about a person
{{mermaid
graph LR
  A["<b>A</b>ffect<br/>what they feel"] --> D["shaped by<br/><b>social presence</b><br/>and situations"]
  B["<b>B</b>ehavior<br/>what they do"] --> D
  C["<b>C</b>ognition<br/>what they think"] --> D
  D --> E["To <b>explain</b><br/>and to <b>predict</b>"]
}}
Not merely describing what happens — saying why, and what would happen under conditions not yet observed.

<!-- slide main-side -->
## The move in Allport's 1954 definition
<!-- @main -->
The field is defined by the presence of other human beings — and that presence counts as

==**actual, imagined, or implied**==

- Alone in a room, believing you might be observed → in scope
- Shaped by a group you will never meet → in scope
<!-- @side -->
::: info
Social psychology is not the study of interaction.

It is the study of **what other minds do to a mind** — including when they are not there.

*Read the original sentence: Allport, 1954, p. 5.*
:::

<!-- slide -->
## What kind of creature is a person?
Social psychology has never settled on one model. Baumeister's inventory:

:::: cols
::: col
- **Hot** processes (motivation) / **cold** processes (cognition)
- **Information seeker** — explains events
- **Consistency seeker** — resolves dissonance
- **Situational responder**
- **Group member**
- **Self-esteem maximizer**
:::
::: col
- **Impression manager**
- **Terror manager**
- **Cultural animal**, naturally selected
- **Benighted layperson** — doesn't know own mind
- **Foolish mistake maker** — source of the field's surprising findings
:::
::::

::: warning
This list is **not neutral**. Situational responder vs consistency seeker is a position on person/situation. Information seeker vs mistake maker is a position on rationality. The models *are* the arguments.
:::

# 1.2 A field defined by its arguments

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Two ways to write a history
<!-- @left -->
{{sp[red] **Progress narrative**}}
- A sequence of discoveries
- Each improves on the last
- The present looks like an endpoint
- New findings have nowhere to attach
<!-- @right -->
{{sp[blue] **Tension narrative**}}
- Opposing positions, both serious
- Both generated real research
- Neither side conceded
- Any paper can be *located* in it

<!-- slide -->
## What makes a "tension"
{{sp[info] Definition}} **Tension** — opposing positions on an important issue that attracted proponents *and* adversaries, and galvanized sustained empirical and theoretical attention.

1. The field has taken **different positions from its inception to the present**
2. The **ebb and flow** of the debate is itself instructive

::: warning
Two pitfalls named: **presentism** (today's position narrated as the resolution) and **American centrism** (one tradition mistaken for the field).
:::

# 1.3 A short history, and the rise of the experiment

<!-- slide -->
## Older than the discipline
:::: cols
::: col
**Religious and philosophical roots**
- Judeo-Christian scriptures — free will, prosocial and antisocial conduct, self, motives, attribution, forgiveness
- Bhagavad Gita — motivation, self, social influence
- Rule of Benedict (6th c.) — individual responsibility, interdependence
- Codex Hammurabi (~1760 BCE) — reciprocity as law
:::
::: col
**The Greeks**
- **Aristotle** — humans inherently social; must understand the social environment
- **Plato** — group function, social contract, obedience, conformity, social facilitation, social loafing
- **Socrates vs the Sophists** — arguably the first two-route model of persuasion
:::
::::

<!-- slide -->
## Five periods
| Period | The turn |
|---|---|
| **1850–1930** Emergence | Cattaneo coins *psicologia sociale* (1864) · Wundt's *Völkerpsychologie* · **Triplett's first experiment, 1897** · Floyd Allport's individualist turn |
| **1930–1945** Maturation & migration | Attitudes research · frustration–aggression · **Lewin flees to the US**, founds the RCGD at MIT |
| **1946–1969** Flourishing | Golden age of the lab experiment · Asch, Milgram, Festinger, Deutsch, Kelley · Division 8 (1947) · **JPSP (1965)** |
| **1970–1990** Ascent of social cognition | Gergen's and McGuire's critiques · Heider, Kelley, Nisbett & Ross, Kahneman & Tversky · dual-process models |
| **1990–present** Biology, culture, big data | fMRI · beyond WEIRD · shift to self-report · **reproducibility crisis** → Module 2 |

<!-- slide -->
## What the timeline shows that a list of findings would not
::: danger
- The first experiment ran in **1897**. The method did not take over until the **1950s**.
- The individual/collective split was a **decision** taken in the 1920s, not a discovery.
- The critiques that opened the 1970s — culture-bound findings, a demographically narrow field, one-variable designs — are ==recognizably the critiques of the 2010s==.
:::
**Tensions, not progress.**

<!-- slide -->
## It was not obvious
::: warning
**Wundt** endorsed the experiment for cognition and perception — and explicitly **excluded** social psychological principles from its remit.
:::
- No other social science used experiments at the time
- Triplett's 1897 study sat essentially idle for **fifty years**
- So experimental social psychology had to be **made possible**, not simply adopted

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Push 1 — Floyd Allport (1924)
<!-- @main -->
Restricted social behavior to **individual behavior directed toward social objects**.

- Large sociocultural forces → ==hard to experiment on==
- Small, isolable elements → ==clean designs become feasible==
- Definition and method were settled **together**

Compare **Ross (1908)**: the field is about uniformities arising from the *interplay of minds* — far closer to sociology.
<!-- @side -->
::: danger
**The casualty:** culture — the phenomenon that would have kept particularism visible.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Push 2 — Kurt Lewin
{{mermaid
graph TD
  L["Lewin's metatheory"] --> A["Person <b>and</b> environment"]
  L --> B["Genotypic process beneath<br/>phenotypic observation"]
  L --> C["Situations as<br/>systems under tension"]
  L --> D["RCGD at MIT — trained Festinger,<br/>Schachter, Deutsch, Thibaut, Kelley"]
}}
Against Allport, for whom any group psychology reduced without remainder to individual psychology.

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Push 3 — Leon Festinger
<!-- @main -->
- Took over Lewin's graduate cohort after Lewin's death in 1947
- Favored **mini-theories** — social comparison, cognitive dissonance
- Defined theory as **causal relations between variables** → elevates the experiment
<!-- @side -->
::: info
Late-1970s SESP survey, three greatest contributors:

**79%** Festinger
**70%** Lewin
No one else above 50%
:::

<!-- slide 2col -->
## What is an experiment *for*?
<!-- @left -->
{{sp[blue] **Lewin**}}
- Capture the **genotypic structure** of a phenomenon
- Transposable to other situations
- Confounds tolerable if structure is revealed
- Aspired to physics-like universal theory
- {{sp[red] **Failed**}}
<!-- @right -->
{{sp[green] **Festinger**}}
- Manipulate **single variables** under control
- **Empirical** purity over theoretical purity
- Meticulous tests of conceptual relations
- Aspired to rigor like non-social psychology
- {{sp[success] **Succeeded**}}

<!-- slide step -->
## Example — was the experiment simply "how science works"?
**Q.** A colleague says the field adopted the experiment because that is just how science is done. What four facts must they explain away?

1. **Wundt excluded social psychology** from the experimental remit
2. **The first experiment sat idle for fifty years** — 1897 to the 1950s
3. **The subject matter had to be redefined first** — Allport's restriction made clean designs conceivable
4. **Two committed experimentalists wanted incompatible things** — Lewin and Festinger disagreed

::: success
**Takeaway:** the experiment's dominance was *achieved*, not discovered — which is not to say it was wrong.
:::

<!-- slide template=section -->
# 1.4 Tension 1 — Particularism vs universalism

<!-- slide 2col -->
## One question, two scopes
<!-- @left -->
**Broad form**
Are some social psychological processes **permanent, unvarying equipment** of our species?

- Largely dormant in the early decades
- Still unsettled
<!-- @right -->
**Narrow form**
What can an experiment reveal **beyond its own operationalizations**?

- {{sp[info] External validity}}
- Dominated the field's attention instead

<!-- slide -->
## Four distinct critiques — not four versions of one
| Critique | The charge |
|---|---|
| **Artificiality** | Lab situations are artificial and unrepresentative |
| **Humanity and agency** | Experiments disregard the participant as an agent |
| **Paradigm narrowness** | Too few paradigms, often not themselves validated |
| **Anti-positivism** | Universal truths may not exist to be found |

::: warning
A rebuttal that answers one leaves the other three standing.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Where this surfaced historically
- **Gergen (1973)** — social psychology cannot be a science on the model of physics, because social behavior varies with **historical time and culture**
- Same period: the field's theorists are **demographically narrow** — the ancestor of the WEIRD critique
- **McGuire (1973)** — designs are simplistic, one variable at a time
- Culture taken seriously only from the **1990s**

::: danger
Fifty years old. Module 2's material is its direct descendant — not a recent emergency.
:::

# 1.5 Tension 2 — Social context vs the individual

<!-- slide main-side -->
## The road not taken
<!-- @main -->
The group-level alternative was **available from the start and set aside, not refuted**:

- **Cattaneo (1864)** — the psychology of *associated* minds
- **Wundt** — mental products of a community, inexplicable through individual consciousness
- **Ross (1908)** — what arises from the interplay of minds

All predate Allport's 1924 restriction.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**The European chorus**

**Tajfel** — intergroup behavior as a meta-theory

**Moscovici** — minority influence; social representations
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Groupy in theory, individualist in practice
Lewin's RCGD trained the next generation, and group dynamics flourished — Festinger on informal communication, Deutsch on cooperation, Asch on conformity, Kelley on interdependence, later the risky shift, groupthink, deindividuation, jury deliberation.

::: warning
Yet most of it remained **individualist in its unit of explanation** — what a person does when a group is present. Basic group research has since declined even as applied group research flourished.
:::
==That pattern is the theory/practice gap in one sentence.==

# 1.6 Tension 3 — Rationality vs irrationality

<!-- slide -->
## The move that reframed the debate
{{mermaid
graph LR
  A["Crowds as irrational,<br/>agents of decivilization"] --> B["Rationalist<br/>counterarguments"]
  B --> C["Separate <b>outcomes</b><br/>from <b>processes</b>"]
  C --> D["Biased outcome,<br/>rational process"]
  D --> E["Motivational director,<br/>cognitive executor"]
  D --> F["Dual-process models"]
}}

<!-- slide 2col -->
## The evidence this tension runs on
<!-- @left -->
**The social-cognition programme**
- **Heider** — common-sense psychology
- **Kelley** — covariation: consensus, distinctiveness, consistency
- **Nisbett & Ross** — inference, and the principle of **construal**
- **Kahneman & Tversky** — heuristics
<!-- @right -->
**Perception failing in replicable ways**
- Misattribution of arousal — the **suspension bridge**
- The **weapon effect**
- **Mere exposure** — even for unrecognizable stimuli: briefly-shown Chinese characters, and tones played to chicks *before hatching*

<!-- slide step -->
## Example — is the bias real?
**Q.** A paradigm built specifically to elicit a bias reliably elicits it. What follows?

1. **Not** that the bias is fake — a designed probe can still detect something real
2. **Not** that the bias is general — the paradigm arranged the conditions
3. What follows is a **burden of proof**: show it appears where nobody arranged for it
4. Note this is the ==paradigm-narrowness critique== from 1.4, arriving from another direction

::: success
**Three newer arguments:** bias vs accuracy · bias as artifact of design · survival, not accuracy, is the criterion
:::

# 1.7 Tension 4 — Dispassionate scientist vs value-driven advocate

<!-- slide -->
## Three debates people keep conflating
1. How researchers' **values shape the questions** asked and answers found
2. Whether **advocacy is faithful** to the strength of the science
3. Whether psychologists should **advocate at all**

::: warning
These have different answers. An argument that settles one settles none of the others.
:::

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Can science be value-free?
<!-- @left -->
**Weber**
- Social science is necessarily **value-relevant**
- All knowledge of cultural reality is from a point of view
- But it *should* be free of value **judgment**
<!-- @right -->
**McMullin**
- The standards for judging a theory are themselves value-laden
- Bohr weighted **predictive accuracy**
- Einstein weighted **consistency** with existing knowledge
- Choosing between them requires values

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Where it bites
<!-- @main -->
**Reicher and Haslam** — the Stanford Prison Experiment and Milgram's obedience paradigm contributed to the creation of **ethics review boards**.

Those boards weigh **societal benefit** against **participant harm** — a value judgment that determines which research is permissible, and therefore which discoveries can be made.
<!-- @side -->
::: danger
**Follow the consequence.**

Ethics constraints are part of why the field moved from behavioral measures toward **self-report** from the 1990s.

A value judgment reshaped the field's **evidence base**.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Two worked cases
:::: cols
::: col
**Lewin's action research**
Wartime work using group pressure to shift household food choices. The field's most influential methodologist was also its most committed applied researcher.
:::
::: col
**Desegregation and the jigsaw classroom**
Simultaneously a scientific programme, a policy instrument, and a moral commitment — so all three value debates apply at once.
:::
::::

# 1.8 The other lens — themes and pillars

<!-- slide -->
## Why there is a second lens
Miller and Laurin chose tensions **because** the previous edition's history — Ross, Lepper and Ward (2010) — had already taken up the field's enduring themes and foundational insights.

::: info
Two Handbook editions organize the same material completely differently. That is itself the best evidence for the claim that **a history is made by its lens**.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Three central themes
{{mermaid
graph TD
  A["<b>Norms &amp; situational influence</b><br/>what people do in groups<br/>that they never do alone"] --> D["Social psychology's<br/>accumulated insight"]
  B["<b>Subjectivity &amp; construal</b><br/>situations are interpreted,<br/>never objective"] --> D
  C["<b>Non-obviousness</b><br/>findings contrary<br/>to expectation"] --> D
}}
Norms → groupthink, deindividuation, social facilitation, later culture and stereotype threat. Construal → the prisoner's dilemma changes with framing. Non-obviousness → drinking norms, proximity and attraction, the smoke-filled room, foot-in-the-door.

<!-- slide -->
## Four pillars
| Pillar | What it names |
|---|---|
| **Naïve realism** | We see the world objectively; those who disagree must be uninformed, irrational or biased |
| **Lay dispositionism** | Over-attributing to stable traits — the fundamental attribution error, false confession, eyewitness error |
| **Overconfidence** | Needs to seem consistent, rational and moral; self-serving bias, downward comparison, positive illusion |
| **Confirmation bias** | Including self-fulfilling prophecy — expectations producing their own confirmation |

<!-- slide -->
## Why experience does not correct lay dispositionism
{{mermaid
graph LR
  A["People <b>choose</b><br/>their situations"] --> B["Situations then <b>guide and<br/>constrain</b> their behavior"]
  B --> C["Person and situation are<br/><b>confounded</b> in everything observed"]
  C --> D["The trait explanation<br/>always seems to fit"]
}}
::: warning
Even social psychologists underestimate situational power — and a **self-serving asymmetry** lets us see the situation's grip on ourselves but not on others.
:::

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Two lenses, one field
<!-- @left -->
{{sp[blue] **Tensions**}}
The field as **unresolved argument**.

Positions have serious proponents on both sides; the movement of a debate is evidence; any paper can be located.

*Good for reading critically.*
<!-- @right -->
{{sp[green] **Themes and pillars**}}
The field as **accumulated insight**.

Things social psychology has learned about people and largely agrees on.

*Good for knowing what the discipline contributes.*

::: danger
**Not rivals.** The pillars *are* positions inside the tensions — lay dispositionism is the person/situation question turned into an object of study; naïve realism and confirmation bias are findings about rationality.
:::
