# 12.1 Map the interdependent situation

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Map the interdependent situation
<!-- @left -->
**Map the situation**
- Available actions
- Jointly determined outcomes
- Information and uncertainty
<!-- @right -->
**Interpretation limits**
- Disagreement need not be fixed-sum
- Good intentions do not remove incentives
- A model is not a report of thought

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Why check understanding of a payoff table?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Choices may reflect misunderstood options or differently valued outcomes.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Choices may reflect misunderstood options or differently valued outcomes. A model-based interpretation assumes the relevant incentives are understood. Comprehension checks assess that assumption without proving a particular motive.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

Mutual cooperation gives each person 3 points; unilateral defection gives the defector 5 and cooperator 0; mutual defection gives each 1. Explain this hypothetical dilemma.
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

Defection yields 5 rather than 3 if the partner cooperates, and 1 rather than 0 if the partner defects. Yet mutual cooperation gives each 3 rather than 1. Individual incentives conflict with a mutually better outcome under these payoffs.

# 12.2 Prosocial outcomes and inferred motives

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Prosocial outcomes and inferred motives
<!-- @left -->
**Keep constructs distinct**
- Intent to benefit another
- Other’s welfare as an ultimate goal
- Equality and reciprocity
<!-- @right -->
**Discriminating conditions**
- Public versus private conduct
- Future interaction versus one encounter
- Equal resources versus unequal need

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Does an equal split prove indifference to need?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

No. The actor may lack need information or consider equality appropriate to the task.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
No. The actor may lack need information or consider equality appropriate to the task. Provide clear information and compare conditions before attributing a preference.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A volunteer publicly helps and receives praise. Is this purely altruistic?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

The act can benefit others while concern, enjoyment, reputation, and reciprocity coexist. Compare conditions that alter incentives. Public praise does not establish that concern is absent, and the helpful act does not establish a pure motive.

<!-- slide -->
## Prosocial outcomes and inferred motives: key distinctions
- Prosocial: intent to benefit, even if unsuccessful.
- Altruism: another’s welfare is an ultimate goal.
- Intent, motive, and observed benefit are distinct (Li, n.d.-b).

# 12.3 The boundaries of cooperation

<!-- slide 2col -->
## The boundaries of cooperation
<!-- @left -->
**Levels**
- Within a dyad
- Within a group
- Across group boundaries
<!-- @right -->
**Boundary questions**
- Who benefits?
- Who bears costs?
- What changes under representation?

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
How could representation increase conflict despite good intentions toward constituents?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Representatives may prioritize group outcomes and anticipate criticism for concessions.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Representatives may prioritize group outcomes and anticipate criticism for concessions. Compare self-representing and group-representing decisions and measure responsibility and audience expectations.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A team shares information internally while blocking another team’s access to shared equipment. Is it cooperative?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

It cooperates internally but imposes costs across the boundary. Specify the level and beneficiaries. Evaluate internal coordination and effects on other users rather than giving one global label.

# 12.4 Communication, trust, and considerate choices

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Communication, trust, and considerate choices
<!-- @left -->
**Possible supports**
- Clarify priorities
- Make credible commitments
- Preserve another’s options
<!-- @right -->
**Distinctions**
- Trust versus trustworthiness
- Talk versus changed incentives
- Consideration versus unlimited sacrifice

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
More discussion produces no improvement. Does this disprove all benefits of communication?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

No. Examine information exchanged, credibility, incentives, and distrust.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
No. Examine information exchanged, credibility, incentives, and distrust. The result concerns the tested procedure and conditions rather than every communication method.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 11, 24, 25, 26.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

Three blue notebooks and one green notebook remain. How can the first chooser preserve options?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

Choosing blue leaves both colors available to the next person. Choosing green removes that option. This illustrates choice structure, not a definitive moral diagnosis: preferences, awareness, and information still matter.

<!-- slide -->
## Communication, trust, and considerate choices: key distinctions
- Noise: intended and received outcomes diverge.
- Strict retaliation can amplify accidental slights.
- Clarification and generosity can interrupt escalation.

# 12.5 Conflict, aggression, and escalation

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Conflict, aggression, and escalation
<!-- @left -->
**Conceptual boundaries**
- Conflict: incompatible interests
- Aggression: intended, unwanted harm
- Harm: an outcome
<!-- @right -->
**Process questions**
- What preceded the response?
- Which interpretation guided action?
- Could the situation change?

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Is demanding immediate forgiveness a sufficient strategy?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

No. It bypasses acknowledgment, safety, incentives, and repair.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
No. It bypasses acknowledgment, safety, incentives, and repair. Assess changed conduct and voluntary participation. Reconciliation is not the same as suppressing concerns or resuming dependence.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 27, 28, 29, 30.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

Collaborators dispute authorship and start withholding information. What should a process account examine?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

Map decisions, expectations, allocation rules, and consequences. Determine whether harm was intended rather than assuming it from disagreement. Consider procedures that clarify contributions and reduce retaliation incentives.

<!-- slide -->
## Conflict, aggression, and escalation: key distinctions
- Aggression involves intended harm unwanted by its target.
- Conflict can occur without aggression.
- Changed incentives and repair matter alongside feelings (Li, n.d.-a).

# 12.6 Negotiation and third-party involvement

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Negotiation and third-party involvement
<!-- @left -->
**Control questions**
- Who organizes the process?
- Who determines the result?
- What participation is possible?
<!-- @right -->
**Evaluation**
- Voice and information
- Implementation
- Fairness and substantive effects

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
A neutral party imposes a decision. What should be assessed beyond completion?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Assess meaningful voice, consideration of evidence, substantive outcomes, and implementation.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Assess meaningful voice, consideration of evidence, substantive outcomes, and implementation. An imposed result does not show that both parties freely preferred it.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 30, 31, 32.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A facilitator structures discussion, but either party can reject a proposal. Which distinction matters?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

The facilitator influences process while parties retain decision control, resembling mediation. Specify voluntariness and actual powers because procedures can differ from the simple category.

# 12.7 Reputation, sanctions, and institutions

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Reputation, sanctions, and institutions
<!-- @left -->
**Supports**
- Repeated interaction
- Credible reputation
- Rules and sanctions
<!-- @right -->
**Tradeoffs**
- Enforcement costs
- Errors and unequal burdens
- Trust and repair

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Does cooperation under monitoring establish changed prosocial motivation?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

No. Monitoring changes consequences and reputational stakes.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
No. Monitoring changes consequences and reputational stakes. Assess behavior when conditions change and measure proposed motives separately. An effective institution need not create a personality change.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 33, 34, 35.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

Cleanup fines improve compliance, but disputed penalties rise and junior staff pay most fines. What belongs in the evaluation?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

Report compliance alongside accuracy, burden distribution, and trust. Investigate schedules and unclear duties. Consider clearer responsibilities and an appeal procedure. Compliance alone does not establish fairness or sustainability.

# 12.8 Integrate a multilevel explanation

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Integrate a multilevel explanation
<!-- @left -->
**Explanatory levels**
- Situation and incentives
- Interpretation and interaction
- Networks and institutions
<!-- @right -->
**Contribution**
- Bounded claim
- Competing explanation
- Result that changes the conclusion

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
What distinguishes integration from listing twelve module topics?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Integration specifies relationships among selected constructs and evidence for each link.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Integration specifies relationships among selected constructs and evidence for each link. A list has no explanatory pathway. Identify a comparison that separates the pathway from an alternative.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 16, 17, 18, 33, 34, 35.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A cooperative scheduling app fails. Give two explanations with different implications.
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

It may preserve incompatible incentives, or users may misunderstand or distrust its records. Assess incentives and comprehension separately. Vary rule clarity and incentive alignment while measuring use and equitable access.
