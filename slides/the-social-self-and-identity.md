<!-- @right -->

<!-- @main -->

<!-- @side -->

<!-- @left -->
**Clarity**

How definite and coherent do my self-beliefs feel?
<!-- @mid -->
**Continuity**

How connected do my past, present, and future selves feel?
<!-- @right -->
**Accuracy**

How well do those beliefs correspond to a defensible criterion?

<!-- slide step -->

## Relational Self


- Traditional social psychology viewed the self as a bounded, autonomous container. While theories like Symbolic Interactionism (Mead) suggested others influence our self-concept, Aron et al. (1991) pushed this further: **Do we literally process close others as part of ourselves?**

- The **Inclusion of Other in the Self** (IOS) model. The core premise is that as relationships grow close, the psychological boundaries between self and other blur. Resources, perspectives, and characteristics of the other are shared and mapped onto the cognitive structure of the self.

- [Study 1 & 2] Participants were presented with trait adjectives on a computer screen and had to press "Me" or "Not Me" as quickly as possible.
	- IV: They evaluated traits for themselves, for a close other (e.g., best friend or romantic partner), and for an acquaintance. Critically, researchers manipulated or analyzed cases where the participant’s traits **overlapped** with the close other vs. where they **diverged** (traits true for the self, but not the friend, or vice versa).
	- DV: Reaction time (latency) in ms. 
	- Hypothesis: If the self and close other share cognitive space, deciding "Me" when a trait describes only the friend (or vice versa) should incur cognitive interference, leading to longer reaction times, compared to an acquaintance.


_Aron, A., Aron, E. N., Tudor, M., & Nelson, G. (1991). Close relationships as including other in the self. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 60(2), 241–253._<!-- slide -->
## Relational Self

:::info
Participants were significantly slower to reject traits that were true of their close other but not true of themselves, compared to traits true of an acquaintance.
:::

This demonstrated a **cognitive confusion effect**: the psychological boundary of "Self" expanded to automatically encompass attributes of the close other.

:::warning
If the self includes the other, what happens structurally to the self-concept during a painful breakup or bereavement?
:::<!-- slide -->
## Relational Self - Measurement validity?

:::info
Does a reaction-time delay truly indicate structural integration of the self, or is it merely semantic priming / associative network overlap? How would modern social neuroscientists test this differently? 
:::

- fMRI studies: the Medial Prefrontal Cortex (tied to self-referential processing) activates similarly when thinking about the self and close others like mothers or romantic partners.
<!-- slide -->
## Relational self vs. self selection? Alt. explanations?

:::info
How do we know close others become part of the self, versus the alternative explanation: that we selectively form close relationships with people who are already remarkably similar to us?
:::

- As Aron and colleagues continued researching, they realized the IOS model was describing a symptom of a much broader, fundamental human motivation. They zoomed out to build **Self-Expansion Theory**: 
	- Humans have a primary, foundational motivation to enhance their potential efficacy and expand the self over time. e.g., acquiring new resources, perspectives, identities, and forming relationships, because bringing another person into your life is the most efficient way to rapidly expand your own capabilities and worldviews.<!-- slide -->
## Relational self vs. self selection?

:::info
How do we know close others become part of the self, versus the alternative explanation: that we selectively form close relationships with people who are already remarkably similar to us?
:::

- Intervention 1: The "Fast Friends" Procedure: Can we artificially induce structural self-expansion and replicate the psychological closeness of a long-term relationship with a total stranger in less than an hour?
	- Dyadic Assignment: Unacquainted pairs of undergraduate students were brought into the lab and randomly assigned to one of two conditions:
	- IV: fast friends condition (36 structured questions broken into 3 progessviely deeper tiers) vs. small-talk condition (superficial, low-disclosure interactions) over 45 minutes
	- Tier 1 (Surface) > Tier 2 (Vulnerability/values) > Tier 3 (Deep intimacy)
    - Fast friend condition - Higher subjective closeness inventory (SCI) score and SCI (even higher than those reported for close friends/romantic partner)

:::warning
Implication: Self-concept boundaries are porous and reactive. Intimacy is not merely a byproduct of time spent together; it is an active cognitive function of reciprocal self-disclosure that forces a rapid recalibration of where "Self" ends and "Other" begins.
:::

_Aron, A., Melinat, E., Aron, E. N., Vallone, R. D., & Bator, R. J. (1997). The experimental generation of interpersonal closeness: A procedure and some preliminary findings. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 23(4), 363–377._<!-- slide -->
## Relational self vs. self selection?

:::info
Beyond conversation, what happens when couples physically engage in novel, challenging, or high-arousal tasks together? Can behaviorally expanding the self reverse relationship boredom and enhance satisfaction?
:::
- Intervention 2


_Aron, A., Norman, C. C., Aron, E. N., McKenna, C., & Heyman, R. E. (2000). Couples' shared participation in novel and arousing activities and experienced relationship quality. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 273–284._<!-- slide 2col -->

<!-- @main -->
- Definitions differ across research traditions.

- The SAFE account proposes a role for environmental fit.
- Felt authenticity does not establish perfect self-knowledge.
<!-- @side -->

## Example: Why does Maya feel more at ease here?
**A community research group feels more authentic than a competitive meeting.**

# 4.3 Processing
## Feedback does not arrive as a random sample

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Selection begins before interpretation
<!-- @left -->
**Before feedback**
- Choose activities and audiences
- Signal a desired impression
- Invite particular kinds of responses
<!-- @right -->
**After feedback**
- Attend to some details
- Interpret ambiguity
- Recall and weight evidence selectively

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Effortful thinking need not correct a preferred view
<!-- @main -->
- Intuitive and deliberative processing both contribute.
- Self-schemas organize accessible evidence.
- Detailed reasoning can defend a desired conclusion.
- A self-serving explanation can contain true facts.
<!-- @side -->
::: warning
**The test**

Does the explanation weight the available evidence in proportion to its diagnostic value?
:::

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Threat-based recall is a conditional account
<!-- @left -->
**Mnemic neglect**

The review describes poorer recall for threatening information about central self-aspects under particular conditions.
<!-- @right -->
**Do not infer the mechanism from valence alone**
- Match feedback length and clarity.
- Check immediate comprehension.
- Distinguish central from peripheral feedback.

<!-- slide step -->
## Example: Praise remembered, criticism forgotten
**Does the pattern establish self-enhancement?**
- Rule out differences in exposure and comprehensibility.
- Vary personal centrality separately from valence.
- Compare immediate processing with delayed recall.
- The selective pattern can support an account without proving a universal rule.

# 4.4 Accuracy
## State the criterion before declaring an error

<!-- slide -->
## Ranking and calibration answer different questions
![Actual scores of 50, 65, and 80 paired with estimates of 60, 75, and 90.](../assets/module4-accuracy.svg =640x)

**Data summary:** These hypothetical scores preserve order perfectly; every estimate is 10 points too high.

<!-- slide step -->
## Worked example: Both conclusions are true
**Actual:** 50, 65, 80 · **Estimated:** 60, 75, 90
- Signed errors: +10, +10, +10
- Mean signed error: +10 points
- Pearson correlation: 1
- Correct ordering does not imply unbiased values.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Who has access to the relevant information?
<!-- @left -->
**Possible self advantage**
- Private experiences
- Thoughts and intentions
- Events across many settings
<!-- @right -->
**Possible observer advantage**
- Visible behavior the actor misses
- Evaluative qualities
- Behavioral patterns without distracting intentions

<!-- slide 3col -->
## Three ratings separate the perspectives
<!-- @left -->
**Self-view**

How Maya evaluates herself.
<!-- @mid -->
**Metaperception**

How Maya expects classmates to evaluate her.
<!-- @right -->
**Social evaluation**

How classmates actually evaluate Maya.

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Meta-insight goes beyond projected self-views
<!-- @main -->
- A raw correlation can reflect shared self-views.
- Measure all three perspectives separately.
- Ask whether metaperceptions add predictive information.
- Neural evidence also needs a precise inference standard.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Neural caution**

Medial prefrontal involvement in a self-task does not identify an exclusive location of the self.
:::

# 4.5 Self-esteem
## Specify the dimension that changes

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Scope and timescale are independent distinctions
<!-- @left -->
**Scope**
- Global evaluation of worth
- Domain-specific evaluation
- Example: academic self-evaluation
<!-- @right -->
**Timescale**
- Usual, relatively enduring evaluation
- Current state report
- A domain-specific belief can be stable.

<!-- slide 3col -->
## The average is only one description
<!-- @left -->
**Level**

How favorable are the evaluations overall?
<!-- @mid -->
**Stability**

How much do evaluations fluctuate across occasions?
<!-- @right -->
**Contingency**

Which outcomes make worth feel conditional?

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Measurement choices carry different limitations
<!-- @left -->
**Direct reports**
- Useful reliability and validity are possible.
- Presentation concerns may affect answers.
- The construct may not be fully accessible.
<!-- @right -->
**Indirect tasks**
- Concealed purpose does not guarantee validity.
- Relative scores may miss ambivalence.
- Test convergence and incremental prediction.

<!-- slide step -->
## Example: Same average, different daily experience
**Two students have identical monthly means.**
- Compare within-person variation.
- Record relevant events and their domains.
- Measure whether worth depends on those outcomes.
- Event-linked variation supports a contingency account more than the mean alone.

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Longitudinal prediction still needs a causal argument
<!-- @main -->
- People can maintain relative standing while means change with age.
- A population trajectory is not every individual's trajectory.
- Earlier self-esteem can predict later outcomes.
- Anticipation and unmeasured common causes remain alternatives.
<!-- @side -->
::: warning
A brief increase in state self-esteem does not establish the effects of enduring high trait self-esteem.
:::

# 4.6 Functions
## What might self-esteem monitor or support?

<!-- slide 3col -->
## Three proposed functional accounts
<!-- @left -->
**Terror management**

Valued standing in a cultural worldview helps manage mortality concerns.
<!-- @mid -->
**Sociometer**

Self-esteem responds to relational value and acceptance.
<!-- @right -->
**Hierometer**

Self-esteem tracks status relevant to assertive action.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Separate status from inclusion
<!-- @left -->
**Respected, but not welcomed**

A group recognizes someone's skill but avoids including them socially.
<!-- @right -->
**Welcomed, without high rank**

A new member is valued socially while having little influence.

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evaluate predictions alongside the evidence limits
<!-- @main -->
- Status and inclusion can both contribute to self-esteem.
- Their independent effects help distinguish mechanisms.
- Terror-management findings include replication concerns.
- One result cannot adjudicate an entire theoretical framework.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Module 2 returns**

Inspect operationalization, comparison conditions, uncertainty, and generalizability.
:::

<!-- slide 3col -->
## Three constructs, three different questions
<!-- @left -->
**Self-esteem**

How do I evaluate my worth?
<!-- @mid -->
**Narcissism**

How central are superiority, entitlement, and admiration?
<!-- @right -->
**Self-compassion**

How do I respond to difficulty—with kindness, shared humanity, and balance?

<!-- slide step -->
## Example: A response to the same error
**Maya receives criticism of a causal inference.**
- Superiority-based defense discounts the evaluator.
- Compassionate acknowledgment permits recognizing the error.
- Measure later engagement and revision quality.
- Do not diagnose a person from a single response.

# 4.7 Motives
## Favorable, familiar, or useful?

<!-- slide -->
## A feedback choice may satisfy several goals
![Self-enhancement favors positive views, verification favors consistency, and improvement favors actionable change.](../assets/module4-feedback-motives.svg =720x)

**Visual description:** Three goals can produce different preferences, but one feedback message may serve several goals at once.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Enhancement and verification often agree
<!-- @left -->
**Positive existing belief**

Confirmation can also be flattering.

Agreement between predictions is not a discriminating test.
<!-- @right -->
**Negative existing belief**

Favorable and confirming feedback can diverge.

Confirmation-seeking does not imply enjoying mistreatment.

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Improvement requires more than an encouraging forecast
<!-- @main -->
- Useful feedback identifies a route to change.
- Upward comparison can inspire or threaten.
- Attainability and resources matter.
- Self-affirmation may support engagement, but effects depend on context.
<!-- @side -->
::: success
**Track the outcome**

Distinguish perceived progress, corrective effort, and demonstrated performance.
:::

<!-- slide step -->
## Example: Why choose a demanding mentor?
**Maya expects detailed methodological criticism.**
- Verification: criticism confirms a familiar self-view.
- Improvement: criticism enables a better design.
- Enhancement: demanding feedback affirms a serious-scholar identity.
- Separate favorability, consistency, credibility, and usefulness.

# 4.8 Integration
## From self-judgment to a test of change

<!-- slide quad -->
## Different biases require different tests
<!-- @tl -->
**Better-than-average**

Compare self and peer standing against a defensible benchmark.
<!-- @tr -->
**Unrealistic optimism**

Compare favorable forecasts with justified probabilities or outcomes.
<!-- @bl -->
**Projection**

Test whether self-information receives excessive weight in judging others.
<!-- @br -->
**Bias blind spot**

Compare perceived susceptibility to bias for self and others.

<!-- slide main-side -->
## A difficult task can reverse a comparative judgment
<!-- @main -->
- Personal difficulty is readily available.
- Other people's difficulty may receive too little weight.
- Below-average judgments can follow.
- Positive self-presentation is not a complete explanation of every comparison.
<!-- @side -->
::: warning
Confidence alone does not establish competence—or incompetence. Specify the task and criterion.
:::

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Design workshop: Reflection and feedback use
<!-- @left -->
**Design**
- Standardized critique
- Random assignment to matched reflections
- Baseline work quality
- A check of the intended process
<!-- @right -->
**Separate outcomes**
- Immediate affect
- Feedback engagement
- Forecasts of improvement
- Blinded revision quality

<!-- slide step -->
## Worked interpretation: Feeling better, same performance
**The reflection changes affect but not detected revision quality.**
- Report the subjective benefit.
- Do not claim improved reasoning.
- Examine uncertainty and measurement sensitivity.
- A performance null is not proof of exact equivalence.
- A mood–performance association would not prove mediation.
