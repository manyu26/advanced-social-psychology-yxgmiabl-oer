## Before anything else — the author's stance
::: warning
Giner-Sorolla **declines** to paint pre-2010 research as lazy or dishonest.

Most researchers genuinely **aligned their idea of good research to the system** they were judged by.

And the new rules remain **open to challenge** too.
:::

# 2.1 The system circa 2010

<!-- slide -->
## Four methodological transformations
{{mermaid
graph LR
  A["~1910<br/>Titchener & Boring:<br/>experimentalist turn"] --> B["1920s<br/>Allport:<br/>individual-based"]
  B --> C["1960s-70s<br/>relevance, ethics,<br/>validity challenged"]
  C --> D["1970s onward<br/>doubts about p &lt; .05<br/>single-study articles"]
}}
The fourth never went away. What changed recently: **gatekeepers started acting on it.**

<!-- slide 2col -->
## The structural tension in every review
<!-- @left -->
{{sp[blue] **Easy, consistent rules**}}
- Apply uniformly, cheap to check
- But may not capture the construct
- `p < .05` is a rule about a *statistic*
<!-- @right -->
{{sp[green] **Complex, nuanced judgment**}}
- Better fit to what we care about
- But different reviewers, different verdicts
- Contested and unpredictable

::: info
Under ~20% acceptance at prestigious journals, researchers had to **anticipate and meet** whichever norms were in force.
:::

# 2.2 The 2011 collision

<!-- slide main-side -->
## A structurally ordinary paper
<!-- @main -->
Nine experiments in the field's **highest-impact empirical journal**, arguing that effects appear on responses *before* the stimulus.

- Extremely novel claim
- Eight significant studies, one marginal at ==p = .098==
- Method variations showing replication and generalization
- State-of-the-art reaction-time priming
<!-- @side -->
::: danger
If valid, it overturns psychology, neurobiology, **and physics**.

So the alternative followed: the **standards** were the problem.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Keep perspective on "crisis"
:::: cols
::: col
**Take it seriously**
Publicized low replication rates can **damage public trust** — there is recent evidence for this.
:::
::: col
**But note the pattern**
Crisis narratives recur in this field. Amid the *crisis of 1977*, someone wrote recalling the *crisis of 1927*.
:::
::::
Neither observation cancels the other.

# 2.3 Eight proposed solutions

<!-- slide -->
## Group them, don't memorize them
| Group | Solutions | The failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| What **enters** the literature | 1 Effect sizes · 2 Nulls within an article · 3 Null-conclusion articles | Only convenient results get in |
| Whether findings **hold** | 4 Direct replication · 5 Statistical power | Detection confused with truth |
| What is **revealed**, and when | 6 Full disclosure · 7 Pre-registration & Registered Reports | Exploration presented as confirmation |
| What the evidence is **for** | 8 Strengthen theory | Weak constructs hollow out any standard |

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Why effect sizes
<!-- @main -->
They answer ==**"but by how much?"**==

- **Meta-analysis** needs a common currency across studies done differently
- Correct **two opposite misreadings**: a significant but tiny effect from a large sample; a null read as "nothing going on"
- Let readers gauge **practical possibilities** — lay readers too, in interpretable units
<!-- @side -->
::: warning
Reporting a statistic ≠ having a **standard** for it.
:::

<!-- slide step -->
## Example — should low effect sizes be rejected?
**Q.** A journal proposes rejecting any manuscript whose central effect size falls below a practical-significance threshold, even when reliably nonzero. Evaluate.

1. The chapter's answer is ==a resounding **no**==
2. It confuses **reporting** (enforceable) with **standard-setting** (no consensus)
3. Small effects can matter — across populations, over time, at low intervention cost
4. It **recreates the pathology**: a threshold on a statistic as proxy for worth, plus new incentives to inflate effect sizes
5. It cuts against Solutions 2 and 3, suppressing exactly the results that reveal an effect is small

::: success
**The remedy is qualification, not exclusion** — and note the general shape: a reform that replaces judgment with a rule tends to *relocate* the problem.
:::

# 2.4 Manipulation and measurement

<!-- slide main-side -->
## "Nothing so theoretical as a good method"
<!-- @main -->
Tighter **constructs** produce clearer **operationalizations**. Theory quality and method quality are one problem seen twice.

Specify which part of a method is **essential** to the theory and which is **incidental**.
<!-- @side -->
::: warning
Meta-analysis over discrepant methodologies yields an average **no more meaningful than a mean body weight taken across every animal in a zoo**.
:::

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Two failures, one root
<!-- @left -->
**Measurement**
- Undisclosed flexibility — which items to use
- Poor reliability, unvalidated scales
- Psychometric concerns *routinely ignored* in research and publishing
<!-- @right -->
**Manipulation**
- A positive result was long taken as proof the manipulation worked
- That inference **collapses** under replication and Registered Reports
- Is a null a near-zero effect, or a failed setup?

<!-- slide -->
## What a manipulation check cannot settle
::: warning
- Same-session measures **cannot by themselves** establish or rule out a manipulation's validity
- **Self-report checks can change the procedure** — they make participants aware of the manipulated variable
- Nonverbal checks have been suggested as alternatives
- A stronger position holds checks unnecessary, since they inform us only at an *operational* level
:::

# 2.5 Challenges to generalizability

<!-- slide main-side -->
## WEIRD is not a sampling footnote
<!-- @main -->
**W**estern **E**ducated **I**ndustrialized **R**ich **D**emocracies — focused attention on cultural limits of participants *(the acronym's scope has itself been criticized)*.

A center–periphery ordering runs from the USA outward. Within countries, **rural** populations are understudied; people outside agrarian or industrial society barely appear.
<!-- @side -->
::: danger
The problem is **structural**: who does research, who is studied, and whose work must justify its scope.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## The documented mechanisms
- Researchers, editors and participants drawn **overwhelmingly from central** nations
- Central-population research treated as ==normal and automatically generalizable==; peripheral groups labeled as **special cases**
- Bibliometrics: central-sample studies are **less likely to signal their origin in the title** — and peripheral work is then perceived as less important
- Surveyed researchers in peripheral zones report hearing their work is too narrow, or asked for demonstrations central-zone work never faces

<!-- slide -->
## How the claim gets made by omission
::: info
Few articles claim to describe **all humankind**.

A typical article does not claim to discuss "humans" — it describes a decision bias, a process, a set of correlations, **without addressing generalizability** — while linking findings to "people."
:::
Easy to spot once named. Hard to unsee. Look for it in every paper this term.

# 2.6 From principle to practice

<!-- slide -->
## Two dimensions, not one
| | Exploratory **question** | Confirmatory **hypothesis** |
|---|---|---|
| **Exploratory analysis** | Legitimate — if honestly labelled | Weakest cell to report as confirmation |
| **Confirmatory analysis** | Plan fixed in advance, question open | ==Strongest evidential claim available== |

::: info
Exploratory: intuition-driven, no strong theory, purpose is to **generate** theory.
Confirmatory: follows a theory strong enough to be wrong, purpose is to **risk** it.
:::

<!-- slide main-side -->
## HARKing
<!-- @main -->
**H**ypothesizing **A**fter **R**esults are **K**nown — presenting a hypothesis formed *after* seeing the data as though it preceded them.

It moves a result to the strongest cell without any of the work that move requires.
<!-- @side -->
::: danger
The damage is not one bad paper.

A field that cannot tell its exploratory results from its confirmatory ones **cannot tell which claims have actually been risked**.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## What a serious pre-registration contains
:::: cols
::: col
- Target sample size **and stopping rule**
- Inclusion / exclusion criteria
- Manipulations and conditions
- How each predictor is constructed
:::
::: col
- Dependent measures and their construction
- Planned covariates
- Specific tests, naming operational variables
- Follow-up analyses · Type I error control
:::
::::

::: warning
**Do not follow it blindly.** The point is to make departures **visible**, not impossible. Report the plan, the deviation, and why. A silently abandoned pre-registration is worse than none — it lends unearned credibility.
:::

# 2.7 Power, and what to do about it

<!-- slide -->
## The intuition is wrong
::: danger
Low power does not only risk **missing** effects. It corrupts the effects you **find**.
:::
- Estimates swing from sample to sample
- Below roughly **50% power**, the average *significant* effect size is dramatically **inflated** — only the large, lucky estimates clear the threshold
- Below about **10% power**, estimates can point in the ==wrong direction entirely==

A significant result from an underpowered design is not merely weak. Its magnitude, and possibly its sign, are untrustworthy.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Raising power without more participants
<!-- @left -->
- **Within-subject designs** — each person is their own comparison
- **Sequential analysis** — pre-specified interim points, stop when the criterion is met
- **Aggregation** across studies designed to combine
<!-- @right -->
- **Multi-lab collaboration** — also buys generalizability
- **Bayesian approaches** — evidence accumulates without a fixed N

::: warning
**The trap:** planning around an optimistic *published* effect size — inflated for the reason above — produces an underpowered study. This is how careful researchers waste their resources.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Three honest ways to report power
| Form | When it fits | What it states |
|---|---|---|
| **A priori** | You chose N | The estimate, its source, target N, padding for exclusions |
| **Sensitivity** | N was fixed by circumstance | What effect size that N could detect, against a field benchmark |
| **Range** | The literature disagrees with itself | Power across the plausible interval of prior estimates |

<!-- slide step -->
## Example — when is a null informative?
**Q.** Your replication returns null. What must hold before that means "no effect"?

1. **Adequate power** — a real effect would probably have been detected
2. **Unlikely to be Type II error**
3. **A manipulation check was included**
4. **An analysis that can support the null** — equivalence testing or Bayesian, not merely failing to reject

::: success
**The check does the decisive work.** Manipulation moved the check but not the DV → the effect really is near zero. Manipulation moved **neither** → you probably never manipulated the construct. The outcome data look identical either way.
:::

<!-- slide template=section -->
# 2.8 Programmatic research

<!-- slide main-side -->
## One study is one brick
<!-- @main -->
A single study is always limited and always biased in some respect. Methodological care is what makes each brick solid enough to build on.

So replication is not policing — it is ==how a literature accumulates==.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
And the three kinds answer **three different questions**.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Three kinds of replication
| Kind | Varies | Buys confidence that… |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | As little as possible | the specific manipulations and measures produce the effect at all |
| **Systematic** | Incidental context — stimuli, cover story, experimenter, sample | those details really were irrelevant, as the original assumed |
| **Conceptual** | The operationalization of the construct itself | the results **mean** what they were claimed to mean |

::: info
Order matters: a conceptual replication that succeeds while the **direct** one fails tells you something very different from one that succeeds alongside it.
:::

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Making claims auditable
<!-- @left -->
**Constraints on generality**
State how far you expect the finding to hold — which stimuli, samples, situations, and why.

Converts §2.5's *implicit* generality claim into a falsifiable one, and gives a replication a fair-test criterion.
<!-- @right -->
**Publication bias**
Positive results publish preferentially, so meta-analysis inherits the selection.

- Lean on **moderator** analyses
- Report **all** results — six tests, not the four that worked
- Seek unpublished work

<!-- slide -->
## The dispute is itself the lesson
:::: cols
::: col
**The critique**
Confidence intervals used inappropriately · replication samples from different populations · incomplete endorsement of protocols by original authors · a rival large project found a higher success rate
:::
::: col
**The reply**
The interval methods were correct · the rival project's results were mischaracterized · and the sharper point — **sample variation in a direct replication is a test of generalizability, not an infidelity**
:::
::::

::: success
**Common ground, which students usually miss:** such a project cannot by itself license a pessimistic verdict on the field, and what counts as a *faithful* replication is a substantive question, not a technicality.
:::

<!-- slide template=closing -->
# One thread
- Evidential standards, measurement validity and generalizability are **one problem, not three**
- You can raise power, pre-register everything, share all data — and still not know *what* you measured or *whom* you measured it on
- Four questions to carry into every reading from Module 3 onward
