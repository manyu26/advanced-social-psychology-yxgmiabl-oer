## Knowing the unknowable — how people read minds they cannot see

::: danger
Person perception looks **far more geared to getting things done than to getting them right**.

# 3.1 The miracle, and why accuracy is the wrong default

<!-- slide 2col -->
## An uncomfortable finding about the field
<!-- @left -->
**Rarely about "persons"**
Not whole autonomous individuals — the people perceived arrive as *partial, broken-up, motionless fragments*.
<!-- @right -->
**Rarely about "perception"**
Not immediate encoding of what reaches eye or ear — what the field calls perception is really **cognition and judgment**.

::: info
A critique of the field's own construct, delivered inside a review of it — and an echo of Module 2 on constructs drifting from their operationalizations.
:::

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Why accuracy is hard to even measure
<!-- @main -->
**The criterion problem.** How do we know there's a *there* there?

- What indicates the target's **actual** feelings or personality?
- Targets' self-report ==cannot simply serve== as the objective criterion
- Even with a standard, raw distance between standard and judgment doesn't capture **ability**
<!-- @side -->
::: warning
Module 2, arriving early: without a validated criterion, a well-powered accuracy study measures *something* — not necessarily accuracy.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Where does accuracy live?
{{mermaid
graph TD
  A["Accurate judgment"] --> B["The perceiver<br/>simply good at<br/>reading minds"]
  A --> C["The target<br/>simply an open book<br/>any perceiver could read"]
  A --> D["The relationship<br/>perceiver and target<br/>resonating together"]
}}
Earlier research was doing the math wrong — and even corrected, the math said nothing about the perceiver's **processes**.

# 3.2 Baselines — before meeting

<!-- slide -->
## Perception begins before input
{{sp[info] The counterintuitive claim}} Person perception **commences before any sensory input**.

{{mermaid
graph LR
  A["Perceivers seek<br/>intent"] --> B["Intent implies<br/>a mind"]
  B --> C["People are moved by their own<br/>thoughts, beliefs, desires"]
  C --> D["Intuitive default:<br/>internal over external movers"]
  D --> E["Applied at times even to<br/>non-human targets"]
}}

<!-- slide 2col -->
## What arrives before the person does
<!-- @left -->
**Valence**
A broadly **optimistic** stance, before meeting at all.
<!-- @right -->
**Content**
That humans have **agency** and experience **complex emotions**.

::: warning
Note what is already committed: the target is an autonomous intentional agent whose unobservable states can be inferred from behavior. The **fundamental attribution error's raw material is laid down here** — an explanatory preference for internal causes, installed before any evidence arrives.
:::

# 3.3 Freeze-frame — categories, warmth, competence

<!-- slide main-side -->
## The first instant
<!-- @main -->
Demographic categories **automatically** capture societal identity — gender, race, class — from **superficial cues**.

The perceiver mind-reads from the outset. But what they mainly decide is:

==**approach** as ingroup, or **avoid** as outgroup==
<!-- @side -->
::: info
Simple trait inferences may follow from categorizing a behavior:

*handing over money reads as generosity*
:::

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Two dimensions, interacting
<!-- @left -->
{{sp[blue] **Warmth / communality**}}
The target's **intent** — their interest in playing well with others.

Signals → **safety**
<!-- @right -->
{{sp[green] **Competence**}}
The target's **capability**.

Signals → **dominance**

::: warning
**Not independent checkboxes.** They interact — a target low on warmth might well be higher on competence. Perceived **interdependence** predicts perceived warmth.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## The Stereotype Content Model's space
| | High competence | Low competence |
|---|---|---|
| **High warmth** | The ingroup and its allies | — |
| **Low warmth** | — | Society's most marginalized |

::: danger
Learn this properly now. **Module 11 introduces no separate mechanism** — stereotyping *is* this category-based process applied to groups, and this space carries over directly.
:::

<!-- slide step -->
## Example — is person perception just prejudice renamed?
**Q.** "Categorization is automatic, from superficial cues, delivering approach-or-avoid. So why are these different topics?"

1. **Partly right** — the chapter does not soften the continuity; same cues, same dimensions, same model
2. **But categorization is one stage** — state decoding, then trait inference, move from category-based toward *individuating*
3. **The baseline stage precedes it** and is not group-specific — intent, mind, agency, applied to the target as a human
4. **Automatic ≠ fixed** — targets curate, interaction is interdependent, interdependence shifts perceived warmth

::: success
**Better formulation:** prejudice and individuating impressions are outputs of the *same apparatus* under different conditions — time, attention, interdependence, motivation. That predicts *when* each occurs.
:::

<!-- slide template=section -->
# 3.4 Real-time — decoding states

<!-- slide 2col -->
## States vs traits — a temporal distinction
<!-- @left -->
**States**
- Momentary, rapidly sensed
- Face, posture, movement
- Felt as **immediately accessible**
- Mind-reading is *deeper* here than in categorization
<!-- @right -->
**Traits**
- Require **accumulation across occasions**
- Inferred from *scraps* of behavior in context
- Should entail **consistency** of multiple observations

<!-- slide template=section -->
# 3.5 Over time — traits and consistency

<!-- slide -->
## The gap where the interesting findings live
- **Normatively:** a trait attribution ought to rest on the same thing recurring across separate occasions
- **But:** spontaneously categorizing what trait a behavior implies is an ==earlier, immediate== inference — from a *single* observation
- So a perceiver holds trait beliefs formed in one instant alongside beliefs accumulated over months
- **With no phenomenological marker distinguishing them**

::: info
**Consistency, weight and context** govern how observations combine — how much one counts, and how its situation modulates what it licenses.
:::

# 3.6 Active targets and interaction

<!-- slide main-side -->
## The target is not a lump
<!-- @main -->
Targets are **steadily and reciprocally managing** what they let out — with goals that may not align with the perceiver's.

The most powerful lever is simply **choosing what to say and what to withhold**.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
James: we carry a distinct social self for every audience whose regard matters to us, showing a **different face to each**.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Self-presentation: five goals, five risks
Being **liked** · **respected** · **admired** · **feared** · **helped**

Each has a way of enacting it — and a characteristic **downside risk**.

::: warning
**Ingratiation** means agreeing with the perceiver — but it must be *sincere*. The risk is appearing inauthentic, and mere flattery backfires.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Both roles at once, interdependently
{{mermaid
graph LR
  A["Person A"] -->|"reads B's cues"| B["Person B"]
  B -->|"reads A's cues"| A
  A -->|"manages own impression"| A
  B -->|"manages own impression"| B
}}
Emotions change in response to others' emotions. Disclosure depth **matches what was just received**.

<!-- slide -->
## The chapter's own spoiler
::: danger
The most significant context for person perception is **interactions between two interdependent people**.
:::
Which means most of the literature — perceivers judging static stimuli — has been studying the **less significant case**.

New neuroimaging and AI methods are what make the naturalistic case tractable.

==Module 2 in different clothes: the paradigm was chosen for feasibility, and the field's picture is shaped accordingly.==

<!-- slide -->
## Perceivers are opaque to themselves
- What survives is usually **the conclusion, not the evidence** behind it
- They cannot say why one face is more attractive, or why they chose one option
- They construct **plausible but possibly irrelevant** explanations instead
- Sometimes they infer their own emotions by watching their own choices — as an external observer would

<!-- slide step -->
## Where attribution enters
1. Anchor on a **character** judgment — *Leslie is cranky*
2. **Correct** toward the situation — *Leslie's parents are away*
3. But people are often **unaware they fail to complete** the correction
4. And they ==overlook most reliably their own contribution to the situation==
5. Correction can be **curtailed** when time or effort runs short

::: info
For the classic systematic account of causal inference, read **Kelley & Michela (1980)**, assigned for this module. Come to it directly — these slides deliberately do not summarize it.
:::

# 3.7 The machinery — automatic and controlled

<!-- slide -->
## Three organizing principles
{{mermaid
graph TD
  A["People deploy automatic and<br/>deliberate processes <b>flexibly</b>"] --> D["Social cognition"]
  B["People attend <b>pragmatically</b><br/>to cues that seem diagnostic"] --> D
  C["People store what looks <b>useful</b><br/>for later"] --> D
}}
All three are **pragmatic** claims — the same stance behind the chapter's rejection of accuracy as the goal.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Will is not intent
<!-- @left -->
**Will**
Goals, and a desired end state.
<!-- @right -->
**Intent**
Choice, attention and effort — with a **presumption of alternatives**.

Someone who could have been on time and was not was late *intentionally*.

::: info
Between them sits **goal-related automaticity** — where most social cognition actually happens. Motives operate along the whole spectrum, and automatic processing sometimes wins *because* a motive favors it.
:::

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Effort does not guarantee accuracy
<!-- @main -->
- A need to belong feeds **ingroup favoritism** without deliberation
- Thinking hard about someone you are attracted to does **not** yield objective information — the motive to affiliate shapes the deliberation itself
<!-- @side -->
::: warning
Deliberate ≠ accurate.

The motive is inside the deliberation, not outside it.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Three kinds of priming — three levels of confidence
| Kind | Activates | Evidential status |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronic / habitual** | Concepts made accessible by repetition; individuals differ in which | Well established |
| **Conscious** | Semantic content, attitudes, evaluations the person is aware of | Well established |
| **Subliminal** | Associations cued without awareness — semantic, affective, **behavioral** | ==Depends sharply on which== |

::: danger
**Subliminal priming does not travel as a unit.** Semantic priming is relatively uncontested. Affective priming is not much disputed. **Behavioral** priming produced many findings that failed to replicate.

Treating "priming" as one phenomenon with one evidential status is exactly the construct-breadth error Module 2 warned about.
:::

# 3.8 Attention, accessibility, memory

<!-- slide 2col -->
## What captures attention
<!-- @left -->
**From faces**
- Babyish features
- Direction of gaze
- Trait inferences straight from appearance — happy-looking reads trustworthy, angry-looking untrustworthy
<!-- @right -->
**Salience, from many sources**
- Visual novelty, color
- Physical position — front vs back
- Goal relevance · emotional relevance
- Rarity · negative behavior

::: danger
Snap competence judgments from photographs **predict voting preferences**. First impressions are not harmless.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Salience → accessibility → judgment
{{mermaid
graph LR
  A["Salience<br/>in the scene"] --> D["Accessibility"]
  B["A prime"] --> D
  C["Chronic habit"] --> D
  D --> E["Shapes the<br/>next judgment"]
}}
Three routes in, one consequence out — §3.7's priming and §3.8's attention are the same construct from different sides.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## How social information is stored
<!-- @left -->
**Associative networks**
Persons – traits – behaviors. Activation makes future activation easier — adaptive, since recently used information is likely needed again. Mainly **declarative**.

**Procedural**: learned sequences, routine judgments.
<!-- @right -->
**Top-down representations**
Categories, concepts, expectations — emphasized more than bottom-up detail.

Categories behave as **fuzzy sets**: exemplars held together by family resemblance around a **prototype**, not by necessary and sufficient conditions.

::: warning
Which is why "all ___ are ___" is so easy to complete and so hard to defend. Hold this until Module 11.
:::

<!-- slide template=section -->
# 3.9 Attribution

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Correspondent inference
<!-- @main -->
When someone acts, does the act point back to a matching intention behind it — and so to some attitude or trait worth attributing to them?

The task is to decide whether behavior is diagnostic of the **person** or merely of the **circumstances**.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
This depends directly on §3.2 — the chain from choice to intent to mind is what makes correspondence look available at all.
:::

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Two biases
<!-- @left -->
**Personal interest shapes the inference**
Your manager fails to greet you → the inference reaches for something about you and your standing.

An unfamiliar colleague does the same → it barely registers as needing explanation.

==The behavior is constant; the inferential effort is not.==
<!-- @right -->
**Dispositional over situational**
Causes inside the person are overweighted, causes in the situation underweighted.

This is the bias §3.6 traced to the **intentional default** installed before any evidence arrives.

<!-- slide -->
## Kelley's covariation criteria
| Criterion | The question | Rules out |
|---|---|---|
| **Consensus** | Do others behave this way toward this target? | A person-level explanation, if high |
| **Distinctiveness** | Does this person behave this way toward other targets? | A target-level explanation, if low |
| **Consistency** | Does this hold across occasions? | A one-off circumstance, if high |

::: info
Its value is not as a description of what perceivers **do** — they often collect none of this — but as a standard for seeing what they **omit**. Note that *consistency across occasions* is the same requirement §3.5 said trait inference demands and routinely fails to meet.
:::

# 3.10 Measuring what is implicit

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Why implicit measures exist
<!-- @main -->
Explicit measures have an obvious weakness: people can decline to report what they would rather not — and per §3.6 may **not have access to it anyway**.

Implicit measures target automatic responses instead. They rely on **priming**.
<!-- @side -->
::: danger
Which means everything in §3.7 about priming's heterogeneity applies to them.
:::

<!-- slide -->
## Why the results vary so much
**What changes the size of a priming effect:**

- **Pairing structure** — stimulus–**response** produces larger effects than stimulus–stimulus. S–R adds a second goal to an already fast task, related in structure to the **Stroop** task
- **Task type** — stereotype-categorization and weapon-identification produce larger effects. A lexical decision task has the opposite virtue: the judgment is *unrelated* to the stereotype
- **Stereotype dimension** — race produces larger priming than gender
- **Prime–target interval** — short or typical beats long

**What barely mattered:** verbal vs non-verbal primes · stimulus content (labels, jobs, traits)

<!-- slide -->
## What follows
::: danger
"An implicit measure showed X" is **not an interpretable claim** on its own.

Which procedure? Which pairing structure? Which interval? Each choice changes the magnitude — and some plausibly change *what is being measured*.
:::
This is Module 2's construct-validity problem in a live literature, and why pooling discrepant methodologies in a meta-analysis estimates nothing in particular.

<!-- slide -->
## A stereotyping mechanism to carry to Module 11
- Obvious categories — race and gender from a face, group membership, accent — **capture attention**
- Accessibility then guides category formation in memory
- Category activation is ==more likely under cognitive load, not less==
- But motivational factors cut the other way: **perspective-taking**, **motivation to be unbiased**, and induced **guilt** each reduce spontaneous stereotyping
- Valence rides along: exposure to faces of a social group can speed recognition of otherwise irrelevant valenced words

<!-- slide template=closing -->
# One thread
- Person perception is built **for use, not for truth** — and that explains its architecture
- Inference before evidence, categories before subtlety, conclusions kept while data are discarded
- The real cost lands last: perceivers **neglect their own contribution** to the situations they explain — and cannot detect that they have
