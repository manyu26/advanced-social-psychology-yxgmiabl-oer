# 9.1 Influence and explanation

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Influence and explanation
<!-- @left -->
**Information**
- Others provide evidence
- Ambiguity can increase reliance
- Private judgment may change
<!-- @right -->
**Social consequences**
- Approval or disapproval
- Audience and status
- Public agreement may exceed private acceptance

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
A group becomes more accurate after discussion. Is that evidence against conformity?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

No. Alignment can occur through useful information and improve accuracy.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
No. Alignment can occur through useful information and improve accuracy. Specify what changed and whether participants used reliable evidence. Conformity describes alignment; its accuracy and mechanism are separate questions. Compare discussion with independent judgments and a relevant criterion.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 3, 4, 5, 6.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A student endorses a group answer publicly but gives the original answer anonymously. What does this pattern suggest?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

It is consistent with public compliance shaped by social consequences. It does not uniquely establish that process: differences in timing, instructions, or perceived confidentiality could matter. Compare equivalent judgments under randomized audience conditions and assess what participants believe others expect.

# 9.2 Value in a social setting

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Value in a social setting
<!-- @left -->
**Inputs to value**
- Expected benefits and effort
- Identity relevance
- Connection, approval, and status
<!-- @right -->
**Evidence about valuation**
- Behavioral choices
- Subjective appraisals
- Neural signals with bounded interpretation

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Brain activity during a message predicts later participation. What additional claim would require different evidence?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

That the activity is necessary or sufficient for the behavioral change requires causal evidence beyond prediction.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
That the activity is necessary or sufficient for the behavioral change requires causal evidence beyond prediction. Also test whether the signal adds information beyond prior motivation and other measured predictors. The prediction can be useful without proving a unique mechanism.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 7, 8, 9, 10, 11.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A message makes volunteering seem more consistent with a student’s identity. What outcome would test the proposed behavior-change pathway?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

Measure perceived identity relevance, the relative appeal of volunteering, and actual participation using an appropriate comparison. An increase in stated identity fit alone establishes neither increased value nor behavior. Even a statistical indirect effect requires assumptions before it can support a causal mediation claim.

# 9.3 Relevance, identity, and autonomy

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Relevance, identity, and autonomy
<!-- @left -->
**Potential benefits**
- Personally relevant consequences
- Connection with valued identity
- Meaningful reasons and feasible choices
<!-- @right -->
**Potential limits**
- Identity threat
- Perceived pressure
- A label is not a mechanism

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
An authority endorsement increases agreement. List a mechanism that concerns information and one that concerns social consequences.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Recipients may infer that an expert has reliable evidence, or they may expect approval for agreement and disapproval for dissent.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Recipients may infer that an expert has reliable evidence, or they may expect approval for agreement and disapproval for dissent. Hold credentials and audience consequences apart where possible. The same source can engage both pathways, so the endorsement effect alone does not discriminate them.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 5, 6, 12, 13, 14, 15, 18, 19, 22, 23, 24.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

Two students receive the same message about contributing to a shared dataset. One values collaboration; the other worries about credit. Why might their responses differ?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

The message can change different components of value for each recipient. Collaboration may be an immediate identity benefit for one, while unresolved credit concerns remain a cost for the other. Measure those appraisals and address the actual barrier rather than assume that greater message repetition solves the problem.

# 9.4 From intention to action

<!-- slide 2col -->
## From intention to action
<!-- @left -->
**Different barriers**
- Unclear next action
- Missing skill or access
- Time and administrative effort
<!-- @right -->
**Different interventions**
- Implementation plan
- Training and resources
- Reduced friction or revised default

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
In a hypothetical randomized trial, 45 of 100 invited students sign up under a simplified process versus 30 of 100 under the standard process. Interpret the difference.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

The observed difference is 15 percentage points, equivalent to a 50% increase relative to the 30% comparison rate.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
The observed difference is 15 percentage points, equivalent to a 50% increase relative to the 30% comparison rate. The estimates need uncertainty intervals and the trial’s assumptions. They describe registration under these conditions, not a guaranteed increase in participation or learning.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A campus program shortens a registration form, and sign-ups rise. Must participants now value the program more?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

No. Lower effort can increase participation without changing evaluation. A revised form might also signal accessibility or institutional support. Measure value and perceived effort separately, and track attendance or completion. Registration is an intermediate behavior rather than the final educational outcome.

# 9.5 Norms and reference groups

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Norms and reference groups
<!-- @left -->
**Descriptive norms**
- What people do
- Which reference group?
- How prevalent is it?
<!-- @right -->
**Injunctive norms**
- What people approve
- Which expectations are salient?
- What consequences are anticipated?

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
A department reports 80% approval of data sharing. Can that number be used as evidence that 80% actually share data?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

No. Approval is an injunctive-norm measure; observed sharing is a descriptive-behavior measure.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
No. Approval is an injunctive-norm measure; observed sharing is a descriptive-behavior measure. Measure each with a clear denominator and time period. Their divergence can be theoretically useful rather than a reason to substitute one for the other.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 5, 6, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A poster says that many students submit work late while urging punctuality. Why might the message have competing effects?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

It communicates disapproval but also suggests that lateness is common. Recipients may infer that the behavior is normal despite the request. Compare messages with accurate, clearly specified norm information and assess perceived prevalence and approval separately before interpreting behavior.

# 9.6 Groups and distributed information

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Groups and distributed information
<!-- @left -->
**Observe the discussion**
- Initial judgments
- Who holds which information?
- Status and speaking opportunities
<!-- @right -->
**Evaluate the result**
- Agreement or extremity
- Accuracy against a criterion
- Use of previously unshared evidence

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
A structured discussion increases the number of speakers but leaves decision quality unchanged. What should an evaluation report?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Participation increased under the measured conditions, while the study did not detect a decision-quality gain.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Participation increased under the measured conditions, while the study did not detect a decision-quality gain. Examine uncertainty and whether the task offered information that discussion could improve. Do not silently replace the original outcome with participation or dismiss participation if it was independently valued.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 3, 4, 5, 25, 29, 30.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A committee agrees unanimously after its chair speaks first. Which observation would help distinguish good integration from deference?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

Compare initial private judgments and examine whether members introduce and use independent evidence. Vary speaking order or collect initial judgments before discussion. Unanimity is compatible with accurate convergence and social pressure; it cannot identify either on its own.

<!-- slide -->
## Groups and distributed information: key distinctions
- Polarization: movement toward a more extreme initial tendency.
- Groupthink: concurrence seeking weakens scrutiny or dissent.
- Assess direction and decision quality separately (Li, n.d.).

# 9.7 Networks and message sharing

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Networks and message sharing
<!-- @left -->
**Sender and network**
- Expected usefulness to others
- Identity and social rewards
- Who can reach whom?
<!-- @right -->
**Recipient and outcome**
- Actual exposure
- Interpretation and credibility
- Later behavior

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Friends adopt similar study tools. What design could provide stronger evidence of peer influence than a one-time network survey?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Randomize an information intervention or access among network members when feasible, then track exposure and adoption over time.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Randomize an information intervention or access among network members when feasible, then track exposure and adoption over time. Account for spillovers and shared contexts. Longitudinal association can improve timing information, but random or plausibly exogenous exposure offers stronger leverage on influence.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 1, 2, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A message receives many shares but no detectable increase in program uptake. What does this establish?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

It documents sharing and no detected uptake change in the evaluated setting. The message may not reach eligible recipients, may be misinterpreted, or may fail to address barriers. Those are hypotheses to test. High reach should not be relabeled as a behavioral effect.

# 9.8 Narratives and evaluation

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Narratives and evaluation
<!-- @left -->
**Narrative processes**
- Attention and identification
- Perspective and social connection
- Possible changes in counterarguing
<!-- @right -->
**Evaluation plan**
- Target behavior and timeframe
- Comparison and mechanism measures
- Maintenance, access, and spillovers

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Propose a design comparing a narrative with a factual message without confounding story format and information quality.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Match the central facts, length, source, and exposure as closely as the research question permits.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Match the central facts, length, source, and exposure as closely as the research question permits. Measure identification and engagement separately from knowledge and behavior. Randomize format and assess uncertainty. Complete equivalence is difficult, so document remaining differences and keep the causal claim tied to the tested materials.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 34, 35, 36, 37.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A campaign improves story recall and intentions, but the follow-up behavior measure is missing for half the sample. What conclusion is justified?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

Report the observed recall and intention outcomes and the limits of the behavioral evidence. Examine attrition patterns and use appropriate sensitivity analyses; missingness may depend on engagement or outcomes. The campaign has not established a robust behavior-change effect merely because upstream measures improved.

# What evidence would change the explanation?
- State the construct precisely.
- Match the measure to the claim.
- Compare explanations with a discriminating result.
- Connect the conclusion to your research commentary.
