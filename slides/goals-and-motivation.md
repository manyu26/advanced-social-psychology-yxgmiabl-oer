## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Adding three alternative study strategies reduces use of the original strategy. Does that prove motivation to learn declined?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

No. Effort may have shifted to substitute means while the broader goal remained important.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
No. Effort may have shifted to substitute means while the broader goal remained important. Measure total goal-directed activity and learning, not only use of the original route. Alternatively, the options may dilute perceived instrumentality; that mechanism requires evidence rather than inference from a single behavior.

## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A student uses a writing group for productivity and friendship, and also writes alone to advance the manuscript. Identify both structural patterns.
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

The group is multifinal because it serves productivity and friendship. The group and solo writing are equifinal routes to manuscript progress. These labels describe connections; they do not establish which route is most effective or motivating. Measure progress and perceived instrumentality separately.

## Goals and means: key distinctions
- Equifinality: several means to a goal.
- Multifinality: one means serves several goals.
- Counterfinality: advancing one goal obstructs another.

## The timing of rewards (Woolley & Fishbach, 2016)

<!-- @left -->
**Paradigm A**: Longitudinal tracking 
-  Survey participants in early January, asked them to rate resolution on (1) delayed rewards and (2) immediate rewards. Follow up in March on the actual adherence and persistence. 
- Results: delayed rewards (health, career) as the reason they chose their resolution. However, delayed rewards completely failed to predict adherence in March. Only the presence of immediate rewards (enjoyment) statistically predicted who was still persisting months later.

<!-- @right -->
**Paradigm B:** Behavioral field experiment.
- Immediate reward condition - choose a workout based on what they would enjoy right now
- Delayed reward condition - choose a workout based on what would be most useful for their long-term health and fitness goals.
- DV: number of sets and repetitions completed, weight used, time spent.
- Results: Participants in the immediate-reward condition completed significantly more sets of their chosen exercise than those in the delayed-reward condition.

## The timing of rewards (Woolley & Fishbach, 2016)

- In another study within the paper focusing on healthy eating, instructing participants to focus on the immediate rewards of healthy food (e.g., "focus on the sweet, crunchy taste of the carrot") increased actual consumption of the healthy food far more than instructing them to focus on its long-term health benefits.

:::info
- Focusing on the immediate reward (how fun the workout was, or the immediate taste/experience of the healthy food) was a significantly stronger predictor of actual persistence.
- Immediate gratification plus long-term goal creates a "perceptual fusion" between the activity and the goal. 
:::


*Woolley, K., & Fishbach, A. (2016). For the fun of it: Harnessing immediate rewards to increase persistence in long-term goals. Journal of Consumer Research, 42(6), 952-966.*<!-- slide 2col -->
## Single-point or range goal?

<!-- @left -->
## Single-point target (e.g., run 5k)

- **Attainability (Self-Efficacy)**: If the target is set too high, early setbacks destroy perceived feasibility, leading to goal abandonment.
- **Challenge (Effort Mobilization)**: If the target is set too low to ensure success, it fails to mobilize maximum effort and leaves potential performance on the table.
- **The Binary Trap**: Single-point targets create a strict pass/fail dichotomy. Missing a daily target by a narrow margin often triggers the "what-the-hell effect" (a complete collapse of self-regulation for the remainder of the period).

<!-- @right -->
## Range goal/Dual reference  (e.g., run 3-5k)
- **The Attainability Floor (The Lower Bound)**: The lower end acts as a psychological safety net. It is highly attainable, which protects baseline self-efficacy and guarantees an early experience of success. 
- **The Challenge Ceiling (The Upper Bound)**: Once the lower bound is secured, the upper end of the range takes over. The ceiling remains as an aspirational target. This engages the goal-gradient effect, pulling the individual toward maximum effort.
<!-- slide -->
## Goal specificity (Scott & Nowlis, 2013)

- Single-Target Condition: Participants were given a specific number of words to find (e.g., "Find exactly 4 words").
- Range-Target Condition: Participants were given a range target with the same objective median (e.g., "Find 2 to 6 words").
- Control / "Do Your Best" Condition: (Used in variations to show the baseline weakness of non-specific goals).
- Subjective Mediators: After the round, participants rated how attainable they felt the goal was, and how challenging they felt the goal was.
- Behavioral DV (Reengagement): Participants were then told the formal experiment was over, but they were given the option to either play another round of the Boggle game for fun or do an alternative, unrelated task. The choice to play again served as the measure of goal reengagement.

:::info
Key findings:
- For the behavioral DV, range-goal condition > single-target
- Attainable and perceived challenge were the mediator
:::<!-- slide -->
## Approach and Avoidance Motivation
- Most fundamental distinctions in motivation science
- **Approach Goals**: The objective is to reduce the discrepancy between the current state and a desired end-state (e.g., "I want to earn an A"). Progress monitoring is straightforward: the closer you get, the clearer the feedback.
- **Avoidance Goals**: The objective is to increase or maintain the discrepancy between the current state and an undesired end-state (e.g., "I must not fail this class"). Progress monitoring is ambiguous because the "anti-goal" is never truly defeated until the time period expires.
- Cognitive load and affective states 
	- Approach triggers an eagerness system, leading to emotions ranging from cheerfulness (when successful) to dejection/sadness (when failing).
	- Avoidance triggers a vigilance system, leading to emotions ranging from quiescence/relief (when successful) to anxiety/agitation (when failing). Avoidance goals inherently consume more working memory because they require continuous scanning of the environment for threats.<!-- slide -->
## Regulatory Focus Theory (Crowe & Higgins, 1997)
- Regulatory Focus Theory. Higgins reconceptualized approach/avoidance as Promotion Focus (approaching ideals and gains) versus Prevention Focus (avoiding ought-failures and losses).
- IV: write about either their hopes and aspirations (Promotion) or their duties and obligations (Prevention)
- DV: signal-detection task (e.g., identifying target words from a list).
- Results: 
	- Promotion-focused individuals wanted to maximize "hits." They generated more alternatives and were willing to guess, making them more creative but prone to false alarms (errors of commission).
	- Prevention-focused individuals wanted to minimize "false alarms." They were highly vigilant, conservative, and rejected risky alternatives, leading to more misses (errors of omission).
    
:::info
Approach and avoidance are not just motivational fuel; they act as distinct cognitive lenses that fundamentally alter how individuals process information, tolerate risk, and define success.
:::
<!-- slide -->
## 2x2 achievement goal framework (Elliot & McGregor, 2001)

- Integrating regulatory focus with Mastery (self-referential improvement) versus Performance (normative comparison against others) 
- Longitudinal study of undergrads, tracking students' study strategies, test anxiety, and final exam performance. They measured students' baseline of:
	- Mastery-Approach (striving to learn)
	- Mastery-Avoidance (striving not to lose skills/forget)
	- Performance-Approach (striving to outperform peers), and
	- Performance-Avoidance (striving not to look dumb compared to peers). 
<!-- slide -->
## 2x2 achievement goal framework (Elliot & McGregor, 2001)

- Results
	- Performance-Avoidance was highly maladaptive. It positively predicted anticipatory test anxiety, surface-level processing (rote memorization), and worse exam performance.
	- Mastery-Approach positively predicted deep cognitive processing and intrinsic motivation.
	- Performance-Approach yielded mixed results—it sometimes boosted actual exam grades, but did not foster long-term intrinsic interest.<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A student enjoys coding and also wants a research job. Must the coding goal be classified as exclusively intrinsic or extrinsic?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

No. The activity can carry immediate enjoyment and value as a route to employment. Measure each source of motivation separately. A change in one does not imply that the other disappeared. The relevant prediction may concern persistence during practice versus commitment to obtaining the credential.

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
A supervisor offers unrestricted choice but no guidance. Why might that fail to support motivation?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Choice alone may leave the task confusing or difficult to carry out.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Choice alone may leave the task confusing or difficult to carry out. Autonomy support can coexist with clear expectations, useful feedback, and resources that support competence. The study should assess how the recipient experiences the arrangement instead of equating a large menu with effective support.

# 8.3 Targets and incentives

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Targets and incentives
<!-- @left -->
**Targets**
- Desired level and deadline
- Feasible actions
- Feedback about the gap
<!-- @right -->
**Incentive design**
- What is rewarded?
- What may be neglected?
- What happens after removal?

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Design an incentive study that can detect both short-term gains and later loss of engagement.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Assign a reward arrangement and a comparison, measure baseline interest and task performance, and follow engagement after rewards stop.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Assign a reward arrangement and a comparison, measure baseline interest and task performance, and follow engagement after rewards stop. Include quality as well as quantity. Differences after removal require comparison with the control trajectory; a simple decline from the reward period is not sufficient evidence of undermining.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A hypothetical reading program rewards the number of articles opened. Students open more articles but cannot explain them better. What changed?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

The rewarded action increased; demonstrated understanding did not improve in the observed comparison. Opening articles is an imperfect proxy for learning. Measure comprehension and sustained engagement, and test whether the incentive shifts effort toward superficial completion. The count should not be relabeled as learning.

# 8.4 Progress and persistence

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Progress and persistence
<!-- @left -->
**Link intention to action**
- Relevant situational cue
- Specific feasible response
- A plan for predictable obstacles
<!-- @right -->
**Interpret progress**
- Proximity can increase value
- Progress can license switching
- Quality can diverge from speed

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
A plan says, “I will become a better writer.” Turn it into an implementation plan without replacing the broader goal.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

For example: after the Tuesday seminar, the student will spend 25 minutes revising one argument in the library.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
For example: after the Tuesday seminar, the student will spend 25 minutes revising one argument in the library. The broad goal remains better writing; the plan identifies a cue and action. Add a feasible alternative if the library is unavailable and evaluate revision quality rather than time spent alone.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 14, 15, 16, 17.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A student has completed 8 of 10 required sections. What percentage of the original target remains, and why does that number not determine motivation?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

Two sections remain, or 20% of the original target. The next section completes half of what remains. Motivation also depends on perceived difficulty, goal commitment, and what the endpoint means. These hypothetical proportions illustrate framing; they do not establish a universal psychological response.

# 8.5 Learning from feedback

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Learning from feedback
<!-- @left -->
**Why feedback may be missed**
- Threat to self-view
- Withdrawal of effort
- Difficulty inferring the correction
<!-- @right -->
**Match the response**
- Reduce defensive processing
- Clarify the actionable lesson
- Measure the next attempt

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
An intervention improves confidence after failure. What evidence would support a learning benefit?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Measure performance on an appropriate subsequent task, preferably with a comparison condition and assessors unaware of assignment.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Measure performance on an appropriate subsequent task, preferably with a comparison condition and assessors unaware of assignment. Track use of feedback and uncertainty. Confidence can be a valuable outcome, but it cannot stand in for improved accuracy, transfer, or quality.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 17, 18, 19.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A student accepts that an answer was wrong but still cannot solve a new problem. Which barrier might remain?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

The student may lack information about the correct principle or how to transfer it. Willingness to accept criticism is not sufficient for learning. Provide a diagnostic explanation and test a new problem, while considering other barriers such as prerequisite knowledge. The result need not indicate defensiveness.

# 8.6 Conflicts and self-control

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Conflicts and self-control
<!-- @left -->
**Managing several goals**
- Compromise or alternate
- Prioritize one goal
- Distinguish substitutes from complements
<!-- @right -->
**Self-control strategies**
- Recognize the recurring conflict
- Change cues and routines
- Inhibit an activated response when needed

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
A study finds that people who wait for a larger reward later achieve better outcomes. What prevents a simple causal conclusion?
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Waiting may be associated with resources, trust in future delivery, prior learning, or other differences that also predict outcomes.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Waiting may be associated with resources, trust in future delivery, prior learning, or other differences that also predict outcomes. Randomized interventions or stronger designs are needed for causal claims. The observation does not establish that making someone wait will reproduce all long-term benefits.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 20, 21, 22, 23, 24.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

A student stops checking messages while writing after moving the phone out of the room. Is this a failure to develop self-control?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

No. Changing the situation can be an effective self-control strategy. The outcome does not require repeated inhibition of an available cue. Evaluate whether the arrangement supports the intended work and whether it is sustainable. Effortful resistance is one possible process, not the definition of successful regulation.

# 8.7 Pursuit with other people

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Pursuit with other people
<!-- @left -->
**Audience and performance**
- Task familiarity
- Evaluation pressure
- Competition and distraction
<!-- @right -->
**Shared work**
- Individual contribution
- Collective output
- Coordination versus free riding

<!-- slide main-side -->
## Evidence and interpretation
<!-- @main -->
Design a writing-group study that distinguishes social support from simply adding more working time.
<!-- @side -->
::: info
**Reasoning checkpoint**

Compare groups with matched scheduled time but different support or coordination procedures.
:::
<!-- @notes -->
Compare groups with matched scheduled time but different support or coordination procedures. Measure attendance, actual work, contribution quality, and manuscript progress. Consider clustering within groups. If the intervention also increases time, estimate that pathway explicitly rather than assuming all gains reflect interpersonal support.
Source trace: assigned chapter PDF pages 24, 25, 26, 27, 28.

<!-- slide 2col -->
## Worked example
<!-- @left -->
**Problem**

Four students each spend less time reviewing a manuscript after dividing its sections. Is this social loafing?
<!-- @right -->
**Worked reasoning**

Not necessarily. If coverage and quality improve because redundant work declines, the pattern may be efficient division of labor. Measure individual contributions and the final review. Social loafing concerns reduced effort in a collective setting; inferring it requires more than a lower time total after task redesign.

<!-- slide -->
## Pursuit with other people: key distinctions
- Others’ presence can strengthen a dominant response.
- Well-practiced and unfamiliar tasks may show different effects.
- Distinguish facilitation, coordination loss, and reduced effort (Li, n.d.).
