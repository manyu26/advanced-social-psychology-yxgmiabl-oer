## Module Summary

Most introductions to a field narrate progress: a sequence of discoveries, each improving on the last. The
anchor reading refuses that shape. Miller and Laurin organize a century of social psychology around four
**tensions** — disagreements that have attracted serious proponents on both sides, driven large bodies of
research, and never been settled. They are explicit that this is a choice of lens rather than a claim about
which debates matter most, and they warn against two temptations: *presentism*, which narrates the current
position as the final one, and American centrism, which mistakes one national tradition for the field.

That framing is what makes this the right opening module for a graduate seminar. A student who learns social
psychology as a list of effects has nowhere to put a new finding. A student who learns it as four live arguments
can locate almost any paper in the field: it is taking a position, usually implicitly, on whether the finding
generalizes, on whether the explanation belongs to the person or the situation they are embedded in, on whether
the behavior is rational, and on what the researcher owes the world outside the laboratory.

The module's second job is to make visible how much of the field's present shape came from contingent
methodological choices rather than from discoveries. The chapter's account of how the experiment came to
dominate is a case study in this: Floyd Allport narrowed the *definition* of social behavior to individual
responses to social objects, which made clean experiments feasible and, not incidentally, cut social psychology
loose from sociology's interest in large social structures. Method and subject matter were decided together.
Students who see that will read the rest of the course differently.

## Core Dependency Chain

- **Social psychology as a discipline** — the study of how the presence of others shapes thought, feeling, and action
  - This enables: the question of what *counts* as a social phenomenon, which is where Allport's restriction bites
- **The rise of the experimental method** (Allport's narrowed definition → Lewin's principles and subjective perception → Festinger's methodological rigor)
  - This enables: **universalism** as a default assumption — an experiment is worth running only if its result travels
  - This enables: the **external validity** critique, which is the narrow form of the universalism question
- **Tension 1 — Particularism versus universalism**
  - This enables: cultural psychology's challenge to assumed universality → **Module 7**
  - This enables: the generalizability thread that runs through the whole course
- **Tension 2 — Social context versus the individual**
  - This enables: social identity and group-level explanation → **Modules 4, 9, 11**
  - This enables: recognizing when an explanation has been relocated into the individual by default rather than by argument
- **Tension 3 — Rationality versus irrationality**
  - This enables: dual-process models and motivated reasoning → **Modules 3, 5, 6**
  - This enables: the accuracy-versus-bias debate about what experiments are designed to elicit
- **Tension 4 — The dispassionate scientist versus the value-driven advocate**
  - This enables: judging the fit between a policy claim and the evidence behind it → serves **CO4**
  - This enables: informed choices in the final project, particularly the OER and commentary options

## Section-Level Concept Map and Objectives

### 1.1 What social psychology studies

**Learning objectives:**

- State the field's subject matter using the affect–behavior–cognition framing.
- Explain what "social presence" covers, and why the actual, imagined and implied cases all count.
- Describe the range of models of the person the field has worked with.

**Concept flow:**

- Three things about a person: affect (what they feel), behavior (what they do), cognition (what they think)
  - Shaped by the presence of other people and the situations they create
  - The ambition is both to explain and to predict
- Allport's 1954 definition scopes the field by the presence of other human beings
  - Counted as actual, imagined, **or implied** — so a person alone but believing they may be observed is in scope
  - The field is therefore not the study of interaction but of what other minds do to a mind
- Models of the person the field has variously assumed (Baumeister's inventory)
  - Hot processes (motivation) and cold processes (cognition)
  - Information seeker · consistency seeker · situational responder · group member
  - Self-esteem maximizer · impression manager · terror manager
  - Naturally selected and cultural animal · benighted layperson · foolish mistake maker
  - These are not neutral: each model takes a side in one of the four tensions

### 1.2 A field defined by its arguments

**Learning objectives:**

- Describe social psychology's subject matter and explain why its boundaries have been contested.
- Explain what Miller and Laurin mean by a *tension* and why they chose that lens over a narrative of progress.
- Identify the two historiographic pitfalls the authors name — presentism and American centrism — and explain why each distorts a field's self-understanding.

**Concept flow:**

- History as lens, not chronicle
  - Previous Handbook histories each adopted a different lens (roots, a formative period, a central concept, enduring themes)
  - A tension = opposing positions that both attracted adherents and generated sustained research
    - Selection criterion 1: the field has taken different positions on it from the beginning to now
    - Selection criterion 2: its ebbs and flows are themselves informative about the field

### 1.3 How the experiment came to dominate

**Learning objectives:**

- Trace the three pushes that established experimentation as social psychology's central method.
- Explain how Allport's definition of social behavior and his methodological preference reinforced each other.
- Contrast Lewin's and Festinger's conceptions of what an experiment is for.

**Concept flow:**

- Starting point: Wundt approved the experiment for cognition and perception but excluded social psychology from its remit
  - Push 1 — Floyd Allport (1924): restricted social behavior to individual responses to social objects
    - Consequence: small, isolable elements replace large sociocultural forces → clean designs become feasible
    - Consequence: separation from sociology's structural concerns
  - Push 2 — Kurt Lewin: underlying principles and subjective perception; groups become studiable
    - Person and environment; genotypic process beneath phenotypic observation; systems under tension
  - Push 3 — Leon Festinger: mini-theories, single-variable manipulation, empirical over theoretical purity
    - Lewin aspired to universal mathematical theory and did not prevail; Festinger aspired to rigorous tests of relations among variables and did

### 1.4 Tension 1 — Particularism versus universalism

**Learning objectives:**

- Distinguish the broad form of the tension (do universal social psychological processes exist?) from the narrow form (what can an experiment reveal beyond its own operationalizations?).
- Define external validity and explain its relationship to claims about human universals.
- Summarize the main lines of critique aimed at the experiment's generalizability.

**Concept flow:**

- Broad form: are there invariant human social processes? — largely unaddressed in the field's early decades
- Narrow form: external validity — can a result travel beyond its specific operationalization?
  - Critique: laboratory situations are artificial and unrepresentative
  - Lewinian counterargument: the goal is abstract universal theory, not generalization to the messy world
  - Critique: experiments disregard participants' humanity and agency
  - Critique: too few operational paradigms, often not themselves validated
  - Anti-positivist critique: universal truths may not exist to be found

### 1.5 Tension 2 — Social context versus the individual

**Learning objectives:**

- Explain what is meant by the charge that social psychology became individualistic, and evaluate it.
- Describe the European tradition's case that groups are causal forces not reducible to individuals.
- Assess whether the field's recent interest in broader social contexts resolves the tension.

**Concept flow:**

- Allport's position: no psychology of groups that is not entirely a psychology of individuals
  - Steiner's critique: the study of group-level phenomena receded
  - Attitudes as a case where group-level influences were argued for
- The European response — Tajfel and Moscovici
  - Tajfel: intergroup behavior as a meta-theory
  - Moscovici: minority influence; social representations
- Person–context interdependence, in two versions
  - A recent expansion of interest into broader contexts, driven partly by methodological innovation and partly by a shift from processes to phenomena

### 1.6 Tension 3 — Rationality versus irrationality

**Learning objectives:**

- Trace the movement from early claims about crowd irrationality to contemporary emphasis on the rationality of underlying processes.
- Distinguish irrational *outcomes* from irrational *processes*.
- Evaluate the arguments that apparent bias is an artifact of how experiments are designed.

**Concept flow:**

- Early position: crowds as irrational, as agents of decivilization
  - Rationalist counterarguments challenge both the outcomes and the processes claimed
- Conformity: suggestion, prestige, the voice of the herd
- Bias research shifts emphasis to the rationality of *processes* even where outcomes look biased
  - Motivated reasoning meets the computer metaphor
  - Reconciliation attempts: motivation as director, cognition as executor; dual-process models
- Newer arguments for rational outcomes
  - Bias versus accuracy in perception
  - Bias as an artifact of experiments designed to elicit it
  - Survival, not accuracy, as the relevant criterion

### 1.7 Tension 4 — The dispassionate scientist versus the value-driven advocate

**Learning objectives:**

- Distinguish the three forms of the values debate the chapter identifies.
- Explain why philosophers of science have questioned whether value-free science is possible.
- Take and defend a position on whether social psychologists should advocate for specific policies.

**Concept flow:**

- What counts as basic science, what counts as relevant research
  - Action research: solving a specific community's concrete problem collaboratively
  - Concept-based intervention research: one concept applied across many domains
- Three forms of the values debate
  - How researchers' values shape which questions get asked (recent focus on political orientation)
  - Whether advocacy is faithful to the strength of the underlying science
  - Whether advocacy by psychologists or their organizations is appropriate at all
- Value neutrality examined
  - Weber: social science is value-relevant, but should be free of value judgment
  - McMullin: the standards for judging a theory are themselves value-laden — the Bohr–Einstein disagreement over predictive accuracy versus consistency
  - Reicher and Haslam: ethics review requires weighing societal benefit against participant harm, which is a value judgment that shapes what can be discovered

### 1.8 The other lens: themes and pillars

**Learning objectives:**

- Describe the three central themes and four pillars offered by the previous Handbook edition's history.
- Compare that lens with the four-tensions lens.
- Say what each makes visible that the other does not.

**Concept flow:**

- Miller and Laurin chose tensions partly because Ross, Lepper and Ward had already taken up enduring themes
- Three central themes
  - Norms and situational influence — what people do in groups that they never do alone; group norms, status, roles; social exchange, group risk tolerance, social facilitation, groupthink, deindividuation, jury deliberation; later culture, intergroup relations, self-construal, implicit bias, stereotype threat
  - Subjectivity and construal — against the behaviorist view that everyone confronts one objective reality; situations are interpreted; the prisoner's dilemma changes with framing; causal inference is itself a subjective act
  - Non-obviousness — findings contrary to expectation; drinking norms, proximity and attraction, the smoke-filled room, foot-in-the-door; justified because non-obvious results reveal a change in the situation's subjective meaning for the actor
- Four pillars
  - Naïve realism — believing we see the world objectively and that dissenters must be uninformed, irrational or biased
  - Lay dispositionism — over-attribution to stable traits; the fundamental attribution error; false confession and eyewitness cases; even social psychologists underestimate situational power; a self-serving asymmetry
    - Experience fails to correct it: people choose situations, which then constrain behavior, so person and situation are confounded in everything observed
  - Overconfidence — self-serving bias, downward comparison, positive illusion
  - Confirmation bias — including self-fulfilling prophecy
- The relation between the lenses
  - Tensions show the field as unresolved argument; themes and pillars show it as accumulated insight
  - Not rivals: the pillars are positions **inside** the tensions — lay dispositionism is the person/situation question made an object of study; naïve realism and confirmation bias are findings about rationality

## Common Student Bottlenecks

| Bottleneck | Conceptual repair |
|---|---|
| Reading "tension" as a problem the field should have fixed by now | A tension persists because both positions capture something real. Ask what each side would lose if it simply conceded. |
| Assuming the experiment was adopted because it was obviously correct | Revisit Wundt's exclusion of social psychology and Allport's redefinition: the method's dominance required narrowing the subject matter first. |
| Collapsing external validity into "was the sample representative?" | Separate the narrow question (does the result travel beyond this operationalization?) from the broad one (are there universal processes at all?). |
| Treating rationality and irrationality as a verdict on human nature | Distinguish outcomes from processes: a biased outcome can be produced by a well-adapted process. |
| Hearing "science is value-laden" as "science is merely opinion" | The claim is about which questions get asked and which standards get weighted — not that evidence fails to constrain conclusions. |
