## Module Summary

Module 1 asked whether social psychology's findings can travel. Module 2 supplies the evidence, and it is
uncomfortable. The anchor chapter's framing choice matters as much as its content: Giner-Sorolla declines to
treat pre-2010 researchers as lazy or dishonest, arguing instead that most genuinely aligned their idea of
good research with the system they worked in — and that the new standards are not themselves beyond
question. That posture is what makes the chapter teachable. A student who leaves believing the old guard was
careless has learned the wrong lesson.

The chapter locates the present moment as the fourth of four methodological transformations in the field's
short life, and takes 2011 as the hinge. A nine-experiment paper claiming evidence for precognition appeared
in the field's highest-impact empirical journal, structurally indistinguishable from any other paper: a novel
claim, eight significant studies, one marginal. If the rules could certify that, the rules were the problem.
What followed was not one reform but eight distinct proposals, each with its own rationale, its own uptake,
and — the chapter is careful here — its own costs.

Two further sections widen the frame beyond statistics. **Measurement and manipulation** asks whether the
field knows what its instruments measure, taking up questionable measurement practices and the problem of
inferring that a manipulation worked from the fact that the result came out. **Generalizability** asks who
the field's findings are about, and shows that the problem is structural rather than incidental: an implicit
hierarchy of nations and populations shapes which research must justify its scope and which is presumed
universal by default. That section connects directly back to Module 1's universalism tension and forward to
Module 7.

## Core Dependency Chain

- **The reporting system circa 2010** — a series of studies supporting a hypothesis, evaluated by shared norms under competition for scarce journal space
  - This enables: understanding why the incentives produced what they produced, without attributing bad faith
- **The 2011 collision and crisis talk**
  - This enables: recognizing that the challenge was to the *rules of evidence*, not to one paper
  - This enables: healthy skepticism about crisis narratives, which the chapter notes recur across the field's history
- **Eight proposed evidence solutions** (effect sizes → null results within and across articles → direct replication → power → full disclosure → pre-registration → theory)
  - This enables: evaluating any paper's evidential claims against a specific standard rather than a vague sense of rigor
  - This enables: the costs-and-limitations habit — every solution buys something and spends something
- **Measurement and manipulation validity**
  - This enables: asking what an instrument measures before asking whether an effect is real → **Modules 3–12**
  - This enables: distinguishing a null result from a failed manipulation
- **Generalizability and WEIRD samples**
  - This enables: asking who a finding is about → **Module 7**
  - This enables: seeing the asymmetry in which research is asked to justify its scope

## Section-Level Concept Map and Objectives

### 2.1 The system of research and reporting, circa 2010

**Learning objectives:**

- Describe the pre-2010 publication model and the evaluative norms researchers were meeting.
- Explain the tension between simple consistent rules and complex nuanced judgments.
- Articulate why the chapter declines to attribute the old system's failures to laziness or dishonesty.

**Concept flow:**

- Four methodological transformations: the turn to experimentalism around 1910; Allport's individual-based social psychology in the 1920s; the relevance and ethics challenges of the late 1960s–70s; and doubts about single-study articles resting on p < .05
- The publication system judges both idea value and evidence strength
  - Rules that are easy to apply consistently but may not capture the construct
  - Judgments that promise nuance at the cost of disagreement
- Competitive pressure: prestigious journals accepting no more than about 20% of submissions
- The interpretive stance: researchers aligned their standards to the system; the new standards are also open to question

### 2.2 The 2011 collision

**Learning objectives:**

- Explain why a single paper could destabilize confidence in the field's evidential standards.
- Describe what made that paper structurally ordinary.
- Evaluate crisis narratives with appropriate historical perspective.

**Concept flow:**

- A nine-experiment precognition paper in the field's highest-impact empirical journal
  - Structurally like any other paper: novel claim, eight significant studies, one marginal at p = .098
  - Included state-of-the-art reaction-time priming methods and variations showing replication and generalization
  - Yet the conclusion would overturn accepted understanding in psychology, neurobiology, and physics
- Immediate critiques targeted the standards, not merely the result
- "Crisis" talk spreads to psychology and to science at large
  - Historical caution: amid the crisis of 1977, an article recalled the crisis of 1927
  - Evidence that publicized low replication rates can damage public trust

### 2.3 Eight proposed solutions

**Learning objectives:**

- State each solution, the problem it addresses, and its principal cost.
- Group the eight by the failure mode each targets.
- Explain why reporting a statistic is not the same as having a standard for it.

**Concept flow:**

- Solutions 1–3, what enters the literature
  - Report effect sizes — answer "by how much?", enable meta-analysis, correct misreadings of p
    - But: reporting is not a threshold; rejecting low effect sizes would be its own error
  - Report null studies within an article — requires deciding the study package before, not after, the research
  - Publish more articles with null conclusions — viable where theory is solid, methods independently validated, and analysis can support the null
- Solutions 4–5, whether findings hold
  - Report independent direct replication
  - Increase statistical power — sample size affects the false-positive risk, but so does the hypothesis's prior likelihood
- Solutions 6–7, what researchers reveal and when
  - Require full disclosure — open data and disclosure statements; on-demand data sharing tends to protect researchers over verification
  - Enable pre-registration, Registered Reports, and honest exploration
- Solution 8, what the evidence is for
  - Strengthen theory — tighter constructs, better-established conceptual priors, validation research and description alongside confirmation
- Every solution has an Uptake and a Costs-and-Limitations section: reform is a trade

### 2.4 Manipulation and measurement

**Learning objectives:**

- Explain why theory quality and method quality are the same problem viewed twice.
- Describe questionable measurement practices and their consequences.
- Explain what a manipulation check can and cannot establish.

**Concept flow:**

- "There is nothing so theoretical as a good method" — specify which parts of a method are essential to the theory and which are incidental
- Meta-analysis across discrepant methodologies yields effect sizes no more meaningful than the average weight of all the animals in a zoo
- Questionable measurement practices: undisclosed flexibility in measurement decisions, poor reliability, unvalidated scales
  - Psychometric concerns routinely ignored in research and publishing
  - Construct validity deficits addressed by mapping nomological networks
- Manipulation rigor
  - A positive result has usually been taken as sufficient evidence that the manipulation worked
  - The problem sharpens when replication fails or a Registered Report may publish a null: is it a true near-zero effect or a failed setup?
  - Manipulation checks help — but same-session measures may not establish validity, and self-report checks can alert participants to the variable

### 2.5 Challenges to generalizability

**Learning objectives:**

- Explain what WEIRD names and what the acronym has been criticized for.
- Describe the structural mechanisms producing generalizability bias.
- Explain how implicit claims of generality operate in ordinary writing.

**Concept flow:**

- Long-standing doubts: student samples from the 1950s–60s onward; laboratory work glorified over real-world relevance; self-report favored over observed behavior
- WEIRD — Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich Democracies — focuses attention on cultural limitations, though its scope has been criticized
- A center–periphery structure, with rural and non-agrarian populations understudied even within countries
- Mechanisms and consequences
  - Researchers, editors and participants drawn disproportionately from central populations
  - Central-population research treated as normal and automatically generalizable; peripheral groups labeled as special cases
  - Bibliometric evidence that central-sample studies less often signal their origin in the title, and that peripheral-sample work is judged less important
  - Reported discrimination against researchers in peripheral zones, including demands for validation that central-zone research is not asked for
- Implicit generality: few articles claim to describe all humankind, but findings described without scope qualifiers are routinely linked to "people"

## Common Student Bottlenecks

| Bottleneck | Conceptual repair |
|---|---|
| Reading the chapter as an indictment of earlier researchers | Return to the chapter's stated stance: researchers aligned to the system they were in, and the new standards are also open to question. |
| Treating the eight solutions as a checklist of virtues | Every one has a Costs and Limitations section. Ask what each spends, not just what it buys. |
| Confusing reporting a statistic with having a standard for it | Effect-size reporting is straightforward to enforce; setting effect-size thresholds for publication is a separate and rejected proposal. |
| Reading a failed replication as proof the effect is not real | Distinguish a true near-zero effect from a manipulation that did not take — which is exactly why manipulation validity matters. |
| Treating WEIRD as a sampling footnote | The chapter's evidence is structural: who does research, who is studied, whose work must justify its scope. |
| Concluding that nothing in the field can be trusted | The same methods that revealed these problems are being used to fix them. Ask which specific claim is weak and why. |
