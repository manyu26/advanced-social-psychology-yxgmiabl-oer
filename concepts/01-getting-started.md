# Course Concept Map: Advanced Social Psychology (PSYC 538)

## Source and Scope

- **Course:** PSYC 538 — Advanced Social Psychology, master's level, seminar format.
- **Foundational source:** *The Handbook of Social Psychology*, 6th edition (D. T. Gilbert, S. T. Fiske,
  E. J. Finkel, & W. B. Mendes, Eds., 2025, Situational Press, https://doi.org/10.70400/NYKH3013),
  open access under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. One Handbook chapter anchors each module. The Handbook is used as a
  **reference** — its facts and findings inform this material; none of its expression is adapted.
- **Additional readings:** primary empirical and theoretical articles assigned per module. These are
  **cited**, never reproduced.
- **Scope:** twelve modules across three units. Module ordering follows the course schedule.
- **Format:** simple Markdown only — no diagrams, images, or plugin syntax, so this file renders anywhere.
- **Package license:** CC-BY-SA-4.0.

## Course Summary

Social psychology asks how the actual, imagined, or implied presence of other people shapes what a person
thinks, feels, and does. This course treats that question at the level a researcher works at: not a survey
of findings, but an examination of the theories that organize them, the methods that produce them, and the
disputes that are still open. The seminar format is doing real work here — the material is arranged so that
students arrive having read primary sources and leave having argued about them.

The course is built on one structural idea: **methodology is not a preliminary, it is a lens that stays on
for the whole semester.** Unit 1 establishes both the field's enduring tensions — individual versus
situation, description versus intervention, the laboratory versus the world — and the credibility questions
raised by replication and measurement work of the last decade. Every later module is then read through that
lens. When students meet an effect in Module 5 or Module 11, the question "how do we know this, and how
well?" is already installed.

Units 2 and 3 divide the field along the line the discipline itself uses. **Unit 2 (Attitudes and Social
Cognition)** works inward: the mental machinery a person brings to social life — how they perceive and
explain others, construct a self, hold and change attitudes, feel, are shaped by culture, and pursue goals.
**Unit 3 (Interpersonal Relations and Group Processes)** works outward: what happens between people and
between groups — influence, attachment and rejection, intergroup hostility, and the conditions under which
cooperation beats conflict. The two halves are not independent, and the course is sequenced so the
dependency is visible: intergroup relations in Module 11 is unintelligible without the categorization and
self-concept work in Modules 3 and 4, and cooperation in Module 12 draws on the goals and motivation
framework from Module 8.

## Course Learning Objectives

Mapped from the course objectives on the syllabus:

- **CO1** — Describe both classic and current trends in social psychological theory and research.
- **CO2** — Explain how humans think, feel, and act in social situations.
- **CO3** — Apply theories and research to the student's own research goals and topics.
- **CO4** — Analyze and comment on existing research in a way that reflects scientific and critical thinking.

## Unit Structure

| Unit | Modules | Central question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Introduction to the Science of Social Psychology | 1–2 | What kind of science is this, and how far can we trust its findings? |
| 2 — Attitudes and Social Cognition | 3–8 | How does the individual mind understand, evaluate, and respond to the social world? |
| 3 — Interpersonal Relations and Group Processes | 9–12 | What happens between people and between groups? |

## Core Dependency Chain

- **Module 1 — Introduction to Social Psychology** (subject matter; a five-period history; the field's enduring tensions; a second historiographic lens)
  - This enables: a working definition of the field, including why imagined and implied social presence count
  - This enables: a frame for locating any later theory in the field's recurring debates
  - This enables: recognizing that today's critiques are old — the 1970s objections are the 2010s objections
  - This enables: Module 2, by raising the question of what social psychology's evidence is worth
- **Module 2 — Research Issues** (replication, measurement validity, construct validation, method bias)
  - This enables: critical reading of every empirical claim in Modules 3–12 → directly serves CO4
  - This enables: the final project's commentary, proposal, and pre-registration options
- **Module 3 — Social Cognition, Impression Formation, and Attribution** (how people perceive and explain others)
  - This enables: Module 4 — the self is itself a target of perception and attribution
  - This enables: Module 11 — stereotyping is social cognition applied to categories
- **Module 4 — The Social Self and Self-Concept** (self-concept, identity, social identity)
  - This enables: Module 7 — cultural variation is partly variation in the construal of self
  - This enables: Module 9 and Module 11 — social identity underwrites group influence and intergroup behavior
- **Module 5 — Attitudes** (structure, function, formation, change, attitude–behavior relations)
  - This enables: Module 9 — persuasion and influence are attitude change in a social setting
  - This enables: Module 11 — prejudice is, in part, an attitude toward a group
- **Module 6 — Emotion and Cognition** (emotion's interplay with judgment and social experience)
  - This enables: Module 10 — relationships are affectively organized
  - This enables: Module 12 — conflict and cooperation are emotionally driven as well as calculated
- **Module 7 — Cultural and Cross-Cultural Psychology** (cultural variation, generalizability)
  - This enables: a standing challenge to every effect met in Modules 3–6 → reinforces CO4
  - This enables: Module 11 and Module 12 — culture shapes intergroup and cooperative norms
- **Module 8 — Goals and Motivations** (goal pursuit, self-regulation)
  - This enables: Module 12 — cooperation is a problem of aligning goals across people
  - This enables: Module 9 — behavior change interventions are motivational interventions
- **Module 9 — Group Processes and Social Influence** (influence, norms, status, behavior change)
  - This enables: Module 11 — intergroup dynamics are group processes across a category boundary
  - This enables: Module 12 — cooperation and conflict occur within and between groups
- **Module 10 — Attraction, Rejection, and Close Relationships** (attraction, rejection, relationship processes)
  - This enables: Module 12 — the dyad is the smallest arena for cooperation and conflict
- **Module 11 — Intergroup Relations** (prejudice, stereotypes, discrimination, contact, allyship)
  - This enables: Module 12 — intergroup conflict is the large-scale case of the cooperation problem
- **Module 12 — Cooperation and Conflict** (prosociality, moral expansiveness, aggression)
  - This enables: synthesis — the course's closing integration across all three units

## Logical Order for Teaching

| Order | Introduce | Reason for placement |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | History and enduring tensions | Gives students a map of the field's recurring debates before any specific theory, so later material has somewhere to attach. |
| 2 | Research issues and credibility | Installs the critical lens early; every subsequent reading is then evaluated, not just absorbed. Serves CO4 from week two onward. |
| 3 | Social cognition and attribution | The most basic inward process — how one mind reads another. Everything in Unit 2 builds on perception and explanation. |
| 4 | The social self | Turns perception on the perceiver; introduces identity, which Units 2 and 3 both require. |
| 5 | Attitudes | Evaluation follows perception and self; sets up persuasion and prejudice later. |
| 6 | Emotion and cognition | Adds affect to the cognitive account built so far, correcting an overly cold model. |
| 7 | Culture | Placed after the core inward processes so students can ask whether each one generalizes — a natural CO4 exercise. |
| 8 | Goals and motivation | Completes Unit 2 by adding the directional, motivated element, and bridges to behavior change in Unit 3. |
| 9 | Group processes and social influence | Opens Unit 3 by moving from one mind to many; draws on attitudes and identity already in place. |
| 10 | Close relationships | Narrows to the dyad, the most intensively studied interpersonal context. |
| 11 | Intergroup relations | Requires categorization (M3), identity (M4), and attitudes (M5); placed once all three are available. |
| 12 | Cooperation and conflict | The integrative capstone, drawing on motivation, groups, relationships, and intergroup processes. |

## Cross-Cutting Threads

Four threads recur across modules and are worth naming explicitly for students, since they are the
strongest source of exam and discussion questions that span the semester:

| Thread | Where it appears | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Methodological credibility | M2 anchors it; revisited in every module | Distinguishes a robust effect from a fragile one; the backbone of CO4. |
| Person and situation | M1 raises it; M3, M8, M9 develop it | The field's oldest tension; a standing question about where explanation belongs. |
| Identity and categorization | M4 anchors it; M7, M9, M11 develop it | The single most portable construct in the course. |
| Generalizability across contexts | M7 anchors it; M11, M12 test it | Asks whether a finding describes people or describes a sample. |

## Common Course-Level Bottlenecks

| Bottleneck | Conceptual repair |
|---|---|
| Treating the replication crisis as a reason to dismiss the field | Reframe as calibration, not demolition: the same methods that revealed the problem are the ones being used to fix it. Ask which specific claim is weak and why. |
| Reading primary articles for conclusions only | Require the pre-seminar reflection to state the design and the inference it licenses, not just the finding. |
| Confusing a theory with an effect | Insist on the distinction in discussion: an effect is an observation, a theory explains a family of them and predicts new ones. |
| Treating culture as a moderator to mention at the end | Place the generalizability question inside each module rather than only in Module 7. |
| Importing undergraduate summaries of classic studies | Return to the primary source; several canonical studies are more limited than their textbook retellings suggest. |
