## Chapter summary

An evaluation can be favorable yet unstable, mixed yet consequential, or persuasive without changing behavior. Begin by specifying what is being evaluated and how it is measured; then ask what supports the evaluation, how it changes, and when it guides action.

## Section-level concept map and objectives

### 5.1 Representing an evaluation

**Learning objectives:**

- **5.1a:** Distinguish attitude valence, evaluative bases, and ambivalence.

**Concept flow:** Object: the particular policy; Valence: favorable or unfavorable; Basis: feeling, belief, or experience; also Positive and negative ratings; Experienced conflict; A midpoint can hide different states.

### 5.2 Functions and behavior

**Learning objectives:**

- **5.2a:** Evaluate attitude–behavior correspondence using matched targets and contexts.

**Concept flow:** Guide approach or avoidance; Express values and identity; Organize relevant information; also Target or time mismatch; Opportunity and resources; Habits and competing goals.

### 5.3 Strength beyond extremity

**Learning objectives:**

- **5.3a:** Separate defining consequences of attitude strength from its predictors.

**Concept flow:** Accessibility and importance; Certainty and knowledge; Moralization and ambivalence; also Persistence over time; Resistance to a challenge; Effects on judgments or behavior.

### 5.4 Learning evaluations

**Learning objectives:**

- **5.4a:** Compare exposure, evaluative pairing, and inference-based learning.

**Concept flow:** Repeat the target; Pair it with valenced material; Provide evidence about attributes; also Familiarity or fluency; Learned evaluative relations; Interpretation of what pairing means.

### 5.5 Reflective change

**Learning objectives:**

- **5.5a:** Distinguish dissonance, self-perception, and correction accounts.

**Concept flow:** Dissonance: reduce inconsistency; Self-perception: infer from conduct; Correction: adjust a suspected influence; also Strength of prior attitude; Perceived choice and justification; Discomfort and perceived bias.

### 5.6 Persuasion as a process

**Learning objectives:**

- **5.6a:** Use elaboration and thought confidence to generate conditional persuasion predictions.

**Concept flow:** Motivation and ability; Argument quality; Expertise can serve several roles; also Thought direction; Confidence in those thoughts; Greater confidence can amplify rejection.

### 5.7 Receptiveness and advocacy

**Learning objectives:**

- **5.7a:** Separate openness, attitude change, and advocacy effectiveness.

**Concept flow:** Willingness to listen; Questions and sustained discussion; Perceived threat to autonomy; also Attitude direction and certainty; Decision to advocate; Effect on the recipient.

## Common student bottlenecks

| Bottleneck | Conceptual repair |
|---|---|
| Representing an evaluation: interpreting an outcome as a unique mechanism | B. Opposing evaluations coexist in the measured representation. |
| Functions and behavior: interpreting an outcome as a unique mechanism | No. The reported intention increased by 20 scale points; attendance did not change in the observed comparison. |
| Strength beyond extremity: interpreting an outcome as a unique mechanism | Measure a proposed predictor, such as baseline certainty, independently of the persuasion outcome. |
| Learning evaluations: interpreting an outcome as a unique mechanism | Not from that association alone. |
| Reflective change: interpreting an outcome as a unique mechanism | No. The reviewer may have corrected in the intended direction while overshooting. |
| Persuasion as a process: interpreting an outcome as a unique mechanism | Agreement could reflect a shortcut based on credentials, or evaluation of the expert’s relevant technical reasoning. |
| Receptiveness and advocacy: interpreting an outcome as a unique mechanism | Randomly assign recipients to messages produced under the advocacy intervention or an appropriate comparison. |
