# Module 5 Practice: Attitudes

::: info
Prepare a response before selecting **Answer**. Each section pairs a reinforcement problem with an interpretation or transfer problem. The worked responses model reasoning rather than a single required opinion. Scenarios and numbers are hypothetical teaching examples. Scientific basis: Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::

## Learning objectives{{attrs[#blk-2npdzx2fdz0e]}}

- **5.1a:** Distinguish attitude valence, evaluative bases, and ambivalence.
- **5.2a:** Evaluate attitude–behavior correspondence using matched targets and contexts.
- **5.3a:** Separate defining consequences of attitude strength from its predictors.
- **5.4a:** Compare exposure, evaluative pairing, and inference-based learning.
- **5.5a:** Distinguish dissonance, self-perception, and correction accounts.
- **5.6a:** Use elaboration and thought confidence to generate conditional persuasion predictions.
- **5.7a:** Separate openness, attitude change, and advocacy effectiveness.

## Question 1 · Objective 5.1a{{attrs[#blk-37gly3qdva07]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 1
Two students both choose the midpoint on a policy scale. One sees few consequences; the other sees substantial benefits and substantial costs. What should the next measure distinguish?
:::
::: tab Answer
Collect separate positive and negative ratings and a report of felt conflict. The first student may be indifferent; the second may be ambivalent. A midpoint alone cannot distinguish them. Do not diagnose ambivalence solely from delayed responding, because uncertainty or difficulty understanding the question can also slow a response.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 2 · Objective 5.1a{{attrs[#blk-tz6qm815qshi]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 2
Which conclusion follows from a high positive rating and a high negative rating? A. The person is indifferent. B. Opposing evaluations coexist. C. Behavior cannot be predicted. D. The attitude is inaccurate.
:::
::: tab Answer
B. Opposing evaluations coexist in the measured representation. Whether the person experiences conflict, whether the evaluation guides behavior, and whether its factual basis is accurate remain separate questions. The other choices turn a structural observation into an unsupported conclusion.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 3 · Objective 5.2a{{attrs[#blk-k13wd2sobq9j]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 3
A survey measures support for public transportation. The outcome is whether a student took an infrequent bus to campus yesterday. What would make the test more informative?
:::
::: tab Answer
Measure evaluation of that journey and document schedule, access, weather, and competing travel needs. Alternatively, retain the broad evaluation and aggregate multiple transport choices. A failure to predict one constrained trip does not show that evaluations are irrelevant. Matched measures improve the interpretation without eliminating contextual constraints.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 4 · Objective 5.2a{{attrs[#blk-e4k1vp7pa99b]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 4
An imagined campaign raises stated intention from 40 to 60 on a 100-point scale, while observed attendance stays at 30%. Can the campaign be described as changing behavior?
:::
::: tab Answer
No. The reported intention increased by 20 scale points; attendance did not change in the observed comparison. Establish uncertainty and assignment before interpreting effectiveness. Investigate access or implementation barriers, but treat those as hypotheses until measured. A scale-point change is not a percentage-point change in attendance.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 5 · Objective 5.3a{{attrs[#blk-2n5w4ctwu04j]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 5
A participant selects the most favorable scale endpoint but changes position after one weak message. Which strength claim has been tested?
:::
::: tab Answer
The endpoint shows extremity; the subsequent change provides evidence about resistance under that particular challenge. It does not support treating extremity as sufficient proof of strength. Assess persistence and behavioral impact separately, and check whether the message was actually weak for this participant rather than only for the researcher.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 6 · Objective 5.3a{{attrs[#blk-tyfobwhfryt8]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 6
A study defines strong attitudes as attitudes that resist persuasion, then concludes that strong attitudes resist persuasion. Repair the design.
:::
::: tab Answer
Measure a proposed predictor, such as baseline certainty, independently of the persuasion outcome. Randomize a relevant message or comparison, then assess change and retention. The original claim restates its definition; the revised design tests whether an independently measured or manipulated factor predicts a strength consequence.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 7 · Objective 5.4a{{attrs[#blk-ondve456f31r]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 7
A fictional badge appears ten times beside pleasant scenes. Liking rises. Which comparison separates repeated exposure from positive pairing?
:::
::: tab Answer
Compare equally repeated badges paired with neutral versus pleasant scenes, with baseline liking and presentation conditions matched. A no-exposure condition can estimate the contribution of repetition. The result identifies a procedural contrast; additional measures or manipulations are needed to distinguish association, fluency, and propositional interpretation.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 8 · Objective 5.4a{{attrs[#blk-ohqdl9svqql3]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 8
Participants choose which reviews to read. Their later attitudes become more extreme. Is selective reading a demonstrated cause?
:::
::: tab Answer
Not from that association alone. Initial attitudes may influence both selection and later reports, and participants may encounter other information. Randomly varying access or assigned reviews can test an information effect. Preserve a separate choice condition if the research question concerns how people create their own evidence environments.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 9 · Objective 5.5a{{attrs[#blk-348p9xknugv8]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 9
A student voluntarily writes in support of an unfamiliar policy and later reports liking it. Why is dissonance not the only explanation?
:::
::: tab Answer
The student might infer support from voluntarily writing the essay, learn new arguments while composing it, or experience inconsistency and resolve it. Assess initial attitude strength, perceived choice, external justification, discomfort, and message learning. An attitude shift alone does not identify which process produced it.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 10 · Objective 5.5a{{attrs[#blk-piha5sn28it6]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 10
A reviewer rejects a paper more harshly after realizing its author is a friend. Does that establish an unbiased correction?
:::
::: tab Answer
No. The reviewer may have corrected in the intended direction while overshooting. Compare judgments with a defensible criterion or blinded evaluation. Awareness of a possible friendship bias establishes a reason for correction, not the amount needed for accuracy. The same caution applies to interpreting changes in attitude certainty.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 11 · Objective 5.6a{{attrs[#blk-tuqkppzjvh06]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 11
Recipients generate mostly negative thoughts about a message. A later manipulation increases confidence in those thoughts. What does a self-validation account predict?
:::
::: tab Answer
Greater reliance on the negative thoughts can reduce persuasion. The account does not predict that confidence always helps the communicator. Check the timing of the manipulation and distinguish confidence in one’s thoughts from confidence in the source. Measure thought direction and evaluate the proposed interaction rather than only an overall main effect.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 12 · Objective 5.6a{{attrs[#blk-29nk61ds09q7]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 12
An expert endorses a technically complex method. Give two explanations for agreement that require different evidence.
:::
::: tab Answer
Agreement could reflect a shortcut based on credentials, or evaluation of the expert’s relevant technical reasoning. Vary argument quality and opportunities for scrutiny, and assess thought content. Credentials alone cannot identify the process. Expertise might also alter attention or thought confidence, so the design should specify the contrast it can actually distinguish.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 13 · Objective 5.7a{{attrs[#blk-dplgvafwxzwg]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 13
A listening workshop increases willingness to discuss a divisive issue but leaves policy evaluations unchanged. Did it fail?
:::
::: tab Answer
The answer depends on the preregistered target. It may have improved receptiveness while showing no detected attitude change. Report these separately with uncertainty. If persuasion was the target, the workshop has not demonstrated it. If constructive dialogue was the target, stable disagreement does not erase an engagement benefit.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 14 · Objective 5.7a{{attrs[#blk-6mcywpzeaxcj]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 14
Design a follow-up that tests whether increased advocacy actually changes another person’s view.
:::
::: tab Answer
Randomly assign recipients to messages produced under the advocacy intervention or an appropriate comparison. Measure baseline and later evaluations, perceived listening, and message exposure. Account for clustering by advocate. More messages sent is an upstream behavior; only recipient outcomes test their persuasive effect.

**Source basis:** Tormala & Rucker (2025).
:::
::::
