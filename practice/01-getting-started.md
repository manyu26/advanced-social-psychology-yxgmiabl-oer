## Section 1.1 — What social psychology studies{{attrs[#blk-5om4ks3r2mtf]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 1
**Objective 1.1a.** Allport's 1954 definition counts the presence of other people as *actual, imagined, or
implied*. Give a case turning on each of the last two, and say what the field would lose if the definition
covered only actual presence.
:::
::: tab Answer
**Imagined presence.** A researcher rehearsing a talk alone in an empty room, whose delivery changes according
to which audience they picture — a friendly seminar or a hostile review panel. No one is present or expected;
the audience exists only as a representation, and it is doing the causal work.

**Implied presence.** A person alone in a laboratory cubicle who believes their responses may later be seen by
someone. Nobody is there and nobody is imagined in particular, but a *possible* observer changes the behavior.
Cues suffice — a camera, a mirror, a note that responses will be reviewed.

**What would be lost under an actual-presence-only definition.** A great deal, and this is the point of the
question.

*Most of the self literature.* Self-presentation, impression management and objective self-awareness all turn
on an audience that is anticipated rather than present.

*Most of attitudes and norms.* A norm operates precisely when nobody is watching; that is what distinguishes an
internalized norm from compliance.

*Stereotyping and prejudice as usually studied.* Judgments about a group are made about people not in the room.

*Much of the field's laboratory work.* Participants respond alone to described or depicted others — under a
narrow definition, that would not be social psychology at all.

**The general point.** The field is not the study of interaction. It is the study of what other minds do to a
mind, including when those minds are absent, hypothetical, or merely possible. That breadth is what makes
social psychology a psychology rather than a sociology, and it is a scope decision — one that Floyd Allport's
1924 restriction, discussed in §1.3, pushed in the opposite direction.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 2
**Objective 1.1a.** Take two models of the person from Baumeister's inventory that are hardest to hold at the
same time. Explain the conflict, and say which tension it belongs to.
:::
::: tab Answer
**A strong pair: the situational responder and the consistency seeker.**

*The situational responder* is moved by the immediate situation. On this model, behavior is best predicted by
knowing the circumstances, and apparent stability is largely an artifact of stable circumstances.

*The consistency seeker* carries an internal drive to keep beliefs, attitudes and actions aligned, and will
change one to fit another when they conflict. Cognitive dissonance is the paradigm case.

**The conflict.** They locate the engine in different places. If situations drive behavior, consistency across
situations is not something a person *achieves* but something the environment supplies. If people actively
pursue consistency, then behavior carries a person-level signature that persists across situations — exactly
what the situational account denies. Held together loosely, they are compatible: a person seeks consistency
*within* the interpretation the situation affords. But the dissonance tradition and the situationist tradition
have historically explained the same findings in incompatible ways, which is the sign of a real conflict rather
than a division of labor.

**Which tension.** This is **Tension 2, social context versus the individual** — indeed it is that tension
restated as a claim about what a person fundamentally is. Note that it also touches Tension 3: the consistency
seeker's drive produces demonstrably irrational outcomes (justifying an action after the fact), so accepting
that model commits you to a position on rationality as well.

**A second defensible pair** is the *information seeker* and the *foolish mistake maker*. One casts the person
as motivated toward accurate understanding; the other treats systematic error as the field's central
phenomenon. That pair belongs to Tension 3, and the outcome/process distinction from §1.6 is exactly the move
that lets both be partly right.
:::

## Section 1.2 — A field defined by its arguments{{attrs[#blk-1skerehzd1lm]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 3
**Objective 1.2a.** The person–situation debate in personality and social psychology asks whether behavior is
better predicted by stable traits or by features of the situation. Does it qualify as an *enduring tension* by
Miller and Laurin's two criteria? Justify your verdict against each criterion separately.
:::
::: tab Answer
It qualifies on both criteria, and the case should be made criterion by criterion.

**Criterion 1 — differing positions from the field's inception to the present.** The debate is not a
historical episode that closed. Allport's restriction of social psychology to individual responses to social
objects already presupposes a position on where explanation belongs, and the question is live in contemporary
work whenever a study must decide whether to model a person-level or context-level predictor. Both sides have
attracted serious proponents throughout.

**Criterion 2 — an instructive ebb and flow.** The pattern is itself informative: periods emphasizing
situational power and periods of renewed interest in individual differences track changes in available
methods and in what the field considered worth explaining. That is exactly the diagnostic Miller and Laurin
say a tension's history provides — a window into the intellectual development and sociology of the field.

**A caution worth adding.** Qualifying does not make it one of *their* four. The authors are explicit that
their selection is incomplete and arguably arbitrary, and that others could reasonably be nominated. The
verdict is "yes, by these criteria," not "yes, they should have included it."
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 4
**Objective 1.2a.** Take one of the four tensions. State what each side would *lose* if it simply conceded to
the other. Explain why this exercise is a better test of understanding than summarizing the two positions.
:::
::: tab Answer
Using **rationality versus irrationality**:

**If the irrationality side conceded entirely**, the field would lose its capacity to explain the phenomena
that motivated the research in the first place — systematic, predictable departures from what a well-calibrated
judgment would produce. Declaring every process rational leaves the observed patterns unexplained, or explains
them only by redescribing them as adaptive after the fact.

**If the rationality side conceded entirely**, the field would lose the constraint that keeps bias research
honest. Without it, any deviation from a normative standard counts as evidence of a defective mind, and there
is no pressure to ask whether the standard was appropriate, whether the paradigm manufactured the deviation,
or whether the process is well tuned to an environment the task does not resemble.

**Why this is the better test.** Summarizing two positions requires only reading comprehension and is
compatible with believing one side is simply mistaken. Naming what each side would lose requires modeling why
intelligent researchers held it — which is precisely what makes a tension endure rather than resolve. It also
inoculates against presentism: a student who can state the losses will not narrate the current position as a
final resolution.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 5
**Objective 1.2a.** State the tension lens as a single question you could put to any empirical paper. Then
explain what a progress-narrative history would have obscured about this field.
:::
::: tab Answer
**The question:** *On which of the field's unresolved disagreements does this paper take a position, and does
the author appear to know they have taken one?*

The second clause carries most of the weight. Papers routinely take positions implicitly — by choosing a
laboratory paradigm over a field study, by locating an explanation in the person rather than the context, by
treating a normative standard as given. Asking whether the author knows separates deliberate commitments from
inherited defaults.

**What a progress narrative would obscure.** Three things. First, that current positions are *positions* and
not settled results — the presentism pitfall. Second, that some of the field's shape came from contingent
choices rather than discoveries: Allport's narrowed definition made experiments feasible and simultaneously
removed culture from view, which is a decision, not a finding. Third, it would obscure the losing arguments,
which is where a progress narrative does the most damage — Lewin's vision of experiments capturing transposable
genotypic structure did not prevail, but a student who never learns it cannot see what the field traded away
when it adopted Festinger's.
:::

## Section 1.3 — How the experiment came to dominate{{attrs[#blk-6eledke9es0r]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 6
**Objective 1.3a.** Explain why Allport's *definition* of social behavior mattered for the field's *method*.
What would have followed for experimental feasibility had he retained a broader conception?
:::
::: tab Answer
Allport restricted social behavior to individual behavior directed toward social objects — reactions to the
language, gestures and movements of other people, as opposed to reactions to non-social objects. The
methodological consequence is direct.

**Under the broad conception**, the forces acting on behavior are large, amorphous, multifaceted sociocultural
phenomena: a country's language, its religion, its political system. None can be manipulated, randomly
assigned, or held constant. Experimentation is not merely difficult but close to unintelligible as a strategy,
which is roughly the position Wundt took and the reason no social science of the period used experiments.

**Under the restricted conception**, the relevant forces are small, decomposable, isolable elements — the
presence or absence of another person being the paradigm case, as in Allport's own social facilitation
studies. These are manipulable, so clean designs become feasible.

**The deeper point** is Danziger's: the methodological innovation was not plausible or even conceivable
without a radical break from how the existing social sciences conceived of the social. Definition and method
were not chosen independently and then found to fit; they were settled together. A secondary consequence was
the separation from sociology, which until then had treated social psychology as a joint endeavor.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 7
**Objective 1.3a.** A study creates two elaborate group "climates" differing in many respects at once, aiming
to capture what distinguishes them as *kinds* of situation. A second study manipulates one variable under
tight control. Identify which conception of the experiment each embodies, and state what each buys and
forfeits.
:::
::: tab Answer
**The first is Lewinian.** Lewin's goal was for an experiment to capture the essential *genotypic* structure
of a phenomenon so that it could be transposed to other situations. Manipulating many things at once was not a
defect if the underlying structure was revealed — Schachter's recollection that earlier experiments were
"morasses" manipulating a million things describes exactly this, and Lewin would likely have accepted the
description without accepting it as criticism.

*Buys:* a result about a recognizable kind of situation, with some claim to transpose elsewhere.
*Forfeits:* the ability to say which ingredient did the work — the causal claim is about the composite.

**The second is Festingerian.** Festinger shared Lewin's interest in psychologically engaging experiments but
insisted that independent variables be manipulated systematically and under control, in keeping with
non-social experimental psychology. He prioritized *empirical* purity over *theoretical* purity, and defined
theory as statements about causal relations between variables.

*Buys:* a clean causal claim about a specified relation, and cumulative testability.
*Forfeits:* assurance that the isolated variable retains its meaning outside the arrangement built for it —
which is where the paradigm-narrowness critique enters.

**The historical outcome:** Lewin aspired to universal, mathematically precise theory and did not prevail;
Festinger aspired to meticulous tests of relations among conceptual variables and did.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 8
**Objective 1.3a.** "The experiment's dominance was achieved, not discovered." State precisely what does
*not* follow from this claim.
:::
::: tab Answer
Four things do not follow.

**It does not follow that the experiment was the wrong choice.** A method whose adoption depended on
historical contingency may still be the best available. The chapter documents how the method won; it does not
argue it should have lost.

**It does not follow that experimental findings are invalid.** The history bears on why the field studies what
it studies and how, not on whether any particular result replicates or holds. That question belongs to Module
2, and is answered with evidence rather than history.

**It does not follow that the excluded alternatives were superior.** Sociology's structural concerns and
Wundt's exclusion are shown to have been displaced, not shown to have been right.

**It does not follow that nothing has been learned.** The cynical reading — the field is merely a set of
conventions — is as much a misreading as the triumphalist one. What follows is narrower and more useful: the
field's scope and its method were settled together, so questions the method cannot reach may be absent from
the literature for reasons having nothing to do with their importance. That is a reason to read actively, not
a reason to dismiss.
:::

## Section 1.4 — Particularism versus universalism{{attrs[#blk-kydyh4hdpkei]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 9
**Objective 1.4a.** Distinguish the broad and narrow forms of the universalism–particularism tension. Then
give a case with high external validity in the narrow sense that says nothing about human universals.
:::
::: tab Answer
**The broad form** asks whether universal social psychological processes exist as fundamental, invariant parts
of human nature. It is ontological, it received little attention in the field's early decades, and it remains
unsettled.

**The narrow form** asks what the experimental method can reveal beyond its own specific operationalizations
of independent and dependent variables — whether a laboratory result about stereotyping tells us anything
about stereotyping outside the laboratory. This is **external validity**, and it dominated the field's
attention instead. The two differ in the breadth of "beyond": generalization to contexts outside the
experiment, versus generalization to principles of universal scope.

**A dissociating case.** A field experiment on the effect of a specific municipality's ballot design on voting
errors in a particular election, replicated across that municipality's precincts, can have high external
validity in the narrow sense: the finding describes real behavior in the setting it is about, generalizing
across the contexts it claims to cover. It says nothing whatever about human universals — ballots are a
recent cultural artifact and the effect may be entirely specific to that design, that electorate, that moment.

**The converse also exists**, which is worth noting: a laboratory paradigm with poor external validity might
still be probing a universal process, if the artificiality strips away context rather than creating the
effect. That is precisely the Lewinian counterargument — that the goal is abstract universal theory, not
generalization to the messy real world.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 10
**Objective 1.4a.** A critic says: "This effect has only ever been shown with one experimental paradigm, and
that paradigm was never validated as a measure of the construct it is supposed to capture." Which critique
line is this, and what would an adequate rebuttal have to establish?
:::
::: tab Answer
This is the **paradigm-narrowness** critique, and the passage states both of its components: too few
operational paradigms to capture the fullness of the concept, and paradigms that were not themselves
empirically validated.

**An adequate rebuttal must establish two separate things**, and defenders frequently supply only the first.

*That the paradigm measures the construct.* This requires validation evidence — that the operationalization
converges with other indicators of the construct and diverges from indicators of neighbouring ones. Repeating
the paradigm more times does not supply this; it establishes reliability, not validity.

*That the construct's range is not confined to the paradigm.* This requires the effect to appear under
operationalizations that do not share the original's incidental features. A demonstration under a genuinely
different paradigm is worth more than many replications of the same one.

**What does not rebut it.** Effect size does not; a large effect within one paradigm is still one paradigm.
Neither does prominence. And note that answering this critique leaves the other three untouched — artificiality,
the disregard of participant agency, and the anti-positivist objection are separate charges, and a defense
that addresses construct coverage says nothing about any of them.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 11
**Objective 1.4a.** Why did early defenders of the experimental method tend to presume that psychological
processes could be universal? Was that presumption logically required?
:::
::: tab Answer
**Why they presumed it.** The presumption follows from what makes an experiment worth running. In arguing that
experimental results applied beyond the confines of the experiment itself, Allport and his contemporaries
implied that experiments revealed invariant processes. If a result held only for the participants, the room
and the moment, the experiment would be an expensive way of learning nothing. Universalism was the assumption
that made the method's output valuable.

The historical setting reinforced it. Modernism and positivism had already consecrated the experiment as
science's principal vehicle, one suited to uncovering universal laws and causal explanations for physical
reality. Extending it to social reality carried that framing along.

Allport's restricted scope then removed the strongest standing objection. By excluding phenomena that could
not be easily decomposed — culture chief among them — the field's definition kept out of view precisely the
evidence that would have reminded researchers of the case for particularism.

**Was it required?** No. The two questions are logically distinct, as the chapter says: one concerns
generalization to contexts outside the experiment, the other generalization to principles of universal scope.
One could hold that experiments reveal robust regularities within a cultural context without claiming those
regularities are invariant features of human nature. The entwining was historical rather than logical — which
is why cultural psychology could later challenge assumed universality without abandoning experimentation.
:::

## Section 1.5 — Social context versus the individual{{attrs[#blk-a99objgxnt4j]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 12
**Objective 1.5a.** The chapter observes that the field embraced a "groupy" approach in theory but rarely
adopted it in practice. What would you look for in a study to tell the difference?
:::
::: tab Answer
The diagnostic is the **unit of explanation**, not the vocabulary.

**Signs the group is genuinely the unit.** The data have group-level structure that the analysis takes
seriously — outcomes belong to groups rather than being individual scores aggregated for convenience.
Participants interact, so that what one does depends on what others did. The theoretical quantity is
irreducibly relational — a norm, a shared representation, a status hierarchy — rather than an individual
attribute that happens to have a social referent. Manipulations act on the group's structure or composition
rather than on each individual's perception of a group.

**Signs it is individual psychology with group vocabulary.** Participants respond alone to a described or
imagined group. "Group identification" enters as an individual difference measured by questionnaire. The
dependent variable is one person's judgment about a category. Nothing in the design would change if the other
group members did not exist.

Neither pattern is a defect in itself — plenty of good questions are about how an individual represents a
group. The defect the chapter identifies is the mismatch: theoretical claims about group-level causation
resting on designs that could only support individual-level ones.

**A test to apply:** ask whether the study's conclusion would survive if you learned the "group" was
simulated. If it would, the group was not the unit of explanation.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 13
**Objective 1.5a.** What does Moscovici's work on minority influence establish that a standard conformity
account cannot? What do social representations locate outside the individual?
:::
::: tab Answer
**Minority influence.** The conformity tradition — suggestion, prestige, the voice of the herd — models
influence as flowing from the many to the few, with the individual's task being whether to yield. Minority
influence shows the arrow can run the other way: a numerically small position can move a majority. This is not
a mere addition of a special case. It undercuts the idea that influence is fundamentally about numerical
pressure on an individual, and requires an account in which the *content* and *consistency* of a position do
work that head-count cannot explain.

**Social representations.** These locate shared understanding at the level of the collective rather than
inside individual heads. A social representation is not the average of what individuals believe, nor a belief
each holds privately; it is a shared framework through which a community makes phenomena intelligible, and
individuals encounter it as already there. The claim is ontological, not just methodological — it says there
is something at the collective level to be studied, which is precisely what Allport's position denied when it
held that there is no psychology of groups that is not entirely a psychology of individuals.

**Why both matter for the tension.** Each supplies what the individualistic program said was unnecessary: a
level of explanation not reducible to individual responses to social objects. Together with Tajfel's
development of intergroup behavior into a meta-theory, they constitute the European tradition's case that
groups are causal forces from which individuals cannot be cleanly separated.
:::

## Section 1.6 — Rationality versus irrationality{{attrs[#blk-1127tu8g63xv]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 14
**Objective 1.6a.** Construct one example of a biased outcome produced by a well-adapted process, and one of a
process that looks irrational but yields accurate outcomes. Spell out the mechanism in each.
:::
::: tab Answer
**Biased outcome, well-adapted process.** Suppose judgments about whether an ambiguous approaching figure is
threatening are systematically skewed toward "threatening." Measured against the base rate, the judgment is
badly calibrated and produces many false alarms. But the *process* generating it is well tuned to an asymmetry
in costs: failing to detect a genuine threat is far more costly than a false alarm. A decision rule that
minimizes expected cost rather than error count will produce exactly this skew. The outcome is biased relative
to accuracy; the process is doing the right thing relative to survival — which is the chapter's point that the
relevant criterion may be survival rather than accuracy.

**Irrational-looking process, accurate outcome.** Suppose someone judges which of two options is better by
attending to a single salient cue and ignoring the rest. Against a normative model that weights and integrates
all available information, this looks like a failure of reasoning. Yet where cues are correlated and the
salient one is the most valid, the simple rule can match or beat the integrative one, particularly when the
integrative model's weights must be estimated from limited data. The process violates the normative standard;
the outputs are accurate.

**What the pair shows.** Outcome and process are separately evaluable, and each can be defective while the
other is sound. This is why the field's shift toward emphasizing the rationality of underlying processes did
not require denying that biased outcomes occur — and why "people are irrational" and "people are rational" are
both too coarse to be verdicts.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 15
**Objective 1.6a.** What evidence would distinguish "this bias is real and general" from "this bias is an
artifact of paradigms designed to elicit it"? How does this connect to a critique from §1.3?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The decisive evidence is appearance where nobody arranged for it.** Specifically:

*Unengineered settings.* The effect shows up in data collected for other purposes — archival records,
behavioral traces, field settings — where no one selected the conditions to make it visible.

*Structurally different operationalizations.* The effect survives paradigms that share the construct but not
the original's incidental features. If it appears only under stimuli chosen to maximize it, the artifact
reading gains.

*Predicted moderation.* The account predicts where the effect should *shrink* or reverse, and it does.
Artifacts rarely honor predictions about their own absence.

*Consequential outcomes.* The effect appears where participants have stake and opportunity to correct, not
only in low-cost laboratory judgments.

**What does not settle it.** Replication within the original paradigm — that establishes reliability, not
generality. And a large effect size within the engineered setting is exactly what a well-designed elicitation
would produce.

**The connection to §1.3.** This is the **paradigm-narrowness** critique arriving from another direction. There
the charge was too few operational paradigms, often unvalidated, to capture the fullness of the concept; here
it is that a paradigm built to elicit an effect cannot by itself establish the effect's range. Both place the
same burden on the researcher: show the phenomenon outside the arrangement built for it. Recognizing that the
module's third tension recycles a critique from its first is a sign the tension lens is doing real work.
:::

## Section 1.7 — The dispassionate scientist versus the value-driven advocate{{attrs[#blk-cvdte9kjlm5d]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 16
**Objective 1.7a.** A researcher's press release describes a modest, mixed-evidence finding as showing that a
policy "works." Which of the three forms of the values debate does this raise, and which does it not?
:::
::: tab Answer
**It raises the second form: advocacy fidelity to the strength of the underlying science.** The chapter
identifies exactly this failure mode — social psychologists exaggerating the strength and consistency of
policy-relevant research out of a desire to promote policies they believe are morally preferable. The defect
is a mismatch between the confidence asserted and the evidence held, and it is a defect regardless of whether
the policy is good.

**It does not, by itself, raise the first form** — how researchers' values shape which questions get asked and
which answers get found. That debate concerns the selection and framing of research problems. This researcher
may have chosen the topic for entirely conventional reasons and gone wrong only at the communication stage.
Evidence about topic selection would be needed to implicate the first form.

**It does not settle the third form** — whether advocacy by psychologists or their organizations is
appropriate at all. Someone who believes advocacy is entirely legitimate can condemn this press release, on
the grounds that advocacy is legitimate only when it represents the evidence honestly. Someone who opposes
advocacy in principle will also condemn it, for a different reason.

**Why the distinction matters.** Conflating the three produces bad arguments in both directions: treating every
overstatement as proof that advocacy is illegitimate, or treating a commitment to advocacy as licence to
overstate. The three debates have independent answers.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 17
**Objective 1.7a.** Explain how ethics review turns a value judgment into a constraint on what can be
discovered. Then state what the claim "science is value-laden" does *not* imply.
:::
::: tab Answer
**The mechanism.** Reicher and Haslam's argument, as the chapter presents it, runs in steps. Classic studies
such as the Stanford Prison Experiment and Milgram's obedience paradigm contributed to the creation of ethics
review boards. Those boards must weigh benefit to society against harm to individual participants. There is no
value-free procedure for determining what counts as a societal benefit, what counts as harm, or how to
magnitude-compare them — the judgment is irreducibly evaluative. That judgment then determines which research
is permissible, and therefore which discoveries can be made. A value judgment upstream becomes a boundary on
the knowable downstream.

**What the claim does not imply.**

*Not that ethics boards should be abolished.* The chapter is explicit on this point; the argument is about
self-understanding, not about dismantling review.

*Not that evidence fails to constrain conclusions.* Weber's position is that social science is value-*relevant*
— all knowledge of cultural reality is from particular points of view — while still holding that science should
be free of value judgment. McMullin's point is that the standards by which we weigh theories are value-laden:
Bohr weighted predictive accuracy, Einstein consistency with existing knowledge, and both are legitimate marks
of a good theory. That is a claim about how competing virtues get ranked, not a claim that data are inert.

*Not that findings are merely opinion.* The claim concerns which questions get asked, which studies are
permitted, and which theoretical virtues are weighted — not whether the world pushes back on our hypotheses.

**The precise conclusion**, as the chapter frames it: believing that values leave the shape of scientific
knowledge untouched is a comfortable illusion rather than an accurate account of how science works.
:::
::::

## Section 1.8 — The other lens{{attrs[#blk-akujbxslnmmk]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 18
**Objective 1.8a.** Take one of the four pillars and place it inside the four-tensions framework: which tension
does it belong to, and on which side?
:::
::: tab Answer
**Taking lay dispositionism.**

*What it names.* The tendency to over-attribute behavior to stable personal attributes rather than to
situations — the fundamental attribution error, with consequential cases in false confession and eyewitness
research. Even social psychologists underestimate situational power, and a self-serving asymmetry lets people
detect the situation's grip on themselves while missing it in others.

*Which tension.* **Tension 2, social context versus the individual.** But notice the twist that makes this
question worth asking: the pillar is not a *position* in that debate, it is a *finding about how ordinary
people take a position* in it. Lay dispositionism says that untrained perceivers side reflexively with the
individual pole. The tension concerns where explanation actually belongs; the pillar concerns where people
place it by default.

*On which side.* It supports the **situational** side, and does so by a route worth spelling out. If people
systematically under-weight situations, then the intuitions the field started from were biased toward
dispositional explanation — so the burden of proof was misallocated from the outset. This is why so many of
the field's celebrated findings are demonstrations of situational power: they are corrections against a known
bias.

*It also touches Tension 3.* A systematic misattribution is an irrational outcome. Whether the *process* is
irrational is a separate question — anchoring on a character judgment and then correcting is arguably an
efficient strategy that fails only when correction is curtailed.

**A second placement, briefly.** Naïve realism — believing one sees the world objectively and that dissenters
must be biased — sits inside **Tension 3**, on the side that says outcomes are systematically irrational. It
also bears on **Tension 1**: a researcher subject to naïve realism will find their own operationalizations
obviously valid and their own sample obviously representative.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 19
**Objective 1.8a.** Why does ordinary experience fail to teach people the power of situations? Reconstruct the
argument, then say what would be needed to correct it.
:::
::: tab Answer
**The argument, in steps.**

1. **People choose their situations.** Where a person is, who they are with, and what they are doing are
   substantially products of their own prior choices.
2. **Those choices are made on the basis of stable attributes** — preferences, abilities, temperament. A person
   who dislikes conflict selects into settings where conflict is rare.
3. **Situations then guide and constrain behavior.** Once in a setting, its demands shape what the person does.
4. **So person and situation are confounded in everything an observer sees.** The behavior a perceiver
   witnesses was produced by a situation the person selected because of their attributes. Disposition and
   situation point the same way.
5. **The dispositional explanation therefore always appears to fit**, and it is confirmed with every
   observation. Experience accumulates evidence consistent with the trait account, because the environment has
   been arranged — by the person — to produce trait-consistent behavior.
6. **Person × situation produces cumulative consequences**, so the confound strengthens over time rather than
   washing out.

**Why this is a deeper argument than "people are careless."** It shows that the error is *not* corrected by
more evidence, because the evidence is systematically confounded. A perceiver could be perfectly attentive and
still draw the wrong conclusion. Compare the self-serving asymmetry: people *can* see the situation's grip on
themselves, because they have access to the choosing — they know they picked this setting and why.

**What would be needed to correct it.**

*Observations across unchosen situations.* Watch someone in a setting they did not select — which is precisely
what an experiment with random assignment provides. Random assignment is not merely a statistical convenience;
it breaks the confound that ordinary experience cannot.

*Counterfactual information.* Knowing what this person does elsewhere, or what other people do here — the
consensus and distinctiveness information Kelley's covariation model asks for and everyday inference rarely
collects.

*Attention to one's own contribution.* The perceiver is often part of the situation producing the behavior —
the point Module 3 returns to as perceiver-induced constraint.

*Knowing the bias exists*, which helps less than one would hope: even social psychologists underestimate
situational power. That is worth stating plainly to students, because it forecloses the comfortable conclusion
that having read this makes them immune.
