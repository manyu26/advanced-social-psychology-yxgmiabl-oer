## Section 2.1 — The system circa 2010{{attrs[#blk-xaxynf8d3cbk]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 1
**Objective 2.1a.** State the chapter's two framing assumptions about pre-2010 research and the new standards.
What does each rule out, and how would your reading change if either were dropped?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The two assumptions.** First, that rather than casting the research of that era as slapdash at best and crooked at worst, researchers seeking publication really did shape their conception of good work around the system they worked within. Second, that the new system's own rules and standards remain open to challenge. The chapter says explicitly that both assumptions shape its review of the issues.

**What the first rules out.** A character explanation. If practices were produced by an incentive structure
rather than by individual failings, then replacing the individuals changes nothing and the analysis must be
about structures. It also rules out reading the reform period as a moral victory.

**What the second rules out.** Treating reform as a completed upgrade. It is what licenses the Costs and
Limitations section attached to every solution, and what makes the chapter's evaluation of the effect-size
threshold proposal possible — you cannot reject a reform proposal if reform is by definition improvement.

**If the first were dropped**, the eight solutions would read as sanctions rather than as design responses,
and the incentive analysis that makes them intelligible would disappear. You would also lose the ability to
ask what *current* practice will look like to a future chapter.

**If the second were dropped**, the chapter would become advocacy. Every Costs and Limitations section would
be an awkward concession rather than part of the argument, and a student would have no basis for evaluating a
new proposal — only for checking compliance with existing ones.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 2
**Objective 2.1a.** A researcher in 2005 wants to maximize the chance of publishing in a prestigious journal.
What would they rationally do? Which reforms target each behavior?
:::
::: tab Answer
Under acceptance rates of no more than about 20%, and norms rewarding a coherent series of studies supporting
a hypothesis, several behaviors are individually rational.

**Report the studies that worked and omit those that did not**, since a clean package reads as stronger
evidence. → Targeted by Solution 2, reporting null studies within an article, and Solution 6, full disclosure.

**Frame findings as confirmations of a hypothesis held in advance**, since exploratory framing reads as weak.
→ Targeted by Solution 7, pre-registration and Registered Reports, which also asks for honest exploration to
have its own legitimate place.

**Stop data collection once significance is reached**, since more data costs money and risks the result.
→ Targeted by Solutions 5 and 6 jointly: powering studies in advance and disclosing stopping rules.

**Pursue novel and surprising effects over confirmatory or null ones**, since novelty is what the system
values. → Targeted by Solutions 3 and 4, publishing null-conclusion articles and reporting independent direct
replications.

**Avoid reporting effect sizes that make a significant result look unimpressive.** → Targeted by Solution 1.

**Keep constructs loose enough that several operationalizations count as tests.** → Targeted by Solution 8 and
by the measurement section.

**The point of the exercise.** Every one of these is a reasonable response to the stated criteria, and none
requires bad faith. That is exactly the chapter's stance, made concrete: the reforms are redesigns of the
criteria, not sanctions on the people who met them.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 3
**Objective 2.1a.** Explain the tension between easy consistent rules and complex nuanced judgments, using an
evaluation rule from your own field or experience.
:::
::: tab Answer
**The tension as the chapter states it.** Publishing decisions judge both how valuable the ideas are and how
strong the evidence is. In both judgments there is a trade-off between rules that anyone can apply the same way but that may miss what they stand for, versus judgments that promise a better fit
along with more controversy, because different people reach different decisions.

**A worked example — using p < .05.** What it captures well: it is unambiguous, checkable by any reviewer, and
produces the same verdict regardless of who applies it. What it proxies badly: it is a statement about the
probability of data given a null hypothesis, not about whether a claim is true, how large the effect is, or
whether it matters. The nuanced alternative — weighing effect size, precision, prior plausibility, and design
quality together — tracks what we care about far better, and produces disagreement between equally competent
reviewers, less predictability for authors, and more room for reviewers' priors to operate unexamined.

**The general shape.** A rule buys consistency and auditability with construct validity. A judgment buys
construct validity with consistency and contestability. Neither is free, which is why the chapter treats the
choice as a tension rather than an error.

**Why this matters for the module.** Several of the eight solutions are attempts to install better rules, and
they inherit this trade-off. The effect-size threshold proposal in §2.3 is the clearest case of a reform that
buys consistency at too high a price.
:::

## Section 2.2 — The 2011 collision{{attrs[#blk-gr8d8718uo8w]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 4
**Objective 2.2a.** Lay out, in explicit steps, the inference from "this paper looks structurally normal" to
"the standards of evidence are inadequate." Which premise would a defender of the standards attack?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The inference.**

1. The paper's conclusion — that responses can be affected by stimuli presented afterward — would, if valid,
   overturn accepted understandings of psychology, neurobiology and physics.
2. Almost no reader accepts that conclusion.
3. So the paper's claim is very probably false.
4. Yet the paper was structurally like any other in the journal: an extremely novel claim, eight studies with
   significant results and one marginal at p = .098, method variations demonstrating replication and
   generalization, using state-of-the-art techniques.
5. It cleared the field's highest-impact empirical journal's review.
6. Therefore a process that certifies conventional-looking papers certified a very probably false one.
7. Therefore the process cannot reliably discriminate true from false claims of this form.

**The premise a defender would attack: (4).** The defense would be that the paper was not really conventional
— that it involved undisclosed flexibility, questionable analytic choices, or a mass of studies from which the
successful ones were selected. Critiques did in fact point out that the paper consisted of many studies of a
particular kind.

**Why this defense is double-edged, and the deeper point.** If those features are what distinguish the paper,
they must be *visible* to reviewers for the process to be vindicated — and they were not, since the paper was
accepted. If they are invisible in conventional reporting, the defense concedes the conclusion: the standards
cannot distinguish papers with those problems from papers without them. This is precisely why the reforms
target disclosure and pre-registration rather than reviewer vigilance.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 5
**Objective 2.2a.** The chapter notes both that crisis narratives recur in this field's history and that
publicized low replication rates can damage public trust. How should a field weigh these?
:::
::: tab Answer
The two observations answer different questions and should not be traded against each other.

**The historical observation is about calibration, not dismissal.** That amid the "crisis of 1977" someone
wrote recalling the "crisis of 1927" is a caution against treating the current period as unprecedented, and
against the rhetorical inflation that makes every methodological concern existential. It is not evidence that
current concerns are unfounded — a recurring alarm can still be a true alarm, and the recurrence may indicate
an unresolved structural problem rather than a habit of overreaction.

**The trust observation is about consequences, not truth.** Evidence that publicized low replication rates can
damage public trust bears on how findings should be communicated. It has no bearing on whether the
replication rates are what they are.

**Why the apparent trade-off is false.** The implied dilemma — publicize and lose trust, or stay quiet and
keep it — assumes concealment is stable. It is not: the alternative to disclosing a field's error rate is
having it disclosed by others, on worse terms. It also assumes trust tracks the absence of bad news, when
warranted trust tracks whether a field is seen to detect and correct its own errors.

**The defensible position.** Report the rates, and report them with the context that makes them
interpretable: what replication failure does and does not establish, which claims are affected, and what the
field is doing about it. Recall that the same methods revealing the problem are the ones being used to fix it
— that fact belongs in the public account, not as spin but because it is true and materially changes what a
reader should conclude.
:::

## Section 2.3 — Eight proposed solutions{{attrs[#blk-08320bx13a56]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 6
**Objective 2.3a.** Sort the eight solutions into groups by the failure mode each attacks. Defend your
taxonomy.
:::
::: tab Answer
A defensible grouping in four:

**What enters the literature (1, 2, 3).** Report effect sizes; report null studies within an article; publish
more articles with null conclusions. The shared failure mode is *selection*: the published record is a biased
sample of what was done and found. Solution 1 belongs here because reporting only significance is a form of
selective reporting within a paper.

**Whether findings hold (4, 5).** Report independent direct replication; increase statistical power. The
shared failure mode is *confusing detection with truth*: a single significant result in an underpowered
design licenses far less than it appears to.

**What is revealed, and when (6, 7).** Require full disclosure; enable pre-registration, Registered Reports
and honest exploration. The shared failure mode is *undisclosed flexibility* — the gap between the analysis
performed and the analysis reported, and between exploration and confirmation.

**What the evidence is for (8).** Strengthen theory. The failure mode is different in kind: if constructs are
loose, every other solution operates on well-measured evidence for an unclear claim.

**Defending the taxonomy.** The first three groups form a rough pipeline — what gets in, whether it holds,
what was hidden — and the fourth is orthogonal, which is why the chapter moves from Solution 8 into
measurement rather than closing there. An alternative sort by *who must act* (authors, editors, the field's
norms) is also defensible but cuts across the failure modes and so is less useful for diagnosing a paper.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 7
**Objective 2.3a.** A journal proposes requiring every submission to exceed a fixed sample-size threshold.
Evaluate using the chapter's reasoning about effect-size thresholds.
:::
::: tab Answer
The reasoning transfers, and noticing that it transfers is the point of the question.

**The parallel.** The chapter distinguishes *reporting* a statistic, which an editor can straightforwardly
check and require, from *setting a threshold* on it as a publication criterion. Requiring power analyses and
reported sample justification is the analogue of requiring effect-size reporting. A fixed minimum n is the
analogue of a minimum effect size, and inherits its problems.

**Specific objections.** A fixed threshold ignores effect size and design: a within-subjects design detecting
a large effect may be well powered at an n that a between-subjects design detecting a small one is not.
It ignores population accessibility, which would disadvantage exactly the peripheral-population research
that §2.5 shows is already disadvantaged. It reproduces the pathology of thresholding a statistic as a proxy
for worth, and creates an incentive toward cheap large-n designs — online self-report over effortful
behavioral observation — which runs directly against a critique the chapter records.

**The false assumption.** Most importantly, it assumes sample size alone determines false-positive risk. The
chapter is explicit that a study's sample size does influence the false-positive rate, and so does how plausible the hypothesis was to begin with. A large-n test of an implausible hypothesis can carry a higher false-positive
risk than a modest test of a plausible one — which is directly relevant to the 2011 case that opened the
module.

**The better version.** Require a stated sampling plan with a justification tied to the smallest effect of
interest — a rule about *disclosure and reasoning*, which is what the chapter's reforms actually do well.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 8
**Objective 2.3a.** Where do two of the solutions pull against each other? Describe the tension and how a
field might manage it.
:::
::: tab Answer
**Pre-registration (7) against exploratory research.** Pre-registration exists to prevent exploration from
being presented as confirmation. Applied bluntly it also discourages exploration itself, since deviation from
a registered plan reads as a defect. Yet exploratory work is where hypotheses come from — and the chapter's
Solution 8 discussion records a call for research to get *looser*, with description and exploration and
validation work needed before constructs are ready for confirmatory testing. Note that the chapter's own
heading names the resolution: pre-registration, Registered Reports, **and honest exploration**. Exploration is
given a labeled, legitimate place rather than being suppressed or disguised.

**Null-result publication (3) against a magnitude threshold.** Solution 3 aims to get null findings into the
literature so dead ends are publicized rather than repeatedly stumbled into. Any criterion that filters on
effect magnitude removes exactly those results. This is one concrete reason the effect-size threshold proposal
fails: it is incompatible with another reform the same field is pursuing.

**Power (5) against generalizability (§2.5).** Raising required sample sizes is easiest to satisfy with
convenient, cheap participants — online panels drawn from central populations, self-report rather than
observed behavior. Pursued alone, a power requirement can worsen both the WEIRD problem and the
self-report-over-behavior problem the chapter records.

**Managing it.** The general move is to make the *reasoning* auditable rather than to fix a numeric bar:
register plans while labeling exploratory analyses honestly; require sampling justification tied to the
smallest effect of interest; require scope statements alongside power statements. Each keeps the reform's
benefit without the rigidity that generates the conflict.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 9
**Objective 2.3a.** Why does increasing sample size not by itself settle false-positive risk? What else
matters?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The claim.** The chapter reports simulation work underscoring that a study's sample size does influence the
false-positive rate, and so does how plausible the hypothesis was to begin with being tested.

**Why the prior matters.** The false-positive risk is the probability that a claim is false given that the
test came out significant. That is not the same as the alpha level, which is the probability of a significant
result given that the null is true. Converting between them requires knowing how likely the hypothesis was
before the test. Testing a hypothesis with a low prior yields a high proportion of false positives among
significant results even at conventional alpha, because there are few true effects available to be detected.
Sample size raises power — the chance of detecting a true effect — but cannot manufacture true effects to
detect.

**The connection to the 2011 case.** This is why the precognition paper's eight significant studies did not
persuade. Its prior was extraordinarily low, so even a well-powered series of significant results left the
claim improbable. The inference readers actually made — the standards must be inadequate — is the same
reasoning stated informally.

**What follows practically.** Power is necessary and not sufficient. Where a hypothesis is speculative, the
appropriate responses are pre-registered replication, independent confirmation, and Solution 8's demand for
better-established conceptual priors — the chapter's own framing is that the background plausibility our evidence rests on ought to be pinned down better, even where no number can be put on it.
:::

## Section 2.4 — Manipulation and measurement{{attrs[#blk-hw3r0gij74jo]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 10
**Objective 2.4a.** Explain the zoo analogy. What would make such a
meta-analysis interpretable?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The analogy.** Meta-analysis cannot give good effect-size estimates when the studies it pools were done in very different ways and each one's influence on the total rests on nothing better than how often that approach got published. The resulting average may mean no more than a figure obtained by averaging the body weights of every animal in a zoo.

**Why the comparison bites.** The zoo average is arithmetically correct and refers to nothing. There is no
animal it describes, no population it estimates, and it moves with the zoo's acquisition history rather than
with any fact about animals. A meta-analytic average across methodologies that operationalize a construct
differently has the same defect: it estimates no quantity, and it shifts with which paradigms researchers
happened to favor.

**What would make it interpretable.**

*Homogeneous, validated methodology.* The chapter's own prescription is standardizing well-validated
methodologies and accounting for method interactions, which yields more accurate estimates of both size and
direction.

*Method as a moderator rather than noise.* If operationalizations differ systematically, model the difference
and report estimates by method instead of a single pooled figure.

*A defined estimand.* State what quantity the average is meant to estimate — the effect under a specified
operationalization in a specified population — so the number refers to something.

*Weighting by design quality rather than prevalence.* Prevalence reflects publication history, not
informativeness.

**The connection to the section's theme.** This is why measurement follows theory in the chapter. Deciding
which methods belong in one analysis requires knowing which features are essential to the construct and which
are incidental — a theoretical question, not a statistical one.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 11
**Objective 2.4a.** A replication attempt yields a null. What evidence would distinguish a genuine near-zero
effect from a manipulation that failed to take?
:::
::: tab Answer
**Why the question became urgent.** For decades a positive result was taken as sufficient evidence that the
manipulation worked — an inference available only when the result is positive. Under independent replication
and under Registered Reports that publish nulls, that inference is unavailable exactly when it is most needed,
and the chapter poses the question directly: how can we be sure a null means the effect really is close to zero rather than that the study was never set up properly?

**Evidence bearing on it.**

*Validated manipulation checks*, showing the independent variable varied appropriately between conditions —
subject to the limits below.

*Positive controls.* The same sample and procedure reproduces a different, well-established effect. This
localizes the failure: if the control works, the setup is functioning.

*Dose–response.* A graded manipulation produces graded movement on the check even where the outcome does not
move.

*Prior validation of the specific protocol*, independent of the outcome — the point of validating
manipulations in their own right rather than inferring backward from results.

*Comparison to the original's own check data*, where reported, to see whether the manipulation was as strong.

**The limits the chapter insists on.** A check taken in the same sitting cannot by itself settle a manipulation's validity, either way. Asking participants directly can alter the procedure itself by tipping them off to what is being varied, which is why nonverbal checks have been proposed. And on a stronger view,
manipulation checks are unnecessary tests informing us only about effects at an operational level. So the
honest answer is that no single check settles it — the case is built from converging evidence, and pre-registered
validation collected *before* the confirmatory test is worth more than any check bolted on afterward.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 12
**Objective 2.4a.** What are "questionable measurement practices," and why does the chapter place measurement
immediately after theory?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The practices.** Critiques of the "measurement, schmeasurement" attitude identify undisclosed flexibility in
measurement decisions — such as choosing which items from a scale to use — together with poor reliability and
unvalidated scales. Studies of measurement practice in psychology support the conclusion that questions of measurement quality are passed over as a matter of course, both in doing research and in publishing it. A related warning concerns a deficit in construct
validity, remedied by carefully mapping constructs using nomological networks.

**Why they are not merely a statistical problem.** Undisclosed flexibility in *measurement* is the same
species of problem as undisclosed flexibility in *analysis*, which Solutions 6 and 7 address — but it operates
earlier and is less visible. Choosing which items to score after seeing the data is a researcher degree of
freedom that no analysis-level reform detects, because by the time analysis begins the variable already
exists.

**Why measurement follows theory.** The chapter says method belongs directly after theory in the order of discussion, and gives the reason: the wish-list for better theorizing includes tighter
construct definitions, which would produce clearer operationalizations of manipulation and measurement.
Greenwald's formulation is the compressed version — nothing carries more theory in it than a well-chosen method. You
cannot say which parts of a procedure are essential and which incidental without a theory specifying what the
construct is. Loose constructs make questionable measurement practices possible and, in a sense, unavoidable:
where a construct does not determine its operationalization, every choice among indicators is discretionary,
and no amount of statistical reform reaches it.
:::

## Section 2.5 — Challenges to generalizability{{attrs[#blk-3a5h2e4w8n8s]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 13
**Objective 2.5a.** Explain why the chapter treats generalizability as structural rather than as a sampling
limitation. Cite the documented mechanisms.
:::
::: tab Answer
A sampling limitation is a property of a study and is fixed by sampling differently. The chapter's evidence
describes a system that reproduces the limitation regardless of any individual study's choices.

**Composition.** Researchers, journal editors and research populations are drawn overwhelmingly from more
central nations and cultures. The people deciding what is publishable and interesting are drawn from the same
populations being studied.

**Asymmetric labeling.** That composition sustains an unstated presumption: work drawing on central
populations reads as the ordinary case whose reach requires no argument, while work on populations further out
is treated as a departure that has to be named and accounted for. That asymmetry is not a property of any study; it is a property of how
studies are read.

**Bibliometric evidence.** Titles disclose a sample's provenance far less often when that sample comes from a
central population — and research that does announce a peripheral sample is judged less important as a result. Unmarked defaults are exactly how structural bias becomes invisible.

**Reported discrimination.** Surveyed social psychologists working outside the central zones describe
widespread unequal treatment — hearing that their work is too narrow or not of general interest, and being
asked to demonstrate validity in ways central-zone submissions never face, such as adding a comparison group
from a central population or separately validating their measures.

**Why the distinction has practical consequences.** If it were a sampling limitation, the fix would be diverse
samples. If it is structural, diverse samples are necessary but insufficient — because the same system will
treat those samples as special cases requiring justification, ask their authors for extra validation, and
judge the resulting work less important. The intervention has to reach the labeling and gatekeeping, not only
the recruiting.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 14
**Objective 2.5a.** How does a paper claim generality without stating it? Describe the mechanism and how to
detect it.
:::
::: tab Answer
**The mechanism is omission.** Explicit claims to describe all of humanity are rare, but
psychology often makes them implicitly. As Henrich and colleagues put it, the ordinary paper never announces that it is about "humans"; it just reports a decision bias, a process, a set of correlations, and says nothing about how far any of it reaches — while the findings are nonetheless linked to "people."

**How it works rhetorically.** Naming a phenomenon without a scope qualifier invites the default reading that
it is a property of people. "Participants showed X" is a claim about a sample; "X occurs when people are under
time pressure" is a claim about people, and papers slide from the first to the second between results and
discussion with no evidence added in between. The generalization is carried by grammar, not argument, which
is why it survives peer review — there is no sentence to challenge.

**Detection.**

*Read the title and abstract for scope markers.* Does the sample's origin appear? Compare with how a paper
using a peripheral sample would be titled — the asymmetry is the tell.

*Track the noun across sections.* Where does "participants" become "people"? That transition is where the
unargued generalization happens.

*Check whether the discussion's claims are indexed* to the population studied, or float free of it.

*Ask whether the limitations section names the sample's cultural scope as a limitation on the claim*, or
mentions demographics without drawing the inferential consequence.

**A caution.** The point is not that every paper must claim only what its sample supports and nothing more —
generalization is what science is for. The point is that it should be *argued*, with reasons for expecting the
process to hold elsewhere, rather than assumed by default for some populations and demanded as proof from
others.
:::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 15
**Objective 2.5a.** How does this module's generalizability section relate to Module 1's universalism tension?
What does it add?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The relation.** Module 1's first tension asked whether findings and theories can apply beyond the
parameters, samples and contexts in which they were demonstrated — in its broad form whether universal social
psychological processes exist, in its narrow form what an experiment reveals beyond its own
operationalizations. Module 2's generalizability section takes the same question and asks it empirically about
the current literature.

**What it adds — three things.**

*Evidence in place of argument.* Module 1 presents the universalism debate as a contest of positions. Module 2
supplies bibliometric findings, survey data on discrimination, and documented composition of researchers and
editors. The question moves from "should we assume universality?" to "here is how the assumption operates and
who bears its costs."

*A mechanism, not just a caution.* Module 1's account explains historically why universalism became a default
— Allport's restricted scope excluded culture, removing the phenomenon that would have kept particularism
visible. Module 2 shows the default being maintained now, by a center–periphery structure in which central-
population research is treated as automatically generalizable and peripheral work must justify itself.

*An asymmetry Module 1 does not name.* The universalism tension is symmetric as stated — two positions, both
with proponents. Module 2 shows the debate is not conducted symmetrically: one kind of research is asked to
demonstrate its scope and the other is not, and unmarked defaults are how that asymmetry stays invisible.

**Where it points.** Forward to Module 7, where cultural and cross-cultural psychology supplies the positive
program — not only that assumed universality is unwarranted, but what varies, how, and what follows for
theory.
