## Section 3.1 — The miracle, and why accuracy is the wrong default{{attrs[#blk-sjzsjd5m25mt]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 1
**Objective 3.1a.** Why can't a target's self-report serve as the objective criterion for whether a perceiver
judged them accurately? What would any adequate criterion have to provide?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The chapter's position** is that targets' self-report cannot serve as the objective criterion. The reasons
compound.

*Targets have limited access to their own states.* The chapter's own closing section establishes this: people
cannot explain why they find one face more attractive or why they chose one option, and they construct
plausible but possibly irrelevant explanations. A criterion drawn from self-report inherits that limitation
wholesale.

*Self-report is itself a self-presentation.* Section 3.6 establishes that targets curate what they share, with
goals that may not align with the researcher's. A self-report is a communicative act produced under those
goals, not a readout.

*The two measures are not independent in the right way.* If the target's report and the perceiver's judgment
both draw on shared cultural models of what people are like, agreement can be produced by that shared model
rather than by the perceiver reading this target.

**What an adequate criterion would need.** Independence from the judgment being validated; access to the state
that does not run through the target's introspection — behavioral outcomes, physiological measures,
aggregated informant reports; a defensible claim to be measuring the same construct the perceiver was judging;
and stability over the interval concerned, or an explicit account of what is being estimated.

**The deeper difficulty.** Even granting a standard, the chapter notes that the sheer distance between
standard and judgment does not account for the perceiver's *ability* to be accurate — a perceiver may be close
by luck, by using a valid base rate rather than reading the target, or because the target is easy to read.
Distance is an outcome measure; ability is a property of the perceiver's process, and the two come apart.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 2
**Objective 3.1a.** A study reports that Perceiver A judges targets' emotions more accurately than Perceiver
B. Which sources of variance could produce this, and what design would separate them?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The three sources**, as the chapter frames them: the **perceiver** may be an astute reader of other minds in
general; the **target** may simply be an open book, easy for any perceiver to read; or perceiver and target
may **resonate**, with accuracy residing in their particular relationship.

**How each could produce the reported result.**

*Perceiver variance.* A genuinely reads minds better than B — the interpretation the result invites.

*Target variance masquerading as perceiver variance.* A and B judged different targets, and A's happened to be
more expressive. The finding is then about targets, not perceivers.

*Relationship variance.* A judged targets they know or resemble; the accuracy belongs to the pairing.

*A fourth possibility the chapter's framing warns about.* A may have better base rates rather than better
reading — knowing what people typically feel in that situation and applying it. That produces accurate
judgments without any target-specific reading.

**The separating design.** A fully crossed design in which every perceiver judges every target, with multiple
targets and multiple perceivers, lets variance be partitioned into perceiver main effects, target main
effects, and the perceiver-by-target interaction that carries relationship accuracy. This is precisely what
the critique of earlier work implied: the field was doing the math wrong by not partitioning these.

To separate base-rate accuracy from target reading, include targets whose actual states depart from the base
rate. A perceiver applying stereotypes will be accurate on typical targets and wrong on atypical ones; a
perceiver reading the target will track both.

**The residual limit.** Even a correct partition tells you *where* accuracy lives, not *how* the perceiver
achieved it — the chapter's point that the math said nothing about the perceiver's processes.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 3
**Objective 3.1a.** The chapter claims work on person perception has rarely been about persons or about
perceptions. Restate the critique, and describe what a research program taking it seriously would look like.
:::
::: tab Answer
**The critique.** "Person perception" misdescribes its subject twice. Not **persons**: the targets are not
whole autonomous individuals but incomplete, fragmented, static chunks — a photograph, a trait word, a
described behavior. Not **perceptions**: not immediate encoding of what impinges on eye, ear or nose, but
cognition and judgment operating on already-interpreted material. What perceivers do in these studies is judge
isolated cues.

**Why it matters rather than being a quibble.** The construct's name licenses generalization the paradigms do
not support. Calling it perception implies immediacy and stimulus-drivenness; calling the stimulus a person
implies the findings describe how we understand people. This is Module 2's construct-validity problem at the
level of a whole subfield — and note that the chapter, unusually, makes the critique from inside.

**A program taking it seriously.**

*Whole targets.* Real people with histories, roles and stakes, encountered over time, rather than isolated
cues — since the chapter concludes that the significant context is interdependent dyads.

*Actual perception where perception is claimed.* Either study the encoding directly, with appropriate temporal
resolution, or rename the process cognition and judgment and stop claiming immediacy.

*Interdependence built in.* Both parties perceiving and being perceived, with real consequences, so
self-presentation and mutual influence are in the design rather than excluded from it.

*Naturalistic methods.* The chapter points here — neuroimaging that permits naturalistic experience, real-time
interaction, and AI-assisted analysis of rich behavioral data.

**The honest trade-off.** Each move costs experimental control, which is why the field made the choices it
did. That is Module 1's argument — method and subject matter settled together — showing up in a specific
literature, and the chapter's admission that the field studied the less significant case for tractability is
an unusually candid instance of it.

## Section 3.2 — Baselines{{attrs[#blk-d7eom2dye2ve]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 4
**Objective 3.2a.** Lay out the inference from seeking intent to requiring mentalizing imagination. Which
steps are empirical rather than definitional?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The chain.**

1. Perceivers seek the target's **intent**.
2. Intent is viewed as reflecting **long-term behavioral tendencies and personality**.
3. **Having intent implies having a mind.**
4. People default to thinking about others in terms of the mental content that distinguishes a human mind —
   not as physical objects pushed by the world, but as moved by their own thoughts, beliefs, desires and states.
5. This intentional default is strong enough to be applied at times even to **non-human** targets.
6. Because the target is an autonomous intentional agent whose central states and traits are **unobservable**,
   the perceiver must infer them from behavior.
7. That inference requires the perceiver to have a **mentalizing imagination**.

**Which steps are empirical.**

*Step 1 is empirical* — a claim about what perceivers actually seek, which could be false.

*Step 2 is empirical* — perceivers might have treated intent as purely situational; that they read it as
dispositional is a finding, and one that matters for attribution.

*Step 4 is empirical* — the strength and automaticity of the intentional default is measurable and could have
come out otherwise.

*Step 5 is empirical and is the strongest evidence for step 4*, because over-application to non-human targets
shows the default is not derived from evidence about the particular target.

*Step 3 is closest to definitional*, or at least conceptual — intent is a mental state, so attributing intent
is attributing a mind. Its content is the claim that perceivers *make* this inference, not that it is valid.

*Steps 6 and 7 are largely analytic* given the preceding: if states are unobservable and must be inferred, a
capacity to represent them is required by definition. The empirical question is whether perceivers deploy it,
and how well.

**Why the sorting matters.** Steps that are definitional cannot be evidence for the framework; they are
consequences of adopting it. The framework's empirical weight rests on 1, 2, 4 and especially 5.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 5
**Objective 3.2a.** What does it show that the intentional default is sometimes applied to non-human targets?
What would a competing account predict?
:::
::: tab Answer
**What it shows.** That the intentional stance is not *derived* from evidence about the particular target. If
perceivers reasoned from observed features to a conclusion about whether this entity has a mind, entities
lacking the relevant features would not receive the attribution. That they do — shapes, robots, systems —
indicates the default is applied first and revised, if at all, afterward.

This supports the chapter's stronger claim that person perception commences **before any sensory input**. A
default applied to targets that do not warrant it is behaving like a prior, not a conclusion.

**It also indicates cheapness and speed.** A default that over-applies is one whose application costs little
and whose errors are tolerable — consistent with the pragmatics-over-accuracy framing. Over-attributing mind
to a machine costs little; under-attributing mind to a person is expensive.

**What a competing account would predict.** An evidence-driven account — perceivers detect cues to
agency (self-propelled motion, contingent responsiveness, goal-directedness) and infer mind from them — makes
different predictions:

*Graded attribution tracking cue presence*, rather than a default applied and then walked back.
*No attribution to entities lacking the cues* — but attribution occurs for entities with minimal cues.
*Longer latencies for ambiguous entities*, since evidence must accumulate; the default account predicts fast
attribution followed by slower correction.
*Elimination by full information* — telling a perceiver an entity has no mind should end the attribution. That
it often does not is the strongest discriminating evidence.

**A caution.** The two accounts are not exclusive; a plausible synthesis has a default applied broadly and
then modulated by cues. The chapter's contribution is establishing that there *is* a default doing work before
the cues arrive — and that is what makes the pre-encounter baseline section more than a preliminary.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 6
**Objective 3.2a.** How does the intentional default relate to the correction failure at the chapter's end?
:::
::: tab Answer
They are the same mechanism seen at the beginning and end of the module, and connecting them is the point of
the chapter's temporal organization.

**At the beginning.** Perceivers arrive with a strong intentional default: people are moved by their own
thoughts, beliefs and desires, and perceivers intuitively default to explaining behavior by **internal movers
over external ones**. This is installed before any evidence about the target.

**At the end.** People begin a social inference by anchoring on a character judgment — *Leslie is cranky* —
and then correct away from it by taking the situation into account. They are often unaware they fail to
complete the correction, and correction can be curtailed when time or effort is short.

**The connection.** The anchor is not arbitrary. It is the intentional default expressing itself as a first
guess: an explanatory preference for internal causes, laid down before the encounter, becomes the starting
point from which situational correction must work. The fundamental attribution error is therefore not a
separate bias bolted on — it is what the module's opening commitment looks like when the correction stage
underperforms.

**Why the correction is fragile.** It is *corrective* rather than primary — a second operation on an output
already produced, requiring time and effort the first operation did not. And it runs out of awareness, so
perceivers have no signal that it was incomplete.

**The sharpest part.** Perceivers **especially neglect their own influence as part of the situation**. This is
worse than ordinary situational neglect, because the perceiver is the one part of the situation they have
privileged access to and full responsibility for. Section 3.6 explains why it is hard to see: in interdependent
interaction each person is simultaneously perceiver and target, so one's own behavior is an input to the very
conduct one is explaining. Behavioral confirmation and perceiver-induced constraint are cases where the
perceiver produced the evidence they then attribute to the target's character.
:::

## Section 3.3 — Freeze-frame{{attrs[#blk-wt650bjgss7d]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 7
**Objective 3.3a.** Explain what warmth and competence each signal, and give a case where treating them as
independent yields the wrong prediction.
:::
::: tab Answer
**What each signals.** **Warmth**, or communality, is the inference about the target's **intent** — their
interest in playing well with others — and it carries the message of **safety**. **Competence** is the
inference about **capability**, and it carries the message of **dominance**. Together they answer the two
questions an approach-or-avoid decision needs: does this person mean me well, and can they act on it?

**Why they interact.** The chapter notes that a target scoring low on communality and warmth might well be
higher on the other dimension. The dimensions are not orthogonal ratings but a space whose regions carry
distinct social meanings — high on both describes the ingroup and its allies; low on both describes society's
most marginalized groups.

**A case where independence fails.** Consider how threat is judged. Treating the dimensions as independent
predicts that perceived threat rises as warmth falls, with competence contributing separately or not at all.
The interactive account predicts something different: **low warmth is threatening in proportion to
competence.** A target seen as ill-intentioned but incapable is not frightening; a target seen as
ill-intentioned and highly capable is the most threatening combination available. Competence *amplifies* the
consequence of low warmth rather than adding to it.

The mirror case: raising a target's perceived competence is beneficial when warmth is high and harmful when
warmth is low. An additive model cannot represent a variable whose sign flips with the level of another, so it
must get one of these wrong.

**A second case.** Perceived **interdependence** predicts perceived warmth. So a manipulation that makes a
perceiver dependent on a target should move warmth — and, through the interaction, change what the target's
competence implies. An independent-dimensions model treats the two as unconnected and predicts no such
downstream shift.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 8
**Objective 3.3a.** Under what conditions does an impression stay category-based rather than becoming
individuating?
:::
::: tab Answer
The chapter's continuum runs from category-based to target-based knowledge, and from more automatic
category-based impressions to more deliberate individuating ones. What moves a perceiver along it is what the
question asks about.

**Conditions favoring category-based impressions.**

*Little time and little cognitive resource.* Individuating inference is deliberate and effortful, and the
chapter notes that out-of-awareness correction can be curtailed when time or effort is short. The same
constraint stops the move toward individuation.

*Nothing at stake.* If the approach-or-avoid decision is all the perceiver needs, the first-instant output is
sufficient and further inference has no payoff. This follows directly from pragmatics rather than accuracy.

*No interdependence.* Interdependence is what makes the target's actual dispositions consequential — and
perceived interdependence predicts perceived warmth, so it changes the impression as well as the motivation to
refine it.

*Thin or fragmentary evidence.* Trait inference requires scraps accumulated across occasions; a single brief
encounter cannot supply them.

*Behavior consistent with the category.* Confirming evidence gives no occasion to revise, and perceivers
override data with expectations and remember selectively.

*Behavioral confirmation.* The perceiver's own conduct can elicit category-consistent behavior, manufacturing
the confirming evidence — which is the perceiver-induced constraint the chapter names at the end.

**Conditions favoring individuation.** Time, resource, stake, interdependence, repeated encounters across
varied contexts, and evidence that violates the category.

**Why this is the important question.** It converts the module from description into prediction, and it is the
form Module 11 will need: prejudice is not a different mechanism but this apparatus operating under the first
set of conditions.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 9
**Objective 3.3a.** Perceived interdependence predicts perceived warmth. What follows, including where this
could be deliberately exploited?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The finding.** An individual's or group's perceived interdependence with the perceiver predicts their
perceived **warmth** — the dimension carrying intent and safety.

**Why it makes sense pragmatically.** If warmth answers "does this person mean me well," then whether our
outcomes are linked is directly relevant. Someone whose success depends on mine has structural reason to
intend me well, independent of their character. The perceiver is reading the incentive structure, not just the
person — which is a *reasonable* inference and further evidence for the pragmatics framing.

**What follows.**

*Warmth judgments are partly about the situation, not the target.* Two perceivers can rate the same target
differently with no disagreement about the target, because their interdependence differs. Warmth ratings are
therefore not straightforwardly a property of the target.

*It gives a lever for intervention.* Restructuring tasks so outcomes are genuinely linked should raise
perceived warmth — the mechanism behind cooperative-interdependence approaches that Module 11 and Module 12
will take up.

*It complicates measurement.* A warmth rating collected in a context where interdependence differs from the
target context measures something else.

**Where it could be exploited.** The manipulation is *signalling* interdependence without creating it —
manufacturing an appearance of shared fate to import warmth the relationship does not warrant. The shape is
familiar: a salesperson framing a transaction as "let's figure out what works for you"; political rhetoric
constructing a shared enemy so that alignment implies benevolence; an institution invoking "we're all in this
together" while outcomes remain one-sided.

**The defense**, which is a good seminar question: ask whether the interdependence is *structural* — would
this person actually bear a cost if I did badly? — rather than rhetorical. Note that the perceiver's opacity
makes this hard, since the warmth impression will be available while its source is not.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 10
**Objective 3.3a.** "Person perception is prejudice with a neutral name." Where is this right, and where does
it overreach?
:::
::: tab Answer
**Where it is right.** The chapter does not soften the continuity. Freeze-frame demographic categories —
gender, race, class — automatically capture an initial impression of societal identity from **superficial
cues**. The perceiver mind-reads from the outset. The primary output is whether to **approach as ingroup or
avoid as outgroup**. And the two dimensions organizing person perception are the same dimensions organizing
the Stereotype Content Model, including the position describing society's most marginalized. Anyone expecting
a clean separation between perceiving individuals and stereotyping groups should abandon it.

**Where it overreaches.**

*Categorization is one stage of a sequence.* The chapter's temporal organization exists because more follows —
state decoding, then trait inference across encounters, moving from category-based toward individuating
knowledge. Prejudice describes impressions that *stay* category-based; person perception includes the
machinery by which they need not.

*The baseline stage precedes categorization and is not group-specific.* Before any input, perceivers assume
intent, mind, agency and complex emotions, often optimistically — applied to the target as a human. That is
the foundation individuation builds on.

*Automatic does not mean fixed.* Targets actively curate what they present, interaction is interdependent, and
perceived interdependence shifts perceived warmth. An account on which the first-moment category settled the
impression could not accommodate self-presentation or behavioral confirmation.

**The better formulation.** Prejudice and individuating impression formation are outputs of the **same
apparatus under different conditions** — time, attention, interdependence, motivation, and the character of
the available evidence. This is stronger than the original claim because it predicts *when* each outcome
occurs, and it is the formulation Module 11 will build on.

**What the objection gets right that a defensive reading would miss.** The continuity is not an embarrassment
to be explained away. That ordinary person perception and prejudice share machinery is why prejudice is
common, durable, and not confined to unusual people — and any account treating them as separate systems would
have to explain that away instead.
:::

## Sections 3.4–3.5 — States and traits{{attrs[#blk-hnx1gttq07vg]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 11
**Objective 3.4a/3.5a.** A colleague arrives at a meeting, says little, and leaves early. Give the state
reading, the trait reading, and the evidence that would settle which is licensed.
:::
::: tab Answer
**The state reading.** The colleague is in a poor or preoccupied mood right now, or is unwell, or is under
time pressure. This is the reading the chapter says perceivers experience as **immediately accessible**, from
posture, facial expression and movement.

**The trait reading.** The colleague is aloof, disengaged, or uninterested in this group.

**Why the trait reading is not licensed here.** Trait inference **should entail consistency of multiple
observations over occasions**, and this is one observation on one occasion. The chapter is candid that
perceivers make immediate trait inferences anyway — spontaneously categorizing what trait a behavior implies
is an earlier, immediate inference — which is exactly the gap between the normative requirement and actual
practice.

**Evidence that would settle it.**

*Consistency across occasions.* Does the pattern recur across meetings separated in time?

*Consistency across contexts.* Does it appear in settings with different demands and different people? Behavior
confined to one context points to the situation rather than the person — which is precisely the correction
perceivers fail to complete.

*Distinctiveness.* Is this colleague quiet in a meeting where others are engaged, or was the whole room flat?

*Situational information.* Independent knowledge of an immediate cause — bad news that morning, a competing
deadline — supplies the situational explanation directly.

*The perceiver's own contribution.* Did I do something that produced this — dominate the discussion, schedule
badly? The chapter emphasizes that perceivers **especially neglect their own influence as part of the
situation**, so this is the check least likely to be run and most worth running.

**The uncomfortable part.** If a trait inference forms immediately, later observations are read through it —
people override data with expectations and remember selectively, and often remember the inference while losing
the data that produced it. So the trait belief may survive as consistency across occasions that was actually
consistency of *interpretation*.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 12
**Objective 3.4a/3.5a.** Why should an inference that feels immediate be treated with more suspicion rather
than less? What follows for a perceiver who cannot tell how a trait belief formed?
:::
::: tab Answer
**Why immediacy warrants suspicion.**

*Immediacy indexes process, not validity.* State inferences feel immediately accessible because they are
computed fast from available cues — a fact about the mechanism, saying nothing about whether the cue-to-state
mapping is correct. Confidence tracks fluency, and fluency tracks speed.

*Immediate inferences skip the checks.* Deliberate inference can weigh consistency, context and alternative
explanations; an immediate one has not, by definition. The correction stage is precisely what perceivers fail
to complete, and it is the stage immediacy bypasses.

*The category-based first pass is the least individuating.* What arrives fastest is the superficial-cue
category read, which is the impression furthest from the target as an individual.

*Immediate trait inferences violate the normative requirement outright.* Trait inference should entail
consistency across occasions; an inference made in one instant from one behavior cannot satisfy that, however
compelling it feels.

**What follows from being unable to tell how a belief formed.**

*Beliefs of very different evidential weight feel the same.* A trait belief formed in one instant and one
accumulated across months carry no distinguishing marker. Confidence therefore cannot be used to sort them.

*Confidence is uninformative and possibly inverted.* An instantly-formed belief may feel *more* certain, since
it was never complicated by disconfirming observation.

*Revision is undermined.* People often remember the inference but not the data that produced it, so the
perceiver cannot audit the belief by reviewing its evidence — the evidence is gone and only the conclusion
remains.

*Self-report about one's own impressions is weak evidence* — a Module 2 point about measurement arriving from
an unexpected direction.

**The practical upshot.** Since introspection cannot sort these beliefs, the sorting has to be external: ask
what you actually observed, on how many occasions, in how many contexts, and whether you had a hand in
producing it. That is a discipline, not an intuition, and the chapter's account explains why it has to be.
:::

## Section 3.6 — Active targets and interaction{{attrs[#blk-z473hgery7r4]}}

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 13
**Objective 3.6a.** Take one self-presentation goal. Describe its enactment, its downside risk, and what a
perceiver who detects the strategy concludes.
:::
::: tab Answer
**Taking ingratiation — the goal of being liked.**

*The enactment.* Agreeing with the perceiver, expressing liking, emphasizing similarity, giving compliments.
The chapter's point is that it must be done **sincerely**: mere flattery backfires.

*The downside risk.* Appearing **inauthentic**. The strategy's success depends on the perceiver not reading it
as a strategy — which makes it self-undermining when detected.

*What a detecting perceiver concludes.* Something worse than "this person is not as agreeable as they seemed."
The inference is about **intent**, which is the warmth dimension: this person is willing to misrepresent
themselves to obtain something from me. Detected ingratiation therefore moves the target *down* on warmth,
which is the very dimension the strategy was aimed at raising. And since the perceiver now attributes a hidden
goal, later agreeable behavior is reinterpreted as further strategy — the impression becomes resistant to
disconfirming evidence.

*The competence dimension complicates it.* Clumsily detected ingratiation may also lower competence. Skillful
ingratiation that is nonetheless detected might raise it — a capable manipulator — which by the interaction of
the dimensions is the most threatening combination.

**The general structure across the five goals.** Each of being liked, respected, admired, feared and helped
has an enactment and a characteristic downside risk, and in each case the risk is realized when the *strategy*
becomes visible rather than the trait. Self-presentation works only while it is read as expression rather than
as tactic.

**Why this matters for the module.** It shows why the target cannot be modeled as a passive source of
information. What the perceiver receives is already shaped by the target's goals, and the perceiver is
simultaneously trying to infer those goals — which is why the chapter concludes that interdependent
interaction, not one-directional judgment, is the significant context.
:::
::::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 14
**Objective 3.6a.** The chapter concludes that interdependent dyads are the most significant context for
person perception. What follows for a literature built largely on perceivers judging static stimuli?
:::
::: tab Answer
**The claim.** The chapter flags its own conclusion in advance: person perception matters most in encounters
between **two people whose outcomes depend on one another**, and it points to new neuroimaging and AI methods
as what makes that case tractable.

**What follows — four things.**

*The field studied the tractable case, not the important one.* This is Module 1's argument in a specific
literature: method and subject matter were settled together, and the paradigm's feasibility shaped what
counted as the phenomenon. The chapter is unusually candid in conceding it from inside.

*Some findings may not survive the move.* Processes evoked by real or perceived human presence differ
**fundamentally** from those in private or with non-human or hypothetical actors. If judging a photograph
engages different processes than facing a person who is judging you back, results from the first do not
straightforwardly transfer. That is a generalizability failure of exactly Module 2's kind — but across
*situations* rather than populations.

*Whole classes of phenomenon were invisible.* Self-presentation, behavioral confirmation, disclosure matching,
perceiver-induced constraint and mutual emotional influence require both parties to be active. A static-stimulus
paradigm cannot exhibit them, so their absence from the literature reflects the method, not the world.

*Effect sizes and reliabilities may be misleading in both directions.* Removing the target's agency removes a
source of variance, potentially making processes look cleaner and more consistent than they are.

**What does not follow.** That the existing literature is worthless. Static paradigms established the
dimensions, the temporal organization and the category-to-individuating continuum, and those frameworks are
what make the naturalistic work interpretable. The correct conclusion is about **scope**: findings from
one-directional judgment describe one-directional judgment, and their extension to interaction is a hypothesis
rather than a given.

**The Module 2 connection worth naming.** This is an implicit generality claim made by omission. Papers report
"how people form impressions" when they have studied how people judge photographs, without a scope qualifier —
the generalization carried by grammar rather than argument.
:::

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 15
**Objective 3.6a.** Why is neglecting one's own contribution to the situation a distinctively costly failure,
and what would it take to study a process participants cannot report on?
:::
::: tab Answer
**Why it is distinctively costly.**

*The perceiver is the part of the situation they are responsible for.* Ordinary situational neglect misses
causes the perceiver did not create. Neglecting one's own contribution misses a cause one authored — so the
error is not only inferential but moral, converting effects one produced into evidence about someone else's
character.

*It is self-sealing.* In behavioral confirmation and perceiver-induced constraint, the perceiver elicits the
behavior they then attribute to the target. The confirming evidence is manufactured by the expectation, so the
impression looks *better* supported the more the perceiver acts on it.

*The correction cannot run.* Correction from a character anchor toward the situation runs out of awareness and
is curtailed under time or effort pressure. Correcting for oneself requires representing one's own behavior as
an input to the other's — an extra step, in the interdependent setting the chapter says is standard.

*The information is uniquely available and uniquely unused.* The perceiver has privileged access to their own
conduct and intentions. That this is the neglected input, not a missing one, is what makes the failure
striking.

**Studying a process participants cannot report on.** The chapter establishes the constraint: people remember
the inference and not the data, cannot explain their preferences, and construct plausible but irrelevant
explanations. So:

*Do not use self-report as the measure of the process.* Use it as data about what people believe about
themselves, which is a different question.

*Measure behavior and outcomes.* Compare the impression formed against what the perceiver actually did, coded
independently.

*Manipulate the perceiver's contribution and observe the attribution.* Vary the perceiver's own behavior
experimentally and see whether their explanation of the target shifts to compensate. If it does not, the
neglect is demonstrated without asking anyone to introspect.

*Use process measures with the right temporal resolution* — response latency, gaze, neural measures — that do
not route through introspection.

*Use interdependent naturalistic paradigms*, since the failure concerns one's own influence and requires a
setting where one has influence.

*Compare perceiver and observer accounts* of the same interaction, with the discrepancy as the dependent
measure rather than either account being taken as truth.
