# Module 10 Practice: Attraction, Rejection, and Close Relationships

## Learning objectives

- **10.1a:** Distinguish general desirability, personal preferences, and unique attraction.
- **10.2a:** Evaluate dyadic, longitudinal, and behavioral evidence in relationship research.
- **10.3a:** Explain relationship outcomes through interdependence and attachment dimensions.
- **10.4a:** Distinguish satisfaction, alternatives, investments, and commitment.
- **10.5a:** Explain how responsiveness, shared goals, and constructive responses can support relationships.
- **10.6a:** Compare relationship trajectories using vulnerability, stress, and adaptation.
- **10.7a:** Distinguish rejection, relationship dissolution, and variation in adjustment.
- **10.8a:** Evaluate generalization and explain relationships as locally organized patterns.

## Question 1 · Objective 10.1a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 1
In a hypothetical additive example, the grand mean is 5, an actor component is +1, a partner component is +0.5, and a relationship component is +0.5. What is the fitted rating?
:::
::: tab Answer
The fitted rating is 7: 5 + 1 + 0.5 + 0.5. Only the final component represents the distinctive pairing beyond those general tendencies. This constructed illustration omits residual error and other model details; it does not show that a single rating can identify all components.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).

## Question 2 · Objective 10.1a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 2
People who are judged warm receive higher attraction ratings. Does this show that people who say they especially value warmth are especially responsive to it?
:::
::: tab Answer
No. A general association of warmth with attraction differs from an interaction between warmth and the evaluator’s stated preference. Test that interaction with appropriate repeated encounters. A trait can be broadly desirable without individual preference reports improving prediction of specific attraction.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 3 · Objective 10.2a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 3
A study obtains two reports from each of 100 couples and analyzes 200 independent people. What should the analysis reconsider?
:::
::: tab Answer
The reports are nested in couples and may be correlated. Use an analysis that represents dyadic dependence and the actor or partner questions being asked. The number of reports does not equal the number of independent couples. Sampling and measurement decisions also determine which effects can be identified.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 4 · Objective 10.2a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 4
Couples with more positive communication are happier. Why is teaching positive communication not already proven to cause happiness?
:::
::: tab Answer
Happier couples may communicate differently, or shared conditions may affect both. An intervention with an appropriate comparison can test a causal effect of a specific communication change. Even then, assess whether the intervention changed the intended process and whether effects generalize beyond the task.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 5 · Objective 10.3a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 5
One partner wants reassurance after a disagreement while the other withdraws. Can their attachment categories be diagnosed from this episode?
:::
::: tab Answer
No. The episode is compatible with several explanations, including stress, conversational timing, and learned expectations. Repeated measures and appropriate instruments can assess attachment-related tendencies. Describe the interaction before assigning a trait, and examine how each response affects the next.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 6 · Objective 10.3a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 6
How could a schedule conflict be reduced without changing either partner’s preferences?
:::
::: tab Answer
Change the available arrangement, such as access to an alternative time or shared support, so that one person’s preferred action imposes less cost on the other. This changes interdependence. It illustrates why relationship outcomes cannot be explained solely by personal traits or affection.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 7 · Objective 10.4a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 7
Two people report equal satisfaction, but one has greater relationship-specific investments and fewer attractive alternatives. What does the investment model predict?
:::
::: tab Answer
Other things equal, the second person may report greater commitment. The prediction concerns commitment, not necessarily better relationship quality. Measure the three predictors separately and avoid treating duration as proof of satisfaction. Observational associations also leave causal questions open.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 8 · Objective 10.4a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 8
An intervention increases relationship duration but does not measure participants’ welfare. Why is its success claim incomplete?
:::
::: tab Answer
Duration alone does not establish that the relationship improved or that staying served the participants. Assess quality, autonomy, and relevant benefits and costs. The investment model helps explain persistence; it does not make persistence the only desirable outcome.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 9 · Objective 10.5a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 9
A student shares news of a small research award. Their partner immediately explains how to win a larger one. Why might the response miss the mark?
:::
::: tab Answer
The student may be seeking shared enjoyment or recognition rather than strategy advice. Assess what response was wanted and how the actual response was experienced. Advice is not inherently unsupportive, but its fit matters. This hypothetical example distinguishes intention to help from perceived responsiveness.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 10 · Objective 10.5a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 10
How would you test whether a good-news response matters beyond the couple’s general satisfaction?
:::
::: tab Answer
Use repeated events, assess prior satisfaction, and observe or manipulate specific response features when appropriate. Measure the recipient’s immediate experience and subsequent interaction. A within-couple comparison can reduce some stable confounds, while an experiment provides stronger evidence about a particular response.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 11 · Objective 10.6a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 11
A couple communicates less warmly during a month of unstable work hours. Why is a purely personality-based account incomplete?
:::
::: tab Answer
The schedule may reduce time, increase stress, and change opportunities for support. Stable traits may still matter, but the within-couple change calls for a contextual explanation. Measure the stressor and interaction over time and compare competing accounts rather than infer a new enduring trait from one difficult month.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 12 · Objective 10.6a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 12
Two couples have the same mean satisfaction but different week-to-week variability. What additional question becomes important?
:::
::: tab Answer
Ask whether the fluctuations reflect changing stress, uncertainty, support, or measurement noise, and whether they predict later outcomes beyond the mean. Equal averages do not imply equivalent experiences. Repeated measurement is necessary to distinguish variability from a single unusual report.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 13 · Objective 10.7a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 13
A study measures distress one week after a breakup and concludes that separation is always harmful. What is missing?
:::
::: tab Answer
The claim exceeds the time window and population studied. Assess prior relationship conditions, changes in resources, longer-term adjustment, and relevant comparison trajectories. Acute distress can coexist with later improvement. Observing an average does not establish that every person would have been better off remaining together.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 14 · Objective 10.7a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 14
Why should a study of rejection collect reports from both people when feasible?
:::
::: tab Answer
The participants can differ in desired closeness, perceived intentions, and interpretations of the same event. Two perspectives can clarify asymmetry without assuming either report fully captures the interaction. Participation and privacy must remain voluntary; the design should not pressure contact where it is unwanted.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 15 · Objective 10.8a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 15
A commitment scale treats any additional romantic partner as evidence of low commitment. What validity problem arises?
:::
::: tab Answer
The scale can confuse commitment with an assumption of exclusivity. In an explicitly consensual arrangement, the item may not measure the intended construct. Define commitment and test whether items function appropriately across relationship forms. This does not imply that every existing scale item is invalid in its original population.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
::::

## Question 16 · Objective 10.8a

:::: tabs
::: tab Q 16
A couple values a weekly walk, while another dislikes scheduled rituals. What would a microcultural account investigate?
:::
::: tab Answer
Ask what the practice means to each pair, how expectations developed, and how the activity fits their circumstances. The first pair’s benefit does not establish a universal prescription. A research design should distinguish the particular activity from shared meaning, coordination, and time together.

**Source basis:** Finkel (2025).
:::
